\chapter{Inverse Dynamics and the Parallel Loop Problem}
\label{ch:inverse_dynamics_parallel}

\epigraph{In physics, we measure what we can and infer what we cannot. But when the system has loops, some things become fundamentally unreachable by inference alone.}{---adapted from R.\ Feynman}

\begin{laymansbox}{Why This Chapter Matters for Golf Biomechanics}{intro:parallel_loops}
The human body is not a stick. It is not even a branching tree of bones. Your two hands grip the club, your two feet push the ground, your spine twists within your rib cage—everywhere, closed loops abound. These loops create a fundamental mathematical barrier that every biomechanist must face: the barrier of inverse dynamics.

Here is the stumbling block: from motion capture technology, we can measure exactly how your skeleton moves. We can film your swing, track every joint angle, compute every velocity and acceleration. The natural question is: \emph{What muscle forces caused this motion?}

For an isolated arm reaching to a table, this question has a unique answer. For the golf swing, which is bolted together by loops and constraints, it often does not. This chapter explains why, what remains measurable despite the barrier, and how the ZTCF framework sidesteps the problem entirely by asking a more robust question.

This is the most technically demanding chapter in this book. Push through it. The reward is a clear-eyed understanding of what biomechanics can and cannot infer from motion capture data.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{Forward and Inverse Dynamics Defined}

\index{forward dynamics}\index{inverse dynamics}

In the language of mechanics, there are two directions of inference:

\begin{definition}{Forward Dynamics}{def:forward_dynamics}
Given all applied forces and torques $\bm{u}(t)$ and an initial state $\bm{q}(0)$, $\dot{\bm{q}}(0)$, \emph{integrate forward} to predict the motion $\bm{q}(t)$. This is the natural direction: causes produce effects.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Inverse Dynamics}{def:inverse_dynamics}
Given a measured trajectory $\bm{q}(t)$, differentiate to compute $\dot{\bm{q}}(t)$ and $\ddot{\bm{q}}(t)$, then \emph{solve backward} to infer the forces and torques $\bm{u}(t)$ that must have produced it. This is the unnatural direction: effects are used to deduce causes.
\end{definition}

The motivating question for inverse dynamics in biomechanics is straightforward:

\begin{laymansbox}{The Measurement Problem}{context:measurement_problem}
You swing a golf club. A motion capture system records your skeleton in three-dimensional space at 120 frames per second. The computer knows exactly how your shoulder, elbow, wrist, hips, and knees moved. But the motion capture system cannot feel your muscles. It cannot measure the electrical signals your motor cortex sends to your muscle fibers, nor the contractile forces those fibers generate.

Yet your muscles caused this motion. Can we compute those muscle forces \emph{backwards} from the motion we measured?

For most of your body, the answer is yes. For the golf swing as a whole—held together by the two-handed grip and the planted feet—the answer is more subtle. Some aspects of muscle recruitment become fundamentally \emph{undetectable} from motion alone.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{The Canonical Equation}
\label{subsec:canonical_eom}

\index{equations of motion}\index{generalized coordinates}

Recall the equation of motion for a mechanical system with $n$ degrees of freedom:
\begin{equation}
\bm{M}(\bm{q})\,\ddot{\bm{q}} + \bm{C}(\bm{q},\dot{\bm{q}})\,\dot{\bm{q}} + \bm{g}(\bm{q}) = \bm{u}
\label{eq:eom_unconstrained}
\end{equation}

Here:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\bm{q} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ are the generalized coordinates (joint angles).
\item $\bm{M}(\bm{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is the mass matrix, symmetric and positive definite.
\item $\bm{C}(\bm{q},\dot{\bm{q}})$ are the Coriolis and centrifugal terms.
\item $\bm{g}(\bm{q})$ is the gravity vector.
\item $\bm{u}$ are the generalized forces (joint torques applied by muscles).
\end{itemize}

\index{mass matrix}\index{Coriolis terms}

For an \emph{unconstrained} system (open chain), this equation has a unique inverse:
\begin{equation}
\bm{u} = \bm{M}(\bm{q})\,\ddot{\bm{q}} + \bm{C}(\bm{q},\dot{\bm{q}})\,\dot{\bm{q}} + \bm{g}(\bm{q})
\label{eq:inverse_open}
\end{equation}

Given $\bm{q}(t)$ (from motion capture), differentiate twice to get $\ddot{\bm{q}}(t)$, compute the mass matrix and its derivatives, plug in numbers, and obtain $\bm{u}(t)$. No ambiguity. This is the foundation of biomechanical inverse dynamics, used in thousands of gait analysis labs worldwide.

But the golf swing is not a simple open chain. It is \emph{constrained}. And that changes everything.

\section{The Open Chain: Why Inverse Dynamics Succeeds}
\label{sec:open_chain_success}

\begin{laymansbox}{Intuition: Pushing a Single Stick}{context:open_chain_intuition}
Imagine a robot arm: shoulder (1 DOF), elbow (1 DOF), wrist (1 DOF). Three joints, three motors. You command the motors with specific torques and watch the arm move. This is forward dynamics: you set the input (motor torques), physics tells you the output (arm trajectory).

Now imagine you video the arm's motion and want to reverse-engineer the motor commands. You measure the arm's angles and angular velocities frame by frame. Then you differentiate to get accelerations. Now, the question: what motors torques caused these accelerations?

The answer is unambiguous. The shoulder motor torque affects only the shoulder. The elbow motor affects only the shoulder and elbow. The wrist motor affects only the wrist motion (plus slight coupling through Coriolis). With three measurements (three joint accelerations) and three unknowns (three motor torques), you solve a square linear system. It has a unique solution.

This simplicity persists for any open chain: $n$ joints means $n$ DOF and $n$ unknowns ($n$ joint torques). When the dimension of the problem matches the dimension of the measurement, inverse dynamics is unique and robust.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{Why the Mass Matrix is Always Invertible}
\label{subsec:mass_matrix_invertible}

\index{positive definite}\index{rank}\index{invertibility}

For an open kinematic chain with $n$ generalized coordinates:

\begin{principle}{Mass Matrix Invertibility}{prin:mass_invertible}
The mass matrix $\bm{M}(\bm{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is \emph{always} symmetric and positive definite. Therefore:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{det}(\bm{M}(\bm{q})) > 0 \quad \forall \bm{q}
\label{eq:det_positive}
\end{equation}

This can be proven by direct construction of kinetic energy. The kinetic energy of a rigid-body system is:
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{1}{2}\,\dot{\bm{q}}^T \bm{M}(\bm{q})\,\dot{\bm{q}}
\end{equation}

Since kinetic energy is always non-negative and is zero only when $\dot{\bm{q}} = \bm{0}$, the matrix $\bm{M}$ is positive definite in the strict sense. A positive definite matrix always has full rank and is always invertible.
\end{principle}

This is a fundamental fact of mechanics: you \emph{cannot} have a configuration where the inertia matrix becomes singular. Nature forbids it.

\subsection{The Recursive Newton-Euler Algorithm}
\label{subsec:rnea}

\index{Recursive Newton-Euler}\index{RNEA}\index{biomechanics}

In practice, biomechanics labs do not invert the mass matrix directly. Instead, they use a computational algorithm that implicitly solves the inverse dynamics problem in $O(n)$ time:

\begin{definition}{Recursive Newton-Euler Algorithm (RNEA)}{def:rnea}
A two-pass algorithm for computing joint torques from measured kinematics:

\textbf{Forward Pass (base to tip):} Starting at the fixed base, compute the velocity $\bm{v}_i$ and acceleration $\bm{a}_i$ of each body $i$ by forward recursion:
\begin{align}
\bm{v}_i &= \bm{v}_{i-1} + \bm{J}_i\,\dot{q}_i \\
\bm{a}_i &= \bm{a}_{i-1} + \bm{J}_i\,\ddot{q}_i + \dot{\bm{J}}_i\,\dot{q}_i
\end{align}

\textbf{Backward Pass (tip to base):} Starting at the distal tip, compute the force $\bm{f}_i$ and moment $\bm{m}_i$ at each joint by backward recursion:
\begin{align}
\bm{f}_i &= \bm{f}_{i+1} + m_i\,\bm{a}_i \\
\bm{m}_i &= \bm{m}_{i+1} + m_i\,\bm{r}_i \times \bm{a}_i + \bm{I}_i\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_i
\end{align}

At each joint, solve for the torque $\tau_i$ that satisfies Newton's second law for that body.
\end{definition}

\index{Newton-Euler equations}

The genius of RNEA is that it solves the inverse dynamics problem without ever forming the mass matrix explicitly. It works sequentially, joint by joint, so it remains numerically stable and computationally fast.

This algorithm is used in every motion-capture biomechanics lab in the world to compute joint torques from motion capture. It works well—\emph{for open chains}.

\section{The Parallel Mechanism: Where It All Breaks Down}
\label{sec:parallel_mechanism}

\begin{laymansbox}{The Two-Handed Grip and the Closed Loop}{context:two_handed_grip}
Now consider a golfer at address. Both hands grip the club. Both feet are planted on the ground. The spine is bent and twisted. There are no loose ends. Every limb is connected to every other limb through a chain of constraints.

This is no longer a tree. It is a \emph{graph}. More precisely, it is a \emph{parallel mechanism}—a system with closed kinematic loops.

A closed kinematic loop imposes a constraint: if one hand moves, the other must move in a coordinated way to maintain the grip. This constraint is not a free parameter you can command with a motor torque. It is an implicit requirement imposed by the physics of contact.

These constraints reduce the number of degrees of freedom but increase the number of unknown forces. Constraint forces (the grip pressure between your hands, the normal force from the ground) are real physical forces that do real mechanical work. But they are internal to the system. You cannot measure them directly. And worse—they entangle the equations of motion in a way that makes inverse dynamics ambiguous.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{Constraints and Constraint Forces}
\label{subsec:constraints}

\index{kinematic constraints}\index{constraint forces}\index{Lagrange multipliers}

For a system with $n$ generalized coordinates and $k$ holonomic constraints, the constrained equations of motion take the form:
\begin{equation}
\bm{M}(\bm{q})\,\ddot{\bm{q}} + \bm{C}(\bm{q},\dot{\bm{q}})\,\dot{\bm{q}} + \bm{g}(\bm{q}) = \bm{B}(\bm{q})\,\bm{u} + \bm{J}_c^T(\bm{q})\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}
\label{eq:eom_constrained}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\bm{u} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ are the \emph{applied} torques (muscle-driven).
\item $\bm{B}(\bm{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ maps actuator torques to generalized forces.
\item $\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}^k$ are the constraint multipliers (Lagrange multipliers).
\item $\bm{J}_c(\bm{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times n}$ is the constraint Jacobian.
\end{itemize}

\index{constraint Jacobian}

The constraint equations are:
\begin{equation}
\bm{\Phi}(\bm{q}) = \bm{0}
\label{eq:constraints}
\end{equation}

Differentiating twice:
\begin{equation}
\bm{J}_c(\bm{q})\,\ddot{\bm{q}} + \dot{\bm{J}}_c(\bm{q},\dot{\bm{q}})\,\dot{\bm{q}} = \bm{0}
\label{eq:constraint_acceleration}
\end{equation}

\section{Loop Closure Equations and Velocity Constraints}
\label{sec:loop_closure_formal}

\index{loop closure}\index{kinematic consistency}\index{velocity constraint}

For the golf swing with a two-handed grip, the fundamental constraint is that both hands must grip the same club. In the 3D spatial case, this imposes 6 constraints (3 positional, 3 rotational) relating the left and right arm configurations.

\begin{definition}{Loop Closure Equations (Formal)}{def:loop_closure_formal}
If the left arm has forward kinematics $\mathbf{T}_L(\bm{q}_L)$ and the right arm has forward kinematics $\mathbf{T}_R(\bm{q}_R)$, where $\mathbf{T}$ are homogeneous transformation matrices, then the loop closure constraint is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{T}_L(\bm{q}_L) = \mathbf{T}_R(\bm{q}_R)
\label{eq:loop_closure_spatial}
\end{equation}

This is a matrix equation in $SE(3)$ (the special Euclidean group). Written as explicit constraints on positions and orientations:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{p}_L(\bm{q}_L) &= \mathbf{p}_R(\bm{q}_R) \quad \text{(3 position constraints)} \\
\mathbf{R}_L(\bm{q}_L) &= \mathbf{R}_R(\bm{q}_R) \quad \text{(3 orientation constraints)}
\label{eq:loop_closure_explicit}
\end{align}

This is a nonlinear system of 6 equations in $n$ unknowns (the joint angles $\bm{q}_L$ and $\bm{q}_R$).
\end{definition}

\subsection{Velocity-Level Constraint}
\label{subsec:velocity_constraint}

Differentiating the loop closure equation with respect to time:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}_L(\bm{q}_L)\,\dot{\bm{q}}_L = \mathbf{J}_R(\bm{q}_R)\,\dot{\bm{q}}_R
\label{eq:velocity_constraint_loop}
\end{equation}

This is the velocity-level loop constraint: the velocities of the left and right end effectors must match (they're gripping the same object).

Rearranging into standard form:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{J}_L & -\mathbf{J}_R \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\bm{q}}_L \\ \dot{\bm{q}}_R \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0}
\label{eq:constraint_jacobian_two_arm}
\end{equation}

The constraint Jacobian is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}_c = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{J}_L & -\mathbf{J}_R \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:constraint_jacobian_definition_two_arm}
\end{equation}

The rank of this Jacobian is typically 6 (for non-singular arm configurations). This means the velocity constraint reduces the effective DOF from $n$ (unconstrained) to $n - 6$ (constrained).

%============================================================================
\subsection{Counting Equations and Unknowns}
\label{subsec:counting}

Here is the fundamental problem:

\begin{constraintbox}{The Underdetermined System}{constraint:underdetermined}
We have:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Equations:} $n$ equations of motion (Equation \ref{eq:eom_constrained})
\item \textbf{Unknowns:} $p$ applied torques $\bm{u}$ plus $k$ constraint multipliers $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$, for a total of $p + k$ unknowns
\end{itemize}

If $p + k > n$, the system is \textbf{underdetermined}. There are more unknowns than equations.

For an open chain: $p = n$ (one motor per DOF), $k = 0$ (no constraints) $\Rightarrow$ $p + k = n$ $\Rightarrow$ square system $\Rightarrow$ unique solution.

For a closed-loop system: $p$ may remain $n$ (muscles still act on all $n$ DOF), but now $k > 0$ (there are loop constraints) $\Rightarrow$ $p + k > n$ $\Rightarrow$ rectangular system $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{no unique solution in general}.
\end{constraintbox}

\index{underdetermined system}

The constraint forces $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ are \emph{real forces}. Your left hand genuinely pushes against your right hand. But you cannot see this force directly. It is internal to the system. And when you try to invert the dynamics—to go from measured motion to inferred torques—the constraint forces and applied torques become entangled in a way that makes them impossible to disentangle uniquely.

\section{The Constraint Jacobian Geometry and DOF}
\label{sec:constraint_jacobian_geometry}

\index{constraint Jacobian}\index{rank}\index{degrees of freedom}

The constraint Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_c(\bm{q})$ is an $k \times n$ matrix, where $k$ is the number of constraints and $n$ is the number of generalized coordinates.

\begin{principle}{DOF Reduction via Constraints}{principle:dof_reduction}
The effective degrees of freedom of a constrained system is:
\begin{equation}
\text{DOF}_{\text{effective}} = n - \text{rank}(\mathbf{J}_c(\bm{q}))
\label{eq:dof_effective}
\end{equation}

At non-singular configurations, the rank of $\mathbf{J}_c$ is typically equal to $k$ (the number of constraints). At singular configurations, the rank drops below $k$, meaning some constraints become redundant or the system gains unexpected DOF.
\end{principle}

\subsection{Rank Loss and Singularities}
\label{subsec:rank_loss_singularities}

\index{singularity}\index{rank deficiency}

A \textbf{singularity} occurs when $\text{rank}(\mathbf{J}_c(\bm{q})) < k$. At a singularity:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The constraint Jacobian has a nontrivial null space.
\item Some constraints become dependent on others.
\item The mechanism may gain an unexpected degree of freedom.
\item The constraint forces may become infinite (if the system is forced through the singularity).
\end{enumerate}

In the golf swing, singularities occur at:
\begin{itemize}
\item Address position (near-singularity with low effective stiffness).
\item Impact (arm fully extended, approach to leg singularity).
\item Transition points between backswing and downswing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Null Space of the Constraint Jacobian: Allowed Motions}
\label{sec:nullspace_constraint_jacobian}

\index{null space}\index{allowed velocities}\index{redundancy}

The null space of $\mathbf{J}_c$ is the set of velocities that don't violate the constraint:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{J}_c) = \{ \dot{\bm{q}} : \mathbf{J}_c(\bm{q})\,\dot{\bm{q}} = \mathbf{0} \}
\label{eq:nullspace_constraint}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}{Null Space Dimension and Redundancy}{def:nullspace_dimension}
The dimension of the null space is:
\begin{equation}
d = n - \text{rank}(\mathbf{J}_c)
\label{eq:nullspace_dim_formula}
\end{equation}

This dimension is the number of "redundant" or "hidden" DOF in the system—joint motions that don't change the constraint (the club's motion remains unchanged).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}{Null Space for Two-Handed Grip}{ex:nullspace_two_hands}
Consider the two arms (14 DOF total) gripping a club with 6 constraints (3 position + 3 orientation).

At a non-singular configuration:
\begin{equation}
d = 14 - 6 = 8
\label{eq:nullspace_dim_two_arms}
\end{equation}

This means there are 8-dimensional family of joint configurations that all result in the same club position and orientation. Some examples of these null space motions:
\begin{itemize}
\item Adjust right-hand grip pressure while maintaining club position.
\item Adjust left-hand grip angle without changing the club's position.
\item Change the plane of the arm motion (more vertical vs. more horizontal) while keeping club trajectory fixed.
\item Adjust wrist cock angle without changing the overall club motion.
\end{itemize}

These are all "free" motions in the sense that they don't require external work—they're constrained by the loop closure but allowed by it.
\end{example}

\subsection{Null Space Projector}
\label{subsec:nullspace_projector}

A useful computational tool is the \textbf{null space projector}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{J}_c^+ \mathbf{J}_c
\label{eq:nullspace_projector}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{J}_c^+$ is the pseudoinverse of $\mathbf{J}_c$.

This matrix projects any velocity onto the null space:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\bm{q}}_{\text{null}} = \mathbf{P}\,\dot{\bm{q}}_{\text{any}}
\label{eq:nullspace_projection}
\end{equation}

Any velocity of the form $\dot{\bm{q}}_{\text{null}}$ satisfies the constraint exactly.

\section{Internal Forces and the Dual Null Space}
\label{sec:internal_forces_dual}

\index{internal forces}\index{range}\index{dual space}

Just as the null space describes allowed velocities, the dual null space describes internal forces.

\begin{definition}{Useful and Internal Forces}{def:useful_internal_forces}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Useful forces:} Those in the range of $\mathbf{J}_c^T$. These forces constrain the motion (they do work on the constraint).

\item \textbf{Internal forces:} Those in the null space of $\mathbf{J}_c^T$. These forces don't constrain motion—they create internal stress without affecting the kinematics.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{principle}{Orthogonality of Null Spaces}{principle:orthogonality_null_duals}
A fundamental property of linear algebra:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{J}_c) \perp \text{Range}(\mathbf{J}_c^T)
\label{eq:orthogonality_null_range}
\end{equation}

Mechanically: a force in the range of $\mathbf{J}_c^T$ does zero work on any velocity in the null space of $\mathbf{J}_c$.
\end{principle}

\begin{laymansbox}{Why Internal Forces Matter for Injury}{context:internal_forces_injury}
Internal forces (those in the null space of $\mathbf{J}_c^T$) are mechanically "invisible"—they don't change the kinematics. But they create real stresses in tissues.

For example, a golfer might grip the club very tightly (large internal grip force) while maintaining identical club trajectory compared to a golfer with light grip. The kinematics are the same, but the internal stresses are very different. The tight-grip golfer may fatigue forearm muscles and risk injury from the excess internal force.

This is why understanding the dual null space is crucial for predicting injury risk and coaching swing efficiency.
\end{laymansbox}

\section{Why Drift Forces Are Not Constrained by the Loop}
\label{sec:drift_not_constrained_inverse}

\index{drift forces}\index{gravity}\index{centrifugal}\index{Coriolis}

A critical insight from Chapter~\ref{ch:parallel_mechanisms}: drift forces are computed from kinematics alone and are NOT affected by the constraint ambiguity.

\subsection{Gravity}
\label{subsec:gravity_inverse}

Gravity acts on the center of mass:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{\text{grav}} = m\mathbf{g}
\label{eq:gravity_force}
\end{equation}

The center of mass position is determined uniquely by the skeleton's configuration $\bm{q}$. Once you know the configuration from motion capture, gravity is completely determined. No ambiguity.

\subsection{Coriolis Force}
\label{subsec:coriolis_inverse}

The Coriolis force is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{\text{Cor}} = -2m\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}
\label{eq:coriolis_general}
\end{equation}

Both $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ (body rotation rate) and $\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}$ (relative velocity) are determined from joint angles and velocities. No ambiguity.

Moreover, Coriolis is \emph{perpendicular} to the velocity that creates it. This perpendicularity is a kinematic fact, not dependent on forces.

\subsection{Centrifugal Force}
\label{subsec:centrifugal_inverse}

The centrifugal acceleration (in the rotating frame) is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_{\text{cent}} = -\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})
\label{eq:centrifugal_accel}
\end{equation}

This depends only on $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ (rotation rate, from kinematics) and $\mathbf{r}$ (position, from kinematics). No ambiguity.

\begin{principle}{Drift Forces Are Observable}{principle:drift_observable_inverse}
From motion capture alone, a biomechanist can compute the total drift force:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{\text{drift}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{grav}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{Cor}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{cent}} + \text{(elastic drift)}
\label{eq:total_drift_force}
\end{equation}

This total drift force is uniquely determined by kinematics. There is no null space ambiguity. The ZTCF framework (Chapter~\ref{ch:zero_torque_counterfactual}) leverages this fact to compute the total net force without needing to resolve the ambiguity in individual muscle torques.
\end{principle}

\section{Active Force Distribution Ambiguity}
\label{sec:active_force_ambiguity}

\index{force distribution}\index{load sharing}\index{ambiguity}

While drift forces are unambiguous, the distribution of \emph{active muscle torques} between the two hands is ambiguous.

\begin{theorem}{The Ambiguity Theorem for Two-Handed Grip}{theorem:ambiguity_two_hands}
In a system where both hands grip a common object:

The total required force (drift + control combined) is uniquely determined:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{\text{total}} = \mathbf{M}\,\ddot{\bm{q}} + \mathbf{C}\,\dot{\bm{q}} + \mathbf{g}
\label{eq:total_force_unique}
\end{equation}

But this total can be decomposed between hands infinitely many ways:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{\text{total}} = \mathbf{F}_L + \mathbf{F}_R
\label{eq:load_decomposition}
\end{equation}

where any pair $(\mathbf{F}_L, \mathbf{F}_R)$ satisfying $\mathbf{F}_L + \mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_{\text{total}}$ is equally valid kinematically.

Mathematically: the solution space is the affine subspace
\begin{equation}
\{ (\mathbf{F}_L, \mathbf{F}_R) : \mathbf{F}_L + \mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_{\text{total}} \}
\label{eq:solution_affine_subspace}
\end{equation}

This is a 3-dimensional affine subspace (codimension 3 in the 6-dimensional space of possible $(\mathbf{F}_L, \mathbf{F}_R)$ pairs).
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Vaughan's Resolution Strategy}
\label{subsec:vaughan_resolution}

\index{Vaughan}\index{optimization}\index{load distribution}

\cite{Vaughan1981,Vaughan1983} proposed several strategies to resolve this ambiguity:

\begin{reference}{Vaughan's Approaches}{ref:vaughan_resolution_strategies}

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Proportional load sharing:} Assume the load is distributed proportionally to the sum of kinetic and potential energies in each limb. This is a heuristic without strong physical justification.

\item \textbf{Optimization criterion:} Minimize a scalar objective such as:
\begin{equation}
J = \sum_i (\tau_i / \tau_{i,\text{max}})^2
\label{eq:optimization_criterion_torque}
\end{equation}

or

\begin{equation}
J = \sum_i (\dot{E}_i)^2 \quad \text{(metabolic cost)}
\label{eq:optimization_criterion_metabolic}
\end{equation}

Subject to the constraint $\mathbf{F}_L + \mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_{\text{total}}$.

\item \textbf{Instrumented measurement:} Use force plates under each hand (or each foot for lower body) to directly measure the constraint forces, breaking the ambiguity.

\end{enumerate}

The optimization approach has become standard in biomechanics, with the understanding that different objective functions (minimize muscle activation, minimize metabolic cost, minimize joint torques, etc.) will yield different load distributions—all of which are mechanically consistent with the measured motion.

\end{reference}

\section{Modern Instrumented Club Approaches}
\label{sec:instrumented_clubs}

\index{instrumented equipment}\index{strain gauges}\index{force sensors}

One way to break the ambiguity is to measure forces directly at the grip.

\subsection{Strain Gauge Instrumentation}
\label{subsec:strain_gauges}

A \textbf{strain gauge} is a thin resistive element that changes resistance when strained. By gluing strain gauges to the club shaft (or grip), researchers can measure the forces and moments acting on the club.

\begin{definition}{Wheatstone Bridge Configuration}{def:wheatstone_bridge}
Strain gauges are typically arranged in a \textbf{Wheatstone bridge} configuration to maximize sensitivity and cancel out temperature-related drift:
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{output}} = V_{\text{input}} \times \frac{R_1 R_3 - R_2 R_4}{(R_1 + R_2)(R_3 + R_4)}
\label{eq:wheatstone_output}
\end{equation}

When one strain gauge experiences tension (strain) and the opposite experiences compression, the output voltage is proportional to the strain.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Piezoelectric Force Sensors}
\label{subsec:piezoelectric_sensors}

\textbf{Piezoelectric sensors} generate an electrical signal when mechanically deformed. They are faster and more sensitive than strain gauges but typically require amplification.

\begin{definition}{Piezoelectric Effect}{def:piezoelectric}
Certain crystals (quartz, PZT ceramics) generate a voltage when stressed:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{d} \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma}
\label{eq:piezoelectric_relation}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{d}$ is the piezoelectric coefficient and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is the applied stress.
\end{definition}

Piezoelectric sensors are often arranged in a hexapod configuration (6 sensors, one for each DOF: 3 forces, 3 moments).

\subsection{Two-Point Wrench Measurement}
\label{subsec:two_point_wrench}

\index{wrench}\index{force and moment}

With force/moment data from multiple points, a biomechanist can compute the resultant wrench (total force and moment) acting on the club.

\begin{definition}{Wrench}{def:wrench}
A \textbf{wrench} is a 6D vector combining force and moment:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{W} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{F} \\ \mathbf{M} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6
\label{eq:wrench_definition}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

If two independent grip points on the club have known wrenches $\mathcal{W}_L$ and $\mathcal{W}_R$, the total wrench is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{W}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{W}_L + \mathcal{W}_R
\label{eq:total_wrench_two_points}
\end{equation}

This directly measures the resultant constraint forces and resolves the ambiguity (at least in the grip forces).

\section{Lower Body Inverse Dynamics: GRF and Feet}
\label{sec:lower_body_inverse_dynamics}

\index{ground reaction force}\index{force plate}\index{lower body}\index{sequential computation}

The lower body biomechanics can be analyzed using the same inverse dynamics framework, but with the advantage that force plate data provides a known boundary condition.

\subsection{Force Plate Boundary Condition}
\label{subsec:force_plate_bc}

A force plate measures the total force and moment exerted by the ground on the golfer's feet. This is a known external force that can be used to "close" the system and allow unique inverse dynamics computation.

\begin{principle}{Force Plate Provides Unique Solution}{principle:force_plate_unique}
With force plate data:
\begin{itemize}
\item The distal boundary condition (force/moment at the foot) is known (measured by force plate).
\item The proximal boundary condition (forces at the hip, if we're analyzing the leg only) is unknown but can be computed.
\item The leg kinematics is measured.
\item RNEA can be applied uniquely to solve for joint torques.
\end{itemize}

This is in contrast to the upper body, where no distal boundary condition is known (the club's acceleration is known, but the forces on the club from external sources—like aerodynamic drag or the ball—are unknown).
\end{principle}

\subsection{Sequential Ankle-Knee-Hip Computation}
\label{subsec:sequential_ankle_knee_hip}

\index{RNEA}\index{sequential computation}

Lower body inverse dynamics is computed sequentially, distal to proximal:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Ankle:} Apply RNEA using the foot's acceleration (from kinematics) and the foot's force/moment (from force plate). Solve for ankle joint torque.

\item \textbf{Knee:} Using the ankle torque and lower leg's acceleration, compute the forces and moments at the knee. Solve for knee joint torque.

\item \textbf{Hip:} Using the knee torque and thigh's acceleration, compute the forces and moments at the hip. Solve for hip joint torque.

\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}{Lower Body Inverse Dynamics Example}{ex:lower_body_id}
A 100 kg golfer during the downswing:
\begin{itemize}
\item Force plate measures vertical GRF: $F_z = 1400$ N (1.4 BW).
\item Force plate measures free moment: $M_z = 80$ N·m.
\item Motion capture shows hip acceleration: $\ddot{\theta}_{\text{hip}} = 200$ rad/s$^2$.
\item Hip inertia: $I_{\text{hip}} = 2$ kg·m$^2$.
\end{itemize}

From kinematics and the force plate data, we can compute the hip torque that produced the observed acceleration.

If the ground torque is $M_z = 80$ N·m (an illustrative value within the range reported in force-plate studies of the golf swing)\citeneeded{force-plate study reporting peak vertical-axis ground torque during the downswing} and this is applied at the feet (roughly 0.2 m from the pelvis), the resulting pelvis torque is approximately $\tau_{\text{pelvis}} = M_z / I_{\text{hip}} \times $ (accounting for moment arms and mass distribution).

This is a well-determined problem because the force plate provides the boundary condition.
\end{example}

\section{Ground Reaction Torque: Initiating Pelvis Rotation}
\label{sec:ground_torque_detailed}

\index{ground reaction torque}\index{free moment}\index{yaw moment}

The ground reaction torque (free moment) $M_z$ is the torque applied by the ground about the vertical axis. This is a fundamental driver of pelvis rotation in the golf swing.

\subsection{Computation of Ground Reaction Torque}
\label{subsec:ground_torque_computation}

If force plate data is available, $M_z$ is directly measured. If not, it can be approximated from the offset of the vertical ground reaction force from the body's center of rotation.

\begin{definition}{Ground Torque from Force Offset}{def:ground_torque_offset}
If the vertical GRF $F_z$ is applied at a horizontal offset $\mathbf{r}$ from the pelvis center, the resulting torque is:
\begin{equation}
M_z = \mathbf{r} \times F_z
\label{eq:ground_torque_offset}
\end{equation}

More precisely, if the center of pressure is at horizontal position $(x_{\text{COP}}, y_{\text{COP}})$ and the pelvis center is at $(x_p, y_p)$, then:
\begin{equation}
M_z = (x_{\text{COP}} - x_p) \times F_z^y - (y_{\text{COP}} - y_p) \times F_z^x
\label{eq:ground_torque_cop}
\end{equation}

where $F_z^x$ and $F_z^y$ are the horizontal components of the GRF vector.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Role in Downswing Initiation}
\label{subsec:ground_torque_downswing}

\begin{principle}{Ground Torque Initiates Downswing}{principle:ground_torque_downswing_role}
During the transition from backswing to downswing, the pelvis begins to accelerate. This acceleration is driven by:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The ground reaction torque $M_z$ (from the force plate).
\item The hip muscle torques (computed from inverse dynamics).
\end{enumerate}

The relative contributions vary, but in professional golfers, the ground reaction torque often contributes 50% or more of the total pelvis acceleration. This is another manifestation of drift dominance: the ground does significant mechanical work on the golfer's body.
\end{principle}

\subsection{Forward and Backward Torque Asymmetry}
\label{subsec:forward_backward_asymmetry}

An interesting feature of ground reaction torque is the asymmetry between forward (toward the target) and backward (away from target) torques.

During the downswing, the ground typically exerts a torque that \emph{accelerates} the pelvis (positive torque in the direction of swing). This is mechanically assisting the swing.

During the follow-through, the ground may exert a \emph{braking} torque (negative torque opposing pelvis rotation) as the golfer's feet push off the ground to stabilize balance.

This asymmetry reveals the "ground-up" power transfer story: the ground actively powers the downswing but passively decelerates the follow-through.

\section{Resolving Ambiguity: EMG, Optimization, and Instrumented Equipment}
\label{sec:resolving_ambiguity}

\index{electromyography}\index{EMG}\index{optimization}\index{muscle activation}

Three general approaches exist to resolve the ambiguity in inverse dynamics of parallel systems:

\subsection{Approach 1: Electromyography (EMG)}
\label{subsec:approach_emg}

\index{EMG}\index{electromyography}\index{muscle activation}

\begin{definition}{Electromyography (EMG)}{def:emg}
\textbf{Electromyography} is the measurement of electrical activity in muscles. Electrodes (surface or intramuscular) detect the action potentials generated by motor units when a muscle contracts.

EMG signal amplitude is typically proportional to muscle activation level (recruitment) and contraction force.
\end{definition}

EMG data can be used to constrain the solution: if EMG shows that the right arm is much more active than the left, the load distribution must favor the right arm.

\begin{laymansbox}{Strengths and Limitations of EMG}{context:emg_strengths_limitations}

\textbf{Strengths:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Direct measurement of muscle activation (neural signal).
\item Relatively non-invasive (surface electrodes).
\item High temporal resolution (can capture millisecond-scale changes in activation).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
\item EMG does not measure force directly—the relationship between EMG amplitude and force is nonlinear and depends on muscle length, velocity, fatigue state, and many other factors.
\item Cross-talk from nearby muscles can contaminate the signal.
\item Some deep muscles (e.g., deep core stabilizers) are difficult to record with surface electrodes.
\item EMG signal is noisy and requires filtering and processing, introducing artifacts.
\end{itemize}

In practice, EMG is most useful as a qualitative indicator (which muscles are active) rather than a quantitative measure of force.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{Approach 2: Optimization}
\label{subsec:approach_optimization}

\index{optimization}\index{criterion}\index{objective function}

An optimization approach assumes the golfer distributes loads according to some implicit criterion (e.g., minimize energy, minimize fatigue, minimize peak joint torque). By applying this criterion, we can select a specific solution from the ambiguous solution space.

\begin{definition}{Static Optimization for Load Distribution}{def:static_optimization}
Minimize a scalar objective function:
\begin{equation}
J = \sum_i (\tau_i)^2 \quad \text{or} \quad J = \sum_i (\tau_i / \tau_{i,\text{max}})^2 \quad \text{or} \quad J = \sum_i |EMG_i|
\label{eq:optimization_objectives}
\end{equation}

Subject to:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_L + \mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_{\text{total}}
\label{eq:optimization_constraint_force}
\end{equation}

and any additional constraints from biomechanical limits or measured data (e.g., EMG).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}{Quadratic Programming Solution}{ex:quadratic_program}
A common choice is to minimize the sum of squared torques:
\begin{equation}
J = \sum_i \tau_i^2
\label{eq:sum_squared_torques}
\end{equation}

Subject to $\mathbf{F}_L + \mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_{\text{total}}$.

This is a quadratic program with a linear constraint. The solution is the "minimum-norm" distribution: the distribution that achieves the required total force while minimizing the total joint torque magnitude.

The solution can be written explicitly using the pseudoinverse and null space projector (Chapter~\ref{ch:parallel_mechanisms}):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{\text{min-norm}} = \mathbf{J}_c^+ \mathbf{F}_{\text{total}}
\label{eq:minimum_norm_force}
\end{equation}
\end{example}

\begin{laymansbox}{Strengths and Limitations of Optimization}{context:optimization_strengths_limitations}

\textbf{Strengths:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Provides a deterministic solution.
\item Computationally straightforward (quadratic programs, convex optimization).
\item Results are often physiologically reasonable (minimum-norm solutions correlate with energy efficiency in some cases).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
\item The choice of objective function is somewhat arbitrary. Different objectives yield different solutions.
\item Golfers may not actually optimize according to the chosen criterion—different golfers may use different strategies.
\item Optimization cannot capture individual variation in control strategy without additional data.
\end{itemize}

The optimization approach is best viewed as a useful heuristic for generating plausible load distributions, not as a definitive answer.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{Approach 3: Instrumented Equipment (Grip Sensors, Force Plates)}
\label{subsec:approach_instrumentation}

\index{instrumentation}\index{force measurement}\index{grip sensors}

The most direct approach: measure the constraint forces directly using sensors.

\begin{definition}{Grip Force Sensors}{def:grip_sensors}
Force sensors mounted on or around the golf club grip can measure:
\begin{itemize}
\item Left-hand grip force (magnitude and direction).
\item Right-hand grip force (magnitude and direction).
\item The combined wrench (resultant force and moment).
\end{itemize}

Modern grip sensors often use capacitive, resistive, or piezoelectric elements. Some are integrated into the grip itself; others are external measurement devices (like a six-axis load cell under the hands).
\end{definition}

With grip force data, the ambiguity is completely resolved:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_L + \mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_{\text{measured}}
\label{eq:measured_constraint_force}
\end{equation}

The constraint forces are known, so the active muscle torques can be computed uniquely (subject only to the null space ambiguity in muscle-by-muscle decomposition, not in joint-level torques).

\begin{laymansbox}{Strengths and Limitations of Instrumentation}{context:instrumentation_strengths_limitations}

\textbf{Strengths:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Direct measurement—no assumptions or modeling required.
\item Resolves the fundamental ambiguity completely.
\item High fidelity and accuracy (modern sensors are very precise).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Adds complexity and cost to the experimental setup.
\item Requires careful calibration.
\item Can affect the golfer's natural swing (extra sensors on the club change its balance and feel).
\item Data collection requires specialized equipment and expertise.
\end{itemize}

Instrumented approaches are typically reserved for detailed research studies, not routine biomechanical screening.
\end{laymansbox}

\section{What CAN We Determine? The Identifiable Subspace}
\label{sec:identifiable}

\begin{laymansbox}{The Glass Half Full}{context:glass_half_full}
Despite the ambiguity, inverse dynamics is not useless. Many aspects of the motion \emph{are} uniquely determined by kinematics. It is important to know exactly which aspects are observable and which are invisible.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{The Identifiable Components}
\label{subsec:identifiable_components}

\begin{definition}{Identifiable vs. Unidentifiable Components}{def:identifiable}
From motion capture data of a system with loop constraints, the following quantities are \textbf{uniquely determined}:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Total generalized force (drift + control):} The sum of all forces and moments required to produce the measured acceleration. This is computed from the right-hand side of the equation of motion and is independent of how the force is distributed among individual actuators.

\item \textbf{Constraint forces (Lagrange multipliers):} The internal forces maintaining the loop closure. These are determined by the constraint kinematics equations:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}_c\,\ddot{\bm{q}} + \dot{\mathbf{J}}_c\,\dot{\bm{q}} = \mathbf{0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \boldsymbol{\lambda} \text{ is determined}
\label{eq:constraint_forces_determined}
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Joint-level torques (sum of all muscles at a joint):} The total torque at a joint (summed across all muscles and passive structures) is uniquely determined by the local dynamics:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{joint}} = I\,\ddot{q} + \text{(local Coriolis and gravity terms)}
\label{eq:joint_torque_unique}
\end{equation}

\end{enumerate}

The following quantities are \textbf{underdetermined}:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Individual muscle forces:} How the total joint torque is distributed among the left vs. right hand, or among the flexor vs. extensor muscles, is not uniquely determined. This is the fundamental load-sharing ambiguity.

\item \textbf{Muscle activation patterns:} Without EMG or other supplementary data, you cannot infer which specific muscles are active or how hard each is working.

\item \textbf{Internal stresses in tissues:} High grip pressure vs. low grip pressure produce identical kinematics but different internal stresses. These internal stresses are invisible to motion capture.

\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Joint Torques Are Identifiable}
\label{subsec:joint_torques_identifiable}

\begin{principle}{Joint-Level Torques from Inverse Dynamics}{principle:joint_torques_identifiable}
At the joint level, inverse dynamics succeeds:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{joint}} = M_{\text{joint}}\,\ddot{q}_{\text{joint}} + C_{\text{joint}}\,\dot{q}_{\text{joint}} + g_{\text{joint}} - \text{(constraint forces from children)}
\label{eq:joint_torque_formula}
\end{equation}

This torque is the \emph{net} torque at the joint—the sum of all forces and moments acting on it (muscles, ligaments, constraint forces, etc.). It is uniquely determined from kinematics.

However, this net joint torque is \emph{not} the same as muscle torque. The net torque includes:
\begin{itemize}
\item Active muscle torques.
\item Passive tissue torques (ligaments, tendons, capsule).
\item Constraint forces.
\end{itemize}

All of these contribute to the net joint torque. To separate them requires additional information (e.g., models of passive tissue stiffness, knowledge of constraint forces from force plates).
\end{principle}

\section{The ZTCF Framework as Forward Dynamics}
\label{sec:ztcf_forward_dynamics}

\index{ZTCF}\index{zero torque counterfactual}\index{forward dynamics}

Here is the insight that makes ZTCF robust to loop constraints:

\begin{principle}{ZTCF is a Forward Problem}{principle:ztcf_forward_problem}
The ZTCF framework (Chapter~\ref{ch:zero_torque_counterfactual}) asks: "If all muscles were deactivated and only passive drift forces acted, what would the motion be?"

This is a \emph{forward dynamics} problem: given the drift forces (which are kinematically determined), integrate forward to predict the motion.

Forward dynamics is uniquely solvable even in parallel systems (recall Theorem \ref{thm:forward_wellposed} in Chapter~\ref{ch:inverse_dynamics_parallel}). Therefore, ZTCF is robust.

The ZTCF result is the "baseline motion" that drift alone would produce. The \emph{actual motion} differs from this baseline because of active muscle control. The difference between actual and ZTCF is the \emph{control-induced perturbation}.

By computing this perturbation directly (rather than trying to invert the forces), the ZTCF framework sidesteps the underdetermined inverse problem entirely.
\end{principle}

\begin{example}{ZTCF for Two-Handed Grip}{ex:ztcf_two_hands_example}
Consider the golf swing with a two-handed grip:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Compute drift forces:} From the measured kinematics (position, velocity, acceleration of joints), compute gravity, Coriolis, and centrifugal forces. These are kinematically determined.

\item \textbf{Forward dynamics with drift only:} Integrate the equation of motion with only drift forces (no muscle torques):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}\,\ddot{\bm{q}} = -\mathbf{C}\,\dot{\bm{q}} - \mathbf{g} + \text{(constraint forces)}
\label{eq:drift_only_forward}
\end{equation}

This is a forward problem (forces given, motion sought). It's well-determined.

\item \textbf{ZTCF prediction:} The solution is the motion that drift alone would produce, subject to the constraints.

\item \textbf{Control input:} The muscle torques are whatever is needed to produce the \emph{actual} motion instead of the ZTCF motion:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_{\text{control}} = \mathbf{M}(\ddot{\bm{q}}_{\text{actual}} - \ddot{\bm{q}}_{\text{ZTCF}}) + \text{(cross terms)}
\label{eq:control_torque_ztcf}
\end{equation}

This control torque is uniquely determined (at the joint level, at least).

\end{enumerate}

By framing the problem as forward dynamics (ZTCF) rather than inverse dynamics, we avoid the ambiguity entirely.
\end{example}

\section{Why Forward Works but Inverse Doesn't: The Asymmetry}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

\begin{laymansbox}{The Direction Matters More Than You Think}{context:direction_asymmetry}
Here is one of the most subtle and important insights in mechanics: \emph{forward integration and inverse inference are not symmetric operations}.

Forward integration is robust. Specify every muscle torque at every instant. Physics takes over and computes the skeleton's motion. This always has a unique answer. Even with loops and constraints, given the applied forces and constraints, the acceleration is unique.

Inverse inference is fragile. Measure the skeleton's motion. Try to reverse-engineer the muscle forces that caused it. In an open chain, this works perfectly. In a closed loop, it fails. The same motion can result from infinitely many different combinations of muscle forces, as long as those combinations produce the same net effect.

The direction matters because causality flows forward (from forces to motion) but measurement flows backward (from motion to forces). These are not inverse operations in the sense of matrix inverse. They are genuinely different logical operations.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{Forward Dynamics: Always Uniquely Solvable}
\label{subsec:forward_unique}

\begin{theorem}{Forward Dynamics is Well-Posed}{thm:forward_wellposed}
Given applied torques $\bm{u}(t)$ and an initial state $\bm{q}(0), \dot{\bm{q}}(0)$, the constrained differential equations
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \bm{M}(\bm{q}) & -\bm{J}_c^T \\ \bm{J}_c & \bm{0} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \ddot{\bm{q}} \\ \boldsymbol{\lambda} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \bm{B}\bm{u} - \bm{h} \\ -\dot{\bm{J}}_c\dot{\bm{q}} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:forward_wellposed}
\end{equation}

always have a unique solution at each time instant. The motion $\bm{q}(t)$ evolves deterministically forward in time.

\textbf{Why?} The coefficient matrix on the left is the KKT matrix (Equation \ref{eq:KKT_matrix}), which is nonsingular for any valid mechanical system. Therefore, the system can be solved uniquely for $\ddot{\bm{q}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ at each instant.
\end{theorem}

Physically: if you know exactly what every muscle is doing, physics determines exactly what the skeleton does. There is no ambiguity.

\subsection{Inverse Dynamics: Not Uniquely Solvable}
\label{subsec:inverse_nonunique}

\begin{theorem}{Inverse Dynamics is Under-Determined in Parallel Systems}{thm:inverse_underdetermined}
Given a measured trajectory $\bm{q}(t), \dot{\bm{q}}(t), \ddot{\bm{q}}(t)$ in a system with loop constraints, the equation
\begin{equation}
\bm{B}(\bm{q})\,\bm{u} + \bm{J}_c^T(\bm{q})\,\boldsymbol{\lambda} = \bm{M}(\bm{q})\,\ddot{\bm{q}} + \bm{h}(\bm{q},\dot{\bm{q}})
\label{eq:inverse_underdetermined_formal}
\end{equation}

does not have a unique solution when $\mathrm{rank}(\bm{B}) < p$ or when internal constraint forces couple with applied torques.

The applied torques $\bm{u}$ lie in an affine subspace of dimension at least 1. The constraint forces $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ are usually uniquely determined.

\textbf{Why?} The measurement gives us $n$ equations (one per coordinate). The unknowns are $\bm{u}$ (applied) and $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ (constraint). If there are more unknowns than equations, the system is underdetermined.
\end{theorem}

Physically: knowing how the skeleton moved does not fully specify what the muscles were doing. The muscles' effort can be distributed infinitely many ways among the different muscle groups, as long as their combined effect matches the observed motion.

\section{Synthesis: What Inverse Dynamics Can and Cannot Tell You}
\label{sec:synthesis_inverse_dynamics}

\index{identifiable}\index{unidentifiable}\index{inverse dynamics limitations}

Let's consolidate what we've learned about the limits and possibilities of inverse dynamics:

\begin{laymansbox}{Inverse Dynamics: The Final Summary}{context:inverse_summary}

\textbf{What inverse dynamics CAN tell you (uniquely, from motion capture alone):}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The total net force and moment required at each joint.
\item The constraint forces (e.g., grip force, ground reaction force).
\item The ground reaction force and torque (if force plates are available).
\item The sequential dynamics of the lower body (ankle → knee → hip, with force plate boundary condition).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{What inverse dynamics CANNOT tell you (without additional information):}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The distribution of load between the two hands or two feet (in a parallel system).
\item Individual muscle forces or activation patterns (without EMG or optimization assumptions).
\item Internal stresses in tissues (without models of passive tissue properties).
\item Which of multiple kinematically equivalent motor strategies the golfer is actually using.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{How to break the ambiguity:}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Add force/moment sensors at the grip (instrumented clubs).
\item Add EMG sensors to the relevant muscles.
\item Apply an optimization criterion (e.g., minimize metabolic cost).
\item Use sport-specific knowledge (e.g., professional golfers likely use near-optimal strategies).
\end{enumerate}

\end{laymansbox}

\begin{exercise}{Numerical: Conditioning of Inverse Dynamics}
