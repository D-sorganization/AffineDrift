\chapter{Motor Control I: The Brain as Controller}
\label{ch:motor_control_brain}

\epigraph{The nervous system solves the equations of motion every time you swing---not by computing them explicitly, but by encoding centuries of evolutionary wisdom about the structure of dynamics.}{}

\begin{laymansbox}{What This Chapter Is About}{box:ch24_intro}
We have spent the preceding chapters studying the mechanics of the golfer--club system: the nonlinear dynamics, the drift-dominated regime, and the trade-off between control and drift. We now turn to a different question. The equations of motion only prescribe what happens given an input trajectory $\bm{u}(t)$. Something has to \emph{generate} that trajectory, and in a human golfer that something is the central nervous system. Across a population of reasonably skilled players the resulting clubhead speeds are reported to vary by only a few percent swing-to-swing, even though the downswing itself lasts only a few hundred milliseconds and every stage of the sensorimotor loop is subject to delays and noise on the order of tens of milliseconds \cite{Kandel2013}. The ranges and mechanisms are discussed quantitatively in the sections that follow.

The chapter reframes the nervous system as a control system in the engineering sense and asks what kind of controller it is. The organizing observation is that the brain does not need to generate every detail of the motion from scratch: it can lean on the drift field $\bm{f}(\bm{x})$ for most of the acceleration and use the active input $\bm{G}(\bm{x})\bm{u}$ for timing and steering. This is the same decomposition we have been developing throughout the book, now viewed from the controller's side.
\end{laymansbox}

\section{The Control Problem: What the Brain Must Solve}
\label{sec:ch24_control_problem}

\index{nonlinear control}
\index{optimal control}
\index{feedforward control}
\index{feedback control}

Recall from Chapters \ref{ch:05_affine_structure} and \ref{ch:zero_torque_counterfactual} that the golf swing is a control-affine system:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\bm{x}} = f(\bm{x}) + G(\bm{x})\bm{u}(t)
\label{eq:ch24_control_affine}
\end{equation}

The state vector $\bm{x}$ contains all joint angles and angular velocities. The drift term $f(\bm{x})$ represents gravity and centrifugal effects---the physics does for free. The control term $G(\bm{x})\bm{u}(t)$ represents what the muscles can accomplish. The control input $\bm{u}(t)$ is the vector of muscle torques, subject to physical bounds: $|\bm{u}| \leq \bm{u}_{\mathrm{max}}$.

The brain's problem: compute $\bm{u}(t)$ as a function of time to produce a desired motion that puts the ball at the target.

This is not a simple problem. It is a \textit{nonlinear optimal control problem in real time}, subject to harsh constraints:

\begin{constraintbox}{Constraints on the Brain's Control Problem}{box:ch24_constraints}
The numerical ranges below are drawn from the neuroscience and motor-control literature. Where sources disagree we give the reported range rather than a point estimate.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Neural transmission delay:} Motor commands travel from the motor cortex down the corticospinal tract to the muscles. Reported peripheral conduction times for upper-limb motoneurons fall in the tens of milliseconds, with the full cortex-to-muscle path typically reported in the 20--50~ms range \cite{Kandel2013, shadmehr2005computational}. The downswing itself lasts on the order of a few hundred milliseconds, so the motor command that initiates the acceleration phase must be launched well before the visible motion begins.

\item \textbf{Activation dynamics:} A motor neuron firing does not produce instantaneous torque. Muscle force rises with a time constant typically reported in the 40--100~ms range depending on the muscle and the task, reflecting the combined electromechanical delay and the twitch dynamics of the active fibers \cite{Zajac1989, Kandel2013}. Torque changes faster than this are not physically realizable by biological actuators.

\item \textbf{Sensory feedback delay:} Proprioceptive information from muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs reaches cortex with reported latencies of a few tens of milliseconds; visual feedback is substantially slower because of retinal transduction and cortical processing, typically reported on the order of 100~ms; vestibular feedback is the fastest of the three \cite{Kandel2013, shadmehr2005computational, shadmehr2010computational}. A downswing is too short for a visual feedback loop to close within it.

\item \textbf{Noisy sensors:} Proprioceptive acuity varies by joint and is coarser at proximal joints than distal ones \cite{Kandel2013}. Vision has high spatial acuity but slow feedback. The nervous system must therefore work with uncertain, delayed measurements and cannot be treated as a perfect-information controller.

\item \textbf{Noisy actuators (signal-dependent noise):} Muscle force variability grows approximately linearly with the commanded force, an observation formalized by \cite{harris1998signal} and subsequently incorporated into most optimal-control models of the motor system \cite{Todorov2002}. This is why maximum-effort swings are typically less reproducible than moderate-effort swings.

\item \textbf{Bounded control:} Peak torques are limited. Proximal joints (shoulder, elbow) can produce substantially larger torques than distal joints (wrist, fingers). The brain cannot exceed these physiological limits; any control strategy must respect the per-joint torque envelope.
\end{enumerate}
\end{constraintbox}

\begin{example}{Delays in the Golf Swing}{ex:ch24_delay_golf}
The downswing takes approximately 250--300~ms. Consider the delay budget:
\begin{itemize}
\item Visual processing: $\sim$100~ms from retina to visual cortex
\item Proprioceptive processing: $\sim$50~ms from muscle spindles to cortex
\item Motor planning and command generation: $\sim$50--100~ms
\item Neural transmission to muscles: $\sim$10--20~ms
\item Electromechanical delay (muscle activation): $\sim$40--60~ms
\end{itemize}
Total round-trip delay: approximately 250--330~ms. This exceeds the entire downswing duration. The implication is stark: \textbf{no corrective feedback is possible during the downswing.} The entire downswing is a pre-programmed ballistic action. Every ``correction'' you feel yourself making was actually planned before the downswing began, triggered by predicted (not actual) sensory feedback.
\end{example}

\subsection{Feedforward vs. Feedback: A Crucial Distinction}
\label{subsec:ch24_ff_vs_fb}

\index{feedforward control}
\index{feedback control}
\index{closed-loop control}
\index{open-loop control}

A traditional control system can work in one of two ways:

\begin{definition}{Feedback (Closed-Loop) Control}{def:ch24_feedback}
The controller measures the current state $\bm{x}(t)$, compares it to the desired state $\bm{x}_d(t)$, computes an error $\bm{e}(t) = \bm{x}_d(t) - \bm{x}(t)$, and adjusts the control input based on this error:
\begin{equation}
\bm{u}(t) = \bm{K}\bm{e}(t) = \bm{K}[\bm{x}_d(t) - \bm{x}(t)]
\end{equation}
where $\bm{K}$ is a gain matrix.

This is reactive: if things are going wrong, the controller corrects.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Feedforward (Open-Loop) Control}{def:ch24_feedforward}
The controller uses a \textit{model} of the system to compute the required control input in advance:
\begin{equation}
\bm{u}(t) = \bm{u}_{\mathrm{planned}}(t)
\end{equation}

This is pre-computed, before feedback arrives. No error correction is possible during execution.
\end{definition}

Now here is the critical insight: \textbf{the golf downswing is dominated by feedforward control}.

Why? The timescales don't work out. Suppose a visual feedback signal detected an error at time $t=100$ ms (early in the downswing). This signal takes 80--150 ms to reach the visual cortex. By the time it arrives, we're at $t \approx 180$--250 ms. The motor system then needs 50--100 ms to generate a corrective command. The correction reaches the muscles at $t \approx 230$--350 ms. But the swing is over by $t \approx 300$ ms. The correction arrives too late.

\begin{mythreality}{The Feedback Fantasy}{box:ch24_feedback_fantasy}
\textbf{Fantasy:} The golf swing is controlled by visual feedback. I see the clubface angle and correct it in real time.

\textbf{Reality:} By the time visual information about clubface angle reaches your motor cortex, the downswing is finished. The correction arrives after impact. What you \textit{think} is feedback is actually \textit{learning from your last swing}. The golf swing is planned and executed as a feedforward command.
\end{mythreality}

Proprioceptive feedback is faster (30--50 ms) but still problematic. A proprioceptive error signal arrives in time for at most one correction cycle. The elite golfer does not rely on this. Instead, the swing is executed as a \textit{ballistic} movement---once it starts, it unfolds according to a pre-planned motor program, with only minimal real-time adjustments.

This realization has significant implications. It means the brain does its real work \textit{before} the swing starts: encoding the initial conditions, setting muscle stiffness, and preparing the motor program. The execution phase is largely deterministic.

\section{Internal Models: The Brain's Simulator}
\label{sec:ch24_internal_models}

\index{internal models}
\index{forward model}
\index{inverse model}
\index{cerebellum}

If the golf swing is feedforward, how does the brain know what command to send? It has a model. The brain maintains an \textit{internal model}---a neural simulation of the body's dynamics.

\begin{definition}{Forward Model}{def:ch24_forward_model}
A forward model predicts the sensory consequences of a motor command. Given the current state $\bm{x}(t)$ and a motor command $\bm{u}(t)$, the forward model predicts the next state:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\bm{x}}(t+\Delta t) = \hat{f}(\bm{x}(t)) + \hat{G}(\bm{x}(t))\bm{u}(t)
\end{equation}

Mathematically, it IS solving the nonlinear dynamics equation. The brain becomes a physicist in practice, even if not in theory.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Inverse Model}{def:ch24_inverse_model}
An inverse model computes the motor command needed to achieve a desired sensory outcome. Given a desired state change $\bm{x}_d(t)$, the inverse model computes:
\begin{equation}
\bm{u}(t) = \hat{G}(\bm{x}(t))^{-1}[\dot{\bm{x}}_d(t) - \hat{f}(\bm{x}(t))]
\end{equation}

This is the inverse dynamics problem: ``What muscles do I fire to move the club from here to there?''
\end{definition}

The forward and inverse models are intimately related. The brain learns them together. A forward model is easy to differentiate numerically to obtain an approximate inverse model: $\bm{u} \approx \hat{G}^{-1}(\bm{x}_d - \hat{f})$.

\subsection{The Cerebellum as the Seat of Internal Models}
\label{subsec:ch24_cerebellum}

\index{cerebellum}
\index{cerebellar learning}
\index{Purkinje cells}

Where in the brain do these internal models live? The leading candidate is the \textit{cerebellum}, a structure at the base of the brain.

The cerebellum is remarkable for several reasons:

\begin{principle}{Cerebellar Architecture}{box:ch24_cerebellum_arch}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Size:} The cerebellum is only ~10\% of the brain's volume, but contains ~70\% of all neurons (approximately 69 billion granule cells---making it the most neuron-dense structure in the brain. It also contains roughly 15 million Purkinje cells, the large output neurons that project to the deep cerebellar nuclei).

\item \textbf{Computational density:} This makes the cerebellum the most densely packed neural tissue in the brain---a parallel processor of extraordinary power.

\item \textbf{Simple circuit:} The cerebellar circuit is highly stereotyped and mathematically simple. It's one of the few neural circuits we nearly understand.

\item \textbf{Learning rule:} The cerebellum uses a clear, identified learning mechanism: long-term depression (LTD) of synapses. When a Purkinje cell receives simultaneous input from a climbing fiber (error signal) and parallel fibers (motor plan), the parallel fiber--Purkinje synapses are weakened. This is an error-correction learning rule.

\item \textbf{Timing:} The cerebellum operates fast, with learning that can occur within a single trial for simple adaptation tasks.
\end{itemize}
\end{principle}

Cerebellar damage produces catastrophic loss of motor coordination---a condition called \textit{ataxia}. Patients with cerebellar ataxia cannot perform smooth, coordinated movements. They cannot hit a golf ball. They cannot reach smoothly for a cup. This is because they have lost their internal model of how their body works.

The cerebellar learning rule is thought to implement the following algorithm:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch24_cerebellar_learning}
\Delta w_{ij} = -\eta \cdot e(t) \cdot x_i(t) \cdot y_j(t)
\end{equation}

where $w_{ij}$ is the synaptic weight between parallel fiber $i$ and Purkinje cell $j$, $e(t)$ is the climbing fiber error signal, $x_i(t)$ is the firing rate of parallel fiber $i$, and $y_j(t)$ is the firing rate of the Purkinje cell. The negative sign indicates that errors drive depression of relevant synapses. Over time, this makes the forward model more accurate.

This is a simplified form of the Marr-Albus-Ito hypothesis. More precisely, the climbing fiber carries an error signal, and parallel fiber synapses that are co-active with climbing fiber input undergo long-term depression (LTD). The learning rule above captures the essential structure: weights decrease when error and pre-synaptic activity coincide.

\cite{wolpert1998internal, flanagan2003control, kawato1999internal}

\subsection{Why Internal Models Matter}
\label{subsec:ch24_why_models_matter}

Without an internal model, the brain cannot solve the golf swing. Here's why:

\begin{example}{Controlling Without a Model}{ex:ch24_no_model}
Suppose you didn't have an internal model. You swing the club and see (after 150 ms) that the clubface was open. You generate a correction. But this correction is based on what your muscles did 150 milliseconds ago, when the club was in a different position. The dynamics are nonlinear---the effect of a torque at position $\bm{x}_1$ is different from the effect of the same torque at position $\bm{x}_2$. Applying a correction based on delayed feedback at the wrong state is futile.

With an internal model, you \textit{predict} what will happen before it happens. You mentally simulate the swing. If the simulation predicts an open clubface, you adjust the plan \textit{before} you swing.
\end{example}

The forward model enables \textit{predictive control}. The inverse model enables \textit{planning}. Together, they enable the feedforward computation of motor commands that reliably produce desired outcomes.

\section{The Predictive Processing Framework}
\label{sec:ch24_predictive_processing}

\index{predictive processing}
\index{free energy principle}
\index{prediction error}
\index{active inference}

Modern neuroscience has converged on a powerful unifying idea: the brain is fundamentally a \textit{prediction machine}. This framework, developed by Karl Friston and others, is called the \textit{free energy principle} and the \textit{predictive processing} framework \cite{friston2010action, clark2013whatever}.

\begin{definition}{Predictive Processing}{def:ch24_predictive_processing}
The brain doesn't passively receive sensory information. Instead, it constantly generates predictions about what it expects to sense. Perception works by comparing predictions with actual sensations. When they match, the brain's model of the world is good. When they don't match, a \textit{prediction error} occurs, and the brain updates its model.
\end{definition}

For the golf swing, this framework explains several phenomena:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Pre-swing planning:} Before you swing, your brain generates a predicted trajectory. This is your forward model running forward: ``If I fire these muscles at these times, the club will follow this path, and the ball will land there.'' This is an \textit{active inference}---you're inferring which motor command will make the world match your prediction (the target).

\item \textbf{During-swing monitoring:} As you swing, your brain continuously compares predicted sensory signals with actual signals. The proprioceptive feedback is compared against the forward model's predictions. If they match, all is well. If they don't, there's a prediction error.

\item \textbf{Post-swing learning:} After the swing, a large prediction error is available: the ball lands at location $\bm{x}_{\mathrm{actual}}$ instead of the predicted location $\bm{x}_{\mathrm{predicted}}$. This error drives learning. The brain updates its internal model to reduce the error on the next swing.

\item \textbf{Lack of time for correction:} During a 300 ms downswing, the brain has time for at most one or two prediction error cycles. Thus, most corrections cannot happen mid-swing. The brain relies on the prior learning.
\end{enumerate}

This framework has significant implications. It says that action is not fundamentally different from perception. Both work through prediction and error correction. The goal of a motor action is to make the actual sensory consequences match your predicted sensory consequences. For the golf swing, the predicted sensory consequence is the \textit{feeling} of a well-struck shot---the proprioceptive pattern that you've learned corresponds to good contact.

\section{Ideomotor Theory: Think the Result, Not the Process}
\label{sec:ch24_ideomotor}

\index{ideomotor theory}
\index{action effect}
\index{motor planning}

William James, in his 1890 \textit{Principles of Psychology}, proposed a radical idea: \textbf{actions are represented by their effects, not by their motor commands}. When you think about throwing a ball, you think about the trajectory of the ball, not the sequence of muscle contractions. Your motor system then computes the commands needed to produce that trajectory.

Modern ideomotor theory \cite{prinz2002perception, hommel2009theory} has formalized this insight:

\begin{theorem}{Ideomotor Principle}{thm:ch24_ideomotor}
An action is coded as a set of desired sensory consequences (the effect). When the action goal is activated, the motor system automatically retrieves the motor commands that, in the past, produced those consequences. The action is executed by commands designed to minimize discrepancy between the predicted sensory consequences and the desired consequences.
\end{theorem}

This has a striking implication for golf coaching: \textbf{the coach should focus the golfer's attention on the desired outcome, not on the mechanics of movement}.

\begin{example}{Ideomotor in Golf}{ex:ch24_ideomotor_golf}
Consider two coaching approaches:

\textbf{Mechanics-focused:} ``Keep your head still. Shift your weight to your front side. Lag the club. Extend through the ball.''

\textbf{Outcome-focused:} ``Feel the swing shape. See the ball flight. Feel the contact. Swing to the target.''

According to ideomotor theory, the second approach is more effective. Here's why: when you think about mechanics, you're thinking about motor commands (``fire the pectoral muscle'', ``lag the club''). This bypasses the action effect pathway. Your motor system is confused---it's trying to both retrieve learned commands and generate new commands based on mechanical rules.

When you think about the outcome (the ball flight, the feeling of good contact), you activate the action effect. Your motor system automatically retrieves the learned motor program that produces that effect. No confusion. No conflict.

This explains why great coaches talk about feel and target, not mechanics.
\end{example}

The ideomotor principle also connects to the drift field. In the ZTCF framework, the brain doesn't need to control every detail of the trajectory. Instead, it specifies the initial conditions and lets physics (the drift field) carry the system to the outcome. In ideomotor terms, the brain specifies the desired sensory consequence (impact with the ball, ball trajectory), and the motor system retrieves the commands that, given physics, produce that consequence.

\cite{prinz2002perception, hommel2009theory, wolfensteller2016action}

\section{The Nervous System Architecture: Hardware Specifications}
\label{sec:ch24_hardware}

\index{motor cortex}
\index{spinal cord}
\index{basal ganglia}
\index{muscle unit recruitment}

To understand how the brain controls movement, we need to understand its hardware architecture. The nervous system is not a single, monolithic controller. It's a hierarchy of controllers, each operating at a different timescale and with different capabilities.

\subsection{The Motor Cortex}
\label{subsec:ch24_motor_cortex}

The primary motor cortex (M1) is located in the frontal lobe, just in front of the central sulcus. It contains roughly 100,000 corticospinal neurons per hemisphere, each sending axons down through the spinal cord to synapse onto motor neurons.

\begin{definition}{Corticospinal Tract}{def:ch24_corticospinal}
The major pathway for voluntary motor control. Fibers originate in the motor cortex, travel through the brain and brainstem, cross the midline (decussate) in the medulla, and descend the spinal cord. Each M1 neuron controls the muscles on the \textit{opposite} side of the body.
\end{definition}

Signal transmission in these fibers is fast---roughly 70--120 m/s in myelinated fibers \citep{Kandel2013}. This means a motor command takes on the order of 30--50 ms to reach the upper limb muscles.

An important principle:

\begin{principle}{Motor Cortex Population Coding}{box:ch24_pop_coding}
The motor cortex doesn't work one neuron per muscle. Instead, it uses \textit{population coding}. Thousands of neurons, each with slightly different directional preferences, are active simultaneously. The vector sum of their activity encodes the direction and magnitude of the desired motion. This population code is robust: loss of individual neurons has little effect because the information is distributed.
\end{principle}

\subsection{The Spinal Cord: Local Control Centers}
\label{subsec:ch24_spinal_cord}

The spinal cord is not just a cable carrying commands down and feedback up. It contains \textit{local circuits} that can coordinate movement independently of the brain.

\begin{definition}{Central Pattern Generator}{def:ch24_cpg}
A neural circuit that produces rhythmic output without rhythmic input. CPGs in the spinal cord generate patterns like walking, swimming, or scratching. They can operate autonomously, though they're normally modulated by brain signals.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Stretch Reflex (Monosynaptic Reflex)}{def:ch24_stretch_reflex}
A rapid response to muscle stretch. When a muscle is stretched, spindle receptors send a signal directly to motor neurons in the spinal cord, which fire and contract the muscle. This entire loop happens in about 30 milliseconds, \textit{without brain involvement}. The brain doesn't know about it until after it happens.
\end{definition}

For the golf swing, the spinal cord implements local stabilizing reflexes. If a gust of wind pushes your arm off its intended path, spinal reflexes resist the perturbation. The brain doesn't have time to react---visual feedback latencies far exceed the duration of a single correction cycle---so the spinal cord handles it automatically \citep{Kandel2013}.

\subsection{The Basal Ganglia: Action Selection}
\label{subsec:ch24_basal_ganglia}

\index{basal ganglia}
\index{action selection}
\index{dopamine}

The basal ganglia are a set of nuclei deep in the brain. Their primary function is \textit{action selection}---deciding which motor program to execute.

\begin{definition}{Motor Program}{def:ch24_motor_program}
A learned sequence of muscle activations that produces a stereotyped action (like a golf swing). Once selected and initiated, the program unfolds with minimal brain intervention.
\end{definition}

The basal ganglia use a neurochemical signal called \textit{dopamine} to encode reward prediction errors. When you execute a motor program and it produces a better-than-expected outcome (a good shot), dopamine neurons fire. This signal reinforces the basal ganglia circuits that led to that action. Over time, actions that produce rewards become more likely.

\begin{example}{Basal Ganglia in Golf}{ex:ch24_bg_golf}
The pre-shot routine involves several motor programs:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The waggle (small oscillations of the club)
\item The step(s) to position
\item The alignment checks
\item The final address
\end{enumerate}

Each of these is a learned motor program. The basal ganglia select which routine to execute and in what order. Once the routine is selected, it unfolds automatically.

When you hit a great shot, dopamine neurons fire (reward). The basal ganglia strengthen the circuits that selected that particular pre-shot routine. Over time, the routine becomes more automatic.

When you hit a poor shot, dopamine neurons are quiet (no reward). The circuits that selected that routine weaken. You're less likely to use that routine next time.

This is \textit{reinforcement learning} implemented in neural circuits.
\end{example}

Parkinson's disease, which damages dopamine neurons, produces severe difficulty in action selection. Patients know what they want to do, but they cannot initiate movement. They can stand frozen, unable to take a step. This illustrates how critical the basal ganglia are for movement initiation.

\subsection{Motor Unit Recruitment: Henneman's Size Principle}
\label{subsec:ch24_recruitment}

\index{motor units}
\index{Henneman's size principle}
\index{recruitment order}

A motor unit is a motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it innervates. A single motor neuron controls 10--1000 muscle fibers, depending on the muscle. The smaller the motor neuron, the fewer fibers it controls.

\begin{principle}{Henneman's Size Principle}{box:ch24_size_principle}
Motor neurons are recruited in order of size: small (slow, weak) units first, large (fast, strong) units last \cite{henneman1965relation}. This ordering is automatic and doesn't require brain control. It's determined by the electrophysiology of the motor neuron pool.
\end{principle}

This principle has important consequences:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{You can't produce maximum force instantly.} To achieve high forces, you must recruit the large motor units. But you can't recruit them without first recruiting all the small units. This takes time---typically 100--500 milliseconds.

\item \textbf{At low effort, you have precision control.} With only small units active, forces are fine-graded and controllable. This is why you can write with a pen or play piano (low forces, high precision).

\item \textbf{At high effort, you lose precision.} At maximum effort, all motor units are active, and you can't fine-tune force. This is why maximum-effort movements are ballistic and imprecise.

\item \textbf{The golf swing transitions from low force to high force.} During setup and the backswing, forces are moderate and the system is highly controllable. As the transition approaches, forces increase. By the downswing, the system is in a high-force regime where ballistic execution is the only option.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bandwidth Limitations: The Brain's Processing Speed}
\label{sec:ch24_bandwidth}

\index{bandwidth limitation}
\index{control bandwidth}
\index{sampling rate}

A fundamental constraint on any controller is its bandwidth---the rate at which it can process information and generate corrections.

\begin{definition}{Control Bandwidth}{def:ch24_bandwidth}
The maximum frequency at which a controller can detect errors and generate corrective responses. For the brain, the limiting factor is not neural transmission speed (which is fast---30--120 m/s) but the time taken to process information and compute responses.
\end{definition}

The conscious brain updates motor plans at roughly 4--10~Hz (one update every 100--250~ms). Visual flicker fusion occurs at $\sim$60~Hz, but conscious decision-making and motor replanning are far slower. This mismatch is critical: sensory data arrives faster than the brain can act on it.

\begin{example}{Flicker Fusion Frequency}{ex:ch24_flicker}
A light flickering at 60 Hz appears steady to your eye, even though it's actually off half the time. This is because 60 Hz exceeds your conscious bandwidth. Each on--off cycle is too fast to perceive as distinct.
\end{example}

For the golf swing, this bandwidth limitation is catastrophic:

\begin{constraintbox}{The Bandwidth Problem in Golf}{box:ch24_bandwidth_problem}
A 300 millisecond downswing at 10 Hz bandwidth means the brain can process roughly 3 distinct time windows during the swing:
\begin{itemize}
\item $t=0$--100 ms: transition phase (beginning of acceleration)
\item $t=100$--200 ms: acceleration phase (maximum force)
\item $t=200$--300 ms: deceleration phase (approaching impact)
\end{itemize}

This is barely enough for one feedback correction cycle. Moreover, sensory feedback for the first window doesn't arrive until after the second window has ended.

In contrast, the golf club itself oscillates at roughly 50--200 Hz during the swing (the fundamental resonance frequencies of the club). The brain's 10 Hz bandwidth is much slower than the club's 100 Hz dynamics.

This is a \textit{mismatch between the controller bandwidth and the plant dynamics}---a classic problem in control engineering, which we encountered as the Disturbance Rejection vs. Control (DCR) problem in Chapter \ref{ch:zero_torque_counterfactual}.
\end{constraintbox}

The solution, as we learned in the physics chapters, is to exploit drift. When the plant (the golf swing) is drift-dominated, a slow controller can still achieve good performance by specifying initial conditions and letting physics do the rest. The brain doesn't need to correct the swing trajectory moment-by-moment. It only needs to get the initial conditions right.

\cite{todorov2005generalized, shadmehr2005computational}

\section{The Brain's Processing Power: Energy and Computation}
\label{sec:ch24_power}

\index{brain power consumption}
\index{computational capacity}
\index{neural computation}

How much ``horsepower'' does the brain have for controlling movement?

\begin{principle}{Brain Power Budget}{box:ch24_power_budget}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Energy consumption:} The brain uses roughly 20 watts of power at rest, about 20\% of the body's total metabolic rate. This seems like a lot, but it's actually quite efficient for the amount of computation being performed.

\item \textbf{Computational capacity:} Estimates of the brain's computational capacity vary widely depending on what counts as an `operation.' If each of the brain's $\sim 10^{14}$ synapses fires at $\sim$10~Hz, the brain performs roughly $10^{15}$ synaptic operations per second \citep{wolpert2011motor}. Under more liberal definitions (including subthreshold integration), estimates reach $10^{16}$--$10^{18}$. These numbers are uncertain, but the order of magnitude is instructive.

\item \textbf{Per-neuron computation:} With ~100 billion neurons, this is about $10^5$ operations per neuron per second, or roughly 100 floating-point operations per action potential. This is much less than a CPU/GPU (which can do $10^9$ operations per cycle per core). The brain compensates with massive parallelism.
\end{itemize}
\end{principle}

However, the brain doesn't use its power to solve optimization problems online. Instead, it learns solutions offline and recalls them online.

\begin{example}{Online Optimization vs. Offline Learning}{ex:ch24_online_vs_offline}
A robotic arm might use real-time trajectory optimization: at each time step (every millisecond), solve a local optimal control problem to compute the next motor command. This requires substantial computation per time step.

The brain does something different: it \textit{learns}, over thousands of trials, a motor program that reliably produces the desired outcome. Then, at execution time, it simply \textit{recalls} and \textit{executes} the learned program. This is much cheaper computationally.

The trade-off: the robot can adapt to novel situations (different dynamics, different payloads) within seconds. The brain requires hours or days to adapt. But the robot uses more power to do it.
\end{example}

This distinction is fundamental to understanding biological motor control. The brain is not solving the golf swing problem online. It's solving it offline, during practice. The swing itself is the execution of a pre-learned motor program.

\begin{principle}{Key Takeaways}{box:ch24_takeaways}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The golf swing is a feedforward, ballistic movement. Once initiated, it unfolds according to a pre-planned motor program. Sensory feedback is too slow to correct the swing trajectory in real time.

\item The brain controls movement using internal models---forward models that predict sensory consequences, and inverse models that compute motor commands. The cerebellum is the likely neural substrate for these models.

\item The brain operates as a predictive processing system. It constantly generates predictions and compares them to actual sensory input. Prediction errors drive learning.

\item Actions are represented by their effects (ideomotor theory), not by motor commands. The brain specifies a desired outcome, and the motor system retrieves the commands that produce that outcome.

\item The nervous system is a hierarchy: the motor cortex (high-level planning), the spinal cord (local control), and the basal ganglia (action selection) all contribute.

\item Bandwidth limitations are severe. The brain operates at 10--40 Hz, while the club oscillates at 50--200 Hz. The brain solves this by exploiting the drift-dominated physics of the swing.

\item The brain learns solutions offline (during practice) and executes them online (during performance). This is more efficient than online optimization, but less flexible.
\end{enumerate}
\end{principle}
