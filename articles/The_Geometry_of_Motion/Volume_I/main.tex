\chapter{Preface}

This book grew from a simple observation that proved surprisingly difficult to articulate cleanly: \emph{superposition is exact in tangent spaces}. Not approximately true. Not ``good enough for small perturbations.'' Exact---by the definition of what a tangent space is.

That observation is not new mathematics. Calculus established it centuries ago. What is new is the insistence that control theory, dynamics, and engineering practice should fully internalize what calculus already proved. When we linearize a nonlinear system, we are not making an approximation. We are computing the exact infinitesimal structure of the dynamics at a point. The approximation enters only when we step away from that point---and even then, the error is not sloppiness but geometry: the curvature of the manifold.

This book develops that perspective systematically. We show that contraction theory, trajectory optimization (LQR, DDP, MPC), and the decomposition of motion into drift and control all rest on the same geometric foundation: \emph{tangent-space dynamics with a metric}.

The treatment is aimed at graduate students and practicing engineers who have encountered linearization, Lyapunov stability, and optimal control as separate topics and want to see them unified. We assume familiarity with linear algebra, multivariable calculus, and ordinary differential equations. Differential geometry is introduced as needed, always paired with physical intuition.

Every chapter follows a consistent structure: a qualitative framing of why the material matters, a plain-language explanation, a geometric intuition box, and then the formal development. This is deliberate. The mathematics must be rigorous, but the reader should always know \emph{why} a calculation is being done before seeing \emph{how}.

A word on notation: we are deliberately explicit. Every matrix dimension, every smoothness assumption, every domain restriction is stated. This is not pedantry---it is the scaffolding that prevents the subtle errors that plague ``hand-wavy'' treatments of linearization and superposition.

\chapter{Differential Geometry Primer}
\label{app:diffgeo}

This appendix collects the key definitions from differential geometry used throughout the
text. The presentation is deliberately concrete; for full generality, see
do~Carmo~\cite{doCarmo1992} or Abraham and Marsden~\cite{AbrahamMarsden1978}.

\section{Smooth Manifolds}

A \emph{smooth manifold} $\mathcal{M}$ of dimension $n$ is a topological space that locally
looks like $\R^n$. Formally, it is equipped with an atlas of charts $\{(U_\alpha, \phi_\alpha)\}$
where each $U_\alpha \subset \mathcal{M}$ is open and $\phi_\alpha: U_\alpha \to \R^n$ is a
homeomorphism, with smooth transition maps $\phi_\beta \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1}$ on overlaps.

\textbf{Examples used in this book:}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\R^n$ (state space of most control systems)
\item $S^1$ (circle; configuration space of a revolute joint)
\item $\mathbb{T}^n = (S^1)^n$ (torus; configuration space of an $n$-joint robot)
\item $\SO(3)$ (rotation group; attitude of a rigid body)
\item $\SE(3)$ (rigid-body pose: rotation + translation)
\end{itemize}

\section{Tangent Spaces and Tangent Bundles}

The \emph{tangent space} $T_p\mathcal{M}$ at a point $p \in \mathcal{M}$ is the vector space
of all tangent vectors at $p$. It can be defined as the set of equivalence classes of curves
through $p$, or as the set of derivations on smooth functions at $p$. In either case,
$T_p\mathcal{M} \cong \R^n$.

The \emph{tangent bundle} $T\mathcal{M} = \bigcup_{p \in \mathcal{M}} T_p\mathcal{M}$ is itself
a smooth manifold of dimension $2n$. A point in $T\mathcal{M}$ is a pair $(p, v)$ with
$p \in \mathcal{M}$ and $v \in T_p\mathcal{M}$. For mechanical systems, $T\mathcal{M}$ is the
\emph{state space} $(\q, \dot\q)$.

\section{Vector Fields and Flows}

A \emph{vector field} $f$ on $\mathcal{M}$ assigns a tangent vector $f(p) \in T_p\mathcal{M}$
to each point $p$. In coordinates, $f = (f_1(\x), \ldots, f_n(\x))^T$. The ODE
$\dot\x = f(\x)$ defines the \emph{flow} $\varphi_t: \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{M}$, which maps
initial conditions to their time-$t$ images.

\section{Riemannian Metrics}

A \emph{Riemannian metric} on $\mathcal{M}$ is a smoothly varying inner product
$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_p$ on each tangent space $T_p\mathcal{M}$. In coordinates,
it is represented by a positive-definite matrix $\mat{G}(p)$:
\[
\langle v, w \rangle_p = v^T \mat{G}(p) w.
\]
For mechanical systems, the mass matrix $M(\q)$ is a Riemannian metric on configuration space.
The contraction metric $\mat{M}(\x)$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:contraction} is a Riemannian metric on
state space.

\section{Lie Groups and Lie Algebras}

The most important manifolds in rigid-body control are matrix Lie groups. A Lie group is both
a smooth manifold and a group, with smooth multiplication and inversion.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\SO(3) = \{ \mat{R}\in\R^{3\times 3}: \mat{R}^T\mat{R}=\mat{I},\,\det(\mat{R})=1\}$ (rotations)
\item $\SE(3) = \left\{\begin{bmatrix}\mat{R}&\vec{p}\\\vec{0}^T&1\end{bmatrix}: \mat{R}\in\SO(3),\,\vec{p}\in\R^3\right\}$ (poses)
\end{itemize}

Their Lie algebras are tangent spaces at identity:
\[
\mathfrak{so}(3)=\{\mat{\Omega}\in\R^{3\times 3}:\mat{\Omega}^T=-\mat{\Omega}\},\quad
\mathfrak{se}(3)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\mat{\Omega}&\vec{v}\\\vec{0}^T&0\end{bmatrix}\right\}.
\]
Using the hat map $\widehat{\omega}$, angular velocity vectors become algebra elements. This is
the algebraic backbone of variational dynamics in Chapter~\ref{ch:variational} and motion
composition in Chapter~\ref{ch:applications}.

\section{Exponential and Logarithmic Maps}

The exponential map converts tangent directions into finite motions. For $\SO(3)$:
\[
\exp(\widehat{\omega}\theta)=\mat{I}
+\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\widehat{\omega}\theta
+\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}(\widehat{\omega}\theta)^2.
\]
Given $\mat{R}\in\SO(3)$ with $\theta=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\trace(\mat{R})-1}{2}\right)$, the
log map recovers axis-angle:
\[
\log(\mat{R})=\frac{\theta}{2\sin\theta}(\mat{R}-\mat{R}^T).
\]
For $\SE(3)$, the exponential combines rotation with translated screw motion and is used directly
in product-of-exponentials kinematics and geodesic interpolation.

\begin{example}{Python Example: SO(3) Exp/Log}{app-so3-exp-log}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm, logm

def hat(w: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]],
                     [w[2], 0, -w[0]],
                     [-w[1], w[0], 0]], dtype=float)

w = np.array([0.3, -0.2, 0.5])
R = expm(hat(w))              # exp map to SO(3)
w_hat = logm(R).real          # log map back to so(3)
\end{lstlisting}
\end{example}

\section{Adjoint Representation}

Adjoint operators transport twists and wrenches across frames:
\[
\mathrm{Ad}_{(\mat{R},\vec{p})}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\mat{R} & \vec{0}\\
[\vec{p}]_\times\mat{R} & \mat{R}
\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathrm{ad}_{(\omega,v)}=
\begin{bmatrix}
[\omega]_\times & \vec{0}\\
[v]_\times & [\omega]_\times
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
In Chapter~\ref{ch:applications}, this map explains why equivalent perturbations look different
between body-fixed and world-fixed coordinates.

\section{Fiber Bundles}

Tangent and cotangent bundles are canonical fiber bundles:
\[
\pi_T:T\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M},\quad \pi_T(p,v)=p,\qquad
\pi_{T^*}:T^*\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}.
\]
The base point is configuration; the fiber stores admissible velocities ($T\mathcal{M}$) or
covectors/momenta ($T^*\mathcal{M}$). This viewpoint clarifies why state and costate evolve on
different but coupled geometric objects in Chapters~\ref{ch:optimal-control}
and~\ref{ch:duality}.

\section{Connections and Parallel Transport}

A connection specifies how to compare tangent vectors at different points. In local coordinates,
the Levi-Civita connection of metric $\mat{G}$ has Christoffel symbols
$\Gamma^k_{ij}$. Covariant acceleration is
\[
\frac{D\dot{q}^k}{dt}=\ddot{q}^k+\Gamma^k_{ij}\dot{q}^i\dot{q}^j.
\]
Parallel transport ($D v/dt=0$ along a curve) is the right way to carry perturbations when
curvature is nonzero. This geometric correction underlies stable trajectory tracking on curved
configuration manifolds.

\section{Curvature and Trajectory Sensitivity}

Curvature quantifies how geodesics separate. Sectional curvature $K(u,v)$ for a 2-plane
$\Span\{u,v\}\subset T_p\mathcal{M}$ predicts local sensitivity:
\begin{itemize}
\item $K>0$: geodesics tend to reconverge (spherical-like geometry)
\item $K<0$: geodesics diverge faster (hyperbolic-like sensitivity)
\end{itemize}
Contraction analysis in Chapter~\ref{ch:contraction} can be interpreted as enforcing metric
dynamics that dominate this geometric divergence.


\chapter{Linear Algebra for Control}
\label{app:linalg}

\section{Matrix Inequalities and the Loewner Order}

For symmetric matrices $\mat{A}, \mat{B} \in \R^{n \times n}$, we write:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mat{A} \succ 0$ ($\mat{A}$ is \emph{positive definite}): $v^T\mat{A}v > 0$ for all $v \neq 0$.
\item $\mat{A} \succeq 0$ ($\mat{A}$ is \emph{positive semidefinite}): $v^T\mat{A}v \geq 0$ for all $v$.
\item $\mat{A} \succeq \mat{B}$: $\mat{A} - \mat{B} \succeq 0$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Key property:} The set of positive semidefinite matrices forms a \emph{convex cone}.
This is why LMI-based searches for contraction metrics (Chapter~\ref{ch:contraction}) are
convex optimization problems.

\section{Schur Complement}

For a block matrix $\mat{M} = \begin{bmatrix} \mat{A} & \mat{B} \\ \mat{B}^T & \mat{C} \end{bmatrix}$
with $\mat{C} \succ 0$:
\begin{equation}
\mat{M} \succ 0 \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \mat{A} - \mat{B}\mat{C}^{-1}\mat{B}^T \succ 0.
\end{equation}
The matrix $\mat{A} - \mat{B}\mat{C}^{-1}\mat{B}^T$ is the \emph{Schur complement} of $\mat{C}$
in $\mat{M}$. This identity is used extensively in Riccati equation derivations
(Chapters~\ref{ch:optimal-control} and~\ref{ch:duality}) and in the mass matrix decomposition
for flexible systems (Chapter~\ref{ch:applications}).

\section{Singular Value Decomposition}

Every matrix $\mat{A} \in \R^{m \times n}$ has an SVD: $\mat{A} = \mat{U}\mat{\Sigma}\mat{V}^T$,
where $\mat{U}$ and $\mat{V}$ are orthogonal and $\mat{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with non-negative
entries $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0$. The SVD is used in this book for:
\begin{itemize}
\item Analyzing the STM $\Phi(t_1, t_0)$: singular values quantify amplification of
      perturbations (Chapter~\ref{ch:variational}).
\item Impact sensitivity analysis: singular values of $J_{\text{impact}} \cdot C \cdot \Phi$
      reveal the most sensitive perturbation directions (Chapter~\ref{ch:applications}).
\item Condition number $\kappa(\mat{A}) = \sigma_1/\sigma_n$: measures sensitivity to
      perturbations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Matrix Exponential}

For $\mat{A} \in \R^{n \times n}$, the matrix exponential is:
\[
e^{\mat{A}t} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(\mat{A}t)^k}{k!}.
\]
It satisfies $\frac{\dd}{\dd t} e^{\mat{A}t} = \mat{A} e^{\mat{A}t}$ and is the state
transition matrix for LTI systems: $\Phi(t, t_0) = e^{\mat{A}(t-t_0)}$.

\textbf{Caution:} For time-varying $\mat{A}(t)$, $\Phi(t, t_0) \neq e^{\int_{t_0}^t \mat{A}(s)\,\dd s}$
in general. Equality holds only when $[\mat{A}(t_1), \mat{A}(t_2)] = 0$ for all $t_1, t_2$
(the commutativity condition for matrix exponentials).

\section{Kronecker Products}

The Kronecker product $\mat{A}\otimes\mat{B}$ expands block structure and is essential for
vectorized Lyapunov/Riccati equations:
\[
\mathrm{vec}(\mat{A}\mat{X}\mat{B})
=(\mat{B}^T\otimes\mat{A})\mathrm{vec}(\mat{X}).
\]
For $\mat{A}^T\mat{P}+\mat{P}\mat{A}+\mat{Q}=0$, vectorization yields
\[
\big(\mat{I}\otimes\mat{A}^T+\mat{A}^T\otimes\mat{I}\big)\mathrm{vec}(\mat{P})
=-\mathrm{vec}(\mat{Q}),
\]
which is directly useful in Chapter~\ref{ch:duality}.

\section{Matrix Calculus}

Control derivations repeatedly require gradients with respect to vectors and matrices:
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^T\mat{A}x)=(\mat{A}+\mat{A}^T)x,\quad
\frac{\partial}{\partial \mat{X}}\trace(\mat{A}^T\mat{X})=\mat{A}.
\]
These identities support second-order expansions in DDP and local quadratic models in
Chapter~\ref{ch:optimal-control}.

\section{Generalized Eigenvalue Problems}

Many stability questions appear as matrix pencils:
\[
\mat{A}v=\lambda \mat{B}v,\quad \det(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{B})=0.
\]
Generalized eigenanalysis is required when constraints, descriptor dynamics, or mass matrices
lead to non-identity state weighting.

\section{Perturbation Theory and Pseudospectra}

Eigenvalues can be highly sensitive in non-normal systems. The $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum is
\[
\Lambda_\varepsilon(\mat{A})=
\{z\in\mathbb{C}: \|(z\mat{I}-\mat{A})^{-1}\|>1/\varepsilon\}.
\]
This captures transient growth that eigenvalues alone miss, which is directly relevant to
``small perturbation, large excursion'' behavior in Chapter~\ref{ch:variational}.

\section{Sparse Matrix Methods}

Large-scale discretizations produce sparse operators. Storing sparse matrices in CSR/CSC formats
reduces memory and enables scalable iterative solvers (CG, GMRES, MINRES). This matters for
trajectory optimization and model predictive control where linear solves dominate runtime.

\begin{example}{Python Example: Sparse Solve with SciPy}{app-sparse-solve}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import numpy as np
import scipy.sparse as sp
import scipy.sparse.linalg as spla

n = 2000
A = sp.diags([2*np.ones(n), -np.ones(n-1), -np.ones(n-1)], [0, -1, 1], format="csc")
b = np.ones(n)
x = spla.spsolve(A, b)
\end{lstlisting}
\end{example}

\section{LMI Fundamentals}

Linear matrix inequalities encode convex constraints such as Lyapunov stability:
\[
\mat{P}\succ 0,\qquad \mat{A}^T\mat{P}+\mat{P}\mat{A}\prec 0.
\]
Controller synthesis and contraction metric search can be cast as semidefinite programs.
In practice, this is solved with CVX-family tools. A minimal Python workflow uses \texttt{cvxpy}.

\begin{example}{Python Example: Lyapunov LMI in cvxpy}{app-lmi-cvxpy}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import cvxpy as cp
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [-2.0, -0.4]])
P = cp.Variable((2, 2), PSD=True)
constraints = [A.T @ P + P @ A << -1e-3 * np.eye(2), P >> 1e-3 * np.eye(2)]
problem = cp.Problem(cp.Minimize(cp.trace(P)), constraints)
problem.solve(solver=cp.SCS)
\end{lstlisting}
\end{example}
