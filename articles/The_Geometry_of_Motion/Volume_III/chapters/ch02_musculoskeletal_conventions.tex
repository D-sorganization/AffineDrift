\chapter[Musculoskeletal Modeling Conventions]{Musculoskeletal\\Modeling Conventions}
\label{ch:msk-conventions}

The same measured motion can yield different reported angles when frame axes,
calibration poses or rotation conventions differ. A useful model must connect
those choices to its inertial parameters and force calculations. Consistency of
coordinates is necessary for physical interpretation; it does not establish
the accuracy of the measurements or identify the subject's controller.

\section{Anatomical Reference Frames}

Biomechanical analysis requires a standardized language for describing body position and
motion. The International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) has published recommendations
for joint coordinate systems \cite{Wu2002, Wu2005}. These are joint-specific
recommendations, not a single universal axis assignment or angle sequence.

\subsection{The Anatomical Position}

The \textbf{anatomical position} provides a reference for directional terminology:
the subject stands upright with arms at the sides and palms facing forward.
It does not automatically define every model's zero joint configuration.
Record the calibration pose, anatomical landmarks, segment-frame offsets and
the convention used to report zero. A subject's measured standing pose need
not coincide with an idealized anatomical neutral pose.

\begin{definition}[unbreakable]{Global Reference Frame}{global-frame}
  For the examples here, choose a fixed global (laboratory) frame $\{G\}$ as:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\hat{\bm{x}}_G$: anterior (forward)
    \item $\hat{\bm{y}}_G$: superior (upward)
    \item $\hat{\bm{z}}_G$: right
  \end{itemize}
  This is a right-handed coordinate system, aligned with the subject in the
  chosen reference pose; it does not rotate with the subject. Right is not
  synonymous with lateral on both limbs. Other laboratory and software systems
  use different conventions. \textbf{Verify handedness, axis directions,
  units and frame origins before combining data.}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Segment Reference Frames}

In a rigid-segment model, a local frame is attached to each segment. Anatomical
frames can be constructed from landmarks and estimated joint centers; a marker
cluster's technical frame generally requires a separate anatomical calibration.
Skin-mounted markers need not remain rigidly attached to the underlying bone.
Report the landmark definitions and estimation method, rather than assuming
that a measured technical frame is already the desired anatomical frame.

\begin{example}{The Thigh Reference Frame}{thigh-frame}
  Wu et al.'s femoral frame for hip reporting \cite{Wu2002}, Section~4.4,
  uses the following definitions:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Origin}: hip joint center
    \item $\hat{\bm{y}}_T$: from the epicondyle midpoint toward the hip center
    \item $\hat{\bm{z}}_T$: in the plane formed by the two epicondyles
          and the hip center, perpendicular to $\hat{\bm{y}}_T$, pointing right
    \item $\hat{\bm{x}}_T$: cross product $\hat{\bm{y}}_T \times \hat{\bm{z}}_T$
          (anterior direction)
  \end{itemize}
  Normalize the axes and check that the landmarks define a nondegenerate plane.
  A frame located at the knee can also be useful, but its origin must be
  transported explicitly; it is not this attributed ISB origin.

  The recommendation favors functional hip-center estimation when adequate
  relative motion is available and discusses predictive alternatives.
  Anthropometric regression, such as Harrington et al.'s MRI-based method
  \cite{Harrington2007}, estimates a center; it does not make that center an
  exact observed landmark or remove calibration and population uncertainty.
\end{example}

\subsection{Connection to Screw Theory}

In the language of Volume~0, each segment frame $\{S_i\}$ is related to the global
frame $\{G\}$ by a homogeneous transformation matrix $T_{GS_i} \in \SE$:
\begin{equation}
  T_{GS_i} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{GS_i} & \bm{p}_{GS_i} \\ \bm{0}^T & 1 \end{bmatrix},
  \label{eq:ch2:homogeneous}
\end{equation}
where $R_{GS_i} \in \SO$ maps segment-coordinate vectors into global coordinates;
its columns are the segment axes expressed globally. The vector
$\bm{p}_{GS_i} \in \Reals^3$ locates the segment origin globally. Thus a point
satisfies $\bm{x}_G=R_{GS_i}\bm{x}_{S_i}+\bm{p}_{GS_i}$.

The \emph{relative configuration} of segment $j$ with respect to proximal segment $i$
is:
\begin{equation}
  T_{S_i S_j} = T_{GS_i}^{-1} T_{GS_j}.
  \label{eq:ch2:relative-config}
\end{equation}
The rotation block is $R_{S_iS_j}=R_{GS_i}^T R_{GS_j}$; the translation is
$R_{GS_i}^T(\bm{p}_{GS_j}-\bm{p}_{GS_i})$. A common rigid change of laboratory
coordinates leaves this relative transform unchanged. Anatomical recalibration
is different: changing the attached segment frames changes the reported
relative coordinates and must also change any frame-dependent inertial data.

\section{Joint Coordinate Systems}

Joint-coordinate conventions relate relative orientation to anatomical terms.
The chosen axes, positive directions, limb side, neutral offsets and angle
branches must all be stated. An Euler/Cardan decomposition is a coordinate
description of the resulting orientation, not a measured temporal sequence
of three isolated motions.

\subsection{The Grood--Suntay Convention for the Knee}

The knee joint coordinate system, originally proposed by Grood and Suntay (1983)
\cite{GroodSuntay1983}, decomposes knee motion into:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Flexion-Extension} ($\alpha$): rotation about the femoral medio-lateral
        axis (the ``fixed'' axis of the femur)
  \item \textbf{Abduction-Adduction} ($\beta$): rotation about the ``floating'' axis
        (perpendicular to both the femoral and tibial body-fixed axes)
  \item \textbf{Internal-External Rotation} ($\gamma$): rotation about the tibial
        longitudinal axis (the ``body-fixed'' axis of the tibia)
\end{enumerate}

The floating axis is perpendicular to the two body-fixed axes; its directed
cross product depends on the declared sign convention. It is undefined when
those axes are parallel. With suitable segment-axis assignments, the
geometric construction can be represented by a ZXY Cardan chart. The labels
ZXY alone do not specify clinical signs for both limbs.

The following listing deliberately computes the \emph{mathematical chart}
in Equation~\eqref{eq:ch2:euler-isb}. It returns active right-hand angles in
degrees, with the middle angle on $(-90^\circ,90^\circ)$ and outer angles on
their principal atan2 branches. It does not automatically label those values
as clinical flexion, adduction or internal rotation. Apply a documented
anatomical calibration and side/sign mapping before making that interpretation.
Input matrices must already be proper rotations; noisy marker data require
a separately stated pose-estimation procedure.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Extracting a declared active ZXY chart from segment orientations.}]
import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

def segment_zxy_angles(
    R_proximal: NDArray[np.float64],
    R_distal: NDArray[np.float64],
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Return chart angles; no clinical side/sign mapping is inferred."""
    frames = []
    for frame in (R_proximal, R_distal):
        if np.iscomplexobj(frame):
            raise ValueError("Expected real rotations")
        R = np.asarray(frame, dtype=float)
        if R.shape != (3, 3) or not np.all(np.isfinite(R)):
            raise ValueError("Expected finite 3-by-3 rotations")
        if not np.allclose(R.T @ R, np.eye(3), atol=1e-9, rtol=0):
            raise ValueError("Rotation columns must be orthonormal")
        if not np.isclose(np.linalg.det(R), 1, atol=1e-9, rtol=0):
            raise ValueError("Rotation must be right-handed")
        frames.append(R)
    R = frames[0].T @ frames[1]
    cos_beta = np.hypot(R[0, 1], R[1, 1])
    if cos_beta < 1e-8:
        raise ValueError("ZXY chart is singular or too ill-conditioned")
    alpha = np.arctan2(-R[0, 1], R[1, 1])
    beta = np.arctan2(R[2, 1], cos_beta)
    gamma = np.arctan2(-R[2, 0], R[2, 2])
    angles = np.degrees([alpha, beta, gamma])
    return float(angles[0]), float(angles[1]), float(angles[2])
\end{lstlisting}

The singularity threshold is an illustrative numerical guard, not a sensor
accuracy specification. Near the singular set, a small orientation error can
produce a large angle error. Crossing an outer-angle branch cut can also create
a $360^\circ$ reporting jump without a comparable physical movement.

\subsection{Multi-Representation Joint Angles}

Following the multi-representation philosophy of this series, we express joint
rotations and rigid displacements in complementary representations:

\textbf{1. Declared Active Intrinsic ZXY Angles:}
Write $R_{ij}=R_{S_iS_j}$ for the distal-to-proximal coordinate map:
\begin{equation}
  R_{ij} = R_z(\alpha) \, R_x(\beta) \, R_y(\gamma),
  \label{eq:ch2:euler-isb}
\end{equation}
Here the three angles are mathematical coordinates with right-hand positive
rotations. They require the mapping discussed above before receiving clinical
names. For $(60^\circ,5^\circ,10^\circ)$ the listing recovers the same triple.
The middle sine is the entry in row~3, column~2, written \texttt{R[2, 1]}
in Python; negating it would instead return $-5^\circ$.

\textbf{2. Rotation Matrix (SO(3)):}
Use $[\bm{a}]_\times\bm{b}=\bm{a}\times\bm{b}$ for the skew map:
\begin{equation}
  R_{ij} = \exp([\bm{\omega}]_\times \theta) \in \SO,
  \label{eq:ch2:rotmat}
\end{equation}
For a rotation, $\bm{\omega}$ is a unit axis and $\theta$ an angle. A matrix
stores nine entries with six independent orthonormality constraints and the
additional choice $\det R=+1$. It avoids an Euler chart's gimbal lock, but its
axis-angle logarithm is not globally unique; the axis is undefined at identity.

\textbf{3. Unit Quaternion:}
\begin{equation}
  \bm{q} = (\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2},\; \bm{\omega}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}),
  \quad \norm{\bm{q}} = 1,
  \label{eq:ch2:quaternion}
\end{equation}
A unit quaternion avoids Euler-chart gimbal lock, but $\bm{q}$ and $-\bm{q}$
represent the same rotation. Choose consistent signs for interpolation and
state angle branches when converting back. Neither a quaternion nor a matrix
removes singularities of the requested clinical-angle chart.

\textbf{4. Screw Axis (Primary):}
\begin{equation}
  T_{ij} = e^{[\screw]\theta} \in \SE,
  \quad \screw = (\bm{\omega}, \bm{v}) \in \Reals^6,
  \label{eq:ch2:screw}
\end{equation}
For a finite displacement with nonzero rotation, normalize
$\norm{\bm{\omega}}=1$; then $\theta$ is a rotation angle and $\bm{v}$ encodes
the axis location and translation per unit angle. Here $[\screw]$ denotes
the $4\times4$ twist matrix with upper blocks $[\bm{\omega}]_\times$ and
$\bm{v}$ and a zero bottom row. For pure translation use
$\bm{\omega}=0$, $\norm{\bm{v}}=1$ and a displacement parameter in units of
length. Identity has no uniquely determined screw axis. Rotation alone does
not determine $T_{ij}$: its translation must also be supplied.

\begin{comparison}{Why Screw Axis Theory Is Natural for Biological Joints}{screw-bio}
  A biological articulation need not obey an ideal fixed revolute constraint.
  Relative translation and rotation can both matter, with their relationship
  dependent on joint geometry, loading and the modeled task. A finite screw
  describes the displacement between two configurations; an instantaneous
  screw axis describes a velocity. They need not coincide, and either can
  change during movement. At zero angular velocity an ordinary finite-pitch
  instantaneous axis is unavailable; pure translation is a distinct case.

  The rigid-displacement framework in Lynch and Park's \emph{Modern Robotics}
  \cite{ModernRoboticsRigidExpReview} is useful for representing these motions.
  Representation alone supplies neither a biological joint law nor evidence
  that a fixed one-degree-of-freedom screw model is adequate.
\end{comparison}

\section{Body Segment Parameters}

\subsection{Regression Equations}

Body segment parameters (BSPs) include mass, center-of-mass location and the
inertia tensor about a specified point and axes. A parameter table is tied to
its segment boundaries, reference landmarks, measurement/modeling method and
sample population. Examples illustrate why these conditions matter:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{de Leva (1996)} \cite{deLeva1996} adjusts the reference points
        of Zatsiorsky--Seluyanov parameters derived from young-adult male and
        female samples. This is a population-based adjustment, not direct
        measurement of every modeled subject or a universal gold standard.
  \item \textbf{Pavol et al.\ (2002)} \cite{Pavol2002BSPReview} describe an
        estimation method for older adults that incorporates body measurements
        and existing data sources. Its validation scope must accompany its use.
  \item \textbf{Jensen (1986)} \cite{Jensen1986BSPReview} studies growth-related
        changes in boys using a longitudinal sample and a geometric body model.
        Pediatric parameters should not be assumed to equal scaled adult values.
\end{enumerate}

Distinguish a mean proportion from a subject-specific estimate and propagate
plausible parameter uncertainty into the output of interest. Transferring a
center-of-mass fraction between different segment endpoints changes its
physical meaning. Likewise, a diagonal inertia tensor in principal axes need
not remain diagonal in an anatomical frame. Changing coordinate axes rotates
the tensor; changing its reference point additionally requires the parallel-axis
theorem. These operations do not compensate for an inappropriate population
or an inaccurate segment model.

\subsection{Computing the Mass Matrix}

Given segment inertial properties, the mass matrix $M(\bm{q}) \in \Reals^{n \times n}$
of the multi-body system is computed using the composite rigid-body algorithm
(see Volume~0's recursive-dynamics treatment). Use angular-first motion
vectors $V_i=(\bm{\omega}_i,\bm{v}_{O_i})$, where $\bm{v}_{O_i}$ is the
segment-origin velocity expressed in that segment's frame. This is the body
twist convention; a spatial twist's linear block in a fixed laboratory frame
is generally not the segment-origin velocity. In the same body frame,
the spatial inertia is \cite{FeatherstoneSpatialV1Review}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{G}_i = \begin{bmatrix} I_i & m_i [\bm{c}_i]_\times \\
  m_i [\bm{c}_i]_\times^T & m_i \mathbb{I}_3 \end{bmatrix} \in \Reals^{6 \times 6},
  \label{eq:ch2:spatial-inertia}
\end{equation}
where $I_i$ is the $3 \times 3$ rotational inertia about the segment frame origin,
$m_i$ is the segment mass, $\bm{c}_i$ is the center of mass position in the segment
frame directed from its origin to the COM, and
$[\bm{c}]_\times\bm{a}=\bm{c}\times\bm{a}$. If the supplied rotational
inertia is instead the central tensor $I_{C_i}$, first form
\begin{equation}
 I_i=I_{C_i}+m_i\bigl(\norm{\bm{c}_i}^2\mathbb{I}_3
              -\bm{c}_i\bm{c}_i^T\bigr).
 \label{eq:ch2:parallel-axis}
\end{equation}
Do not add that term again when $I_i$ is already about the origin.

The off-diagonal signs follow from point transport,
$\bm{v}_{C_i}=\bm{v}_{O_i}+\bm{\omega}_i\times\bm{c}_i$:
\begin{equation}
 \tfrac12 V_i^T\mathcal{G}_i V_i
 =\tfrac12\bm{\omega}_i^T I_{C_i}\bm{\omega}_i
  +\tfrac12m_i\norm{\bm{v}_{O_i}+\bm{\omega}_i\times\bm{c}_i}^2.
 \label{eq:ch2:energy}
\end{equation}
A symmetric positive-definite matrix alone is not sufficient verification:
reversing both cross blocks is equivalent to using the opposite COM offset
and can still give a positive-definite matrix. Check energy and momentum
against point transport. Physical central principal moments are nonnegative
and satisfy triangle inequalities; nondegenerate central inertia gives positive
definiteness, while point/line idealizations can be semidefinite.

If $V_i=J_i(\bm{q})\dot{\bm{q}}$ with consistent origins and axes, summing
segment energies gives $M(\bm{q})=\sum_i J_i^T\mathcal{G}_iJ_i$ for this
rigid-segment model. Euler-angle rates are not Cartesian angular-velocity
components. Generalized forces follow from power: a segment wrench $F_i$
contributes $J_i^TF_i$, not an arbitrary selection of its Cartesian entries.
For a golf swing, for example, an apparent change in joint angles or inferred moments can
reflect calibration, parameter or coordinate choices as well as a change in
the swing. A coordinate correction is not evidence of a different muscle or
neural strategy.

\section*{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Coordinate System Verification.}
        Verify that the declared ZXY listing returns zero for aligned frames
        and recovers mixed positive and negative chart angles. Premultiply both
        input frames by the same rotation and verify invariance. Specify the
        additional calibration and side/sign mapping needed for clinical labels.

  \item \textbf{Euler Angle Singularity.}
        Identify the singular ZXY configurations and explain why they depend
        on the middle angle, not on flexion magnitude alone. Perturb a near-singular
        rotation and quantify angle sensitivity. Distinguish this chart problem
        from a physiological restriction; no claim about gait or squat ranges
        follows from the algebra alone.

  \item \textbf{Multi-Representation Conversion.}
        Write Python functions to convert between all four representations
        (Euler ZXY, rotation matrix, quaternion, screw displacement) for chart
        angles $(60^\circ,5^\circ,10^\circ)$ and a separately specified
        translation $\bm{p}=(0.01,0,0)^T$ m. State angle/logarithm branches and
        check reconstruction. Explain why the three angles alone are insufficient
        for the rigid-displacement conversion.

  \item \textbf{BSP Sensitivity.}
        For an isolated horizontal rigid segment of mass $4$ kg, compare COM
        distances $0.20$ m and $0.22$ m from a fixed support using $g=9.81$
        m/s$^2$. Compute the balancing moment magnitudes and percentage change.
        These are manufactured parameter choices, not two published population
        estimates. Explain why the net support moment does not identify muscle
        force without moment arms and load-sharing assumptions.

  \item \textbf{(Programming.)} Implement the spatial inertia matrix computation
        for a 3-segment leg model (thigh, shank, foot), using the companion
        repository's \texttt{affine\_control.}\allowbreak\texttt{dynamics} function
        \texttt{spatial\_inertia(mass, com, inertia\_com)}.
        State its central-inertia input convention. Verify symmetry, physical
        central moments and kinetic energy under simultaneous rotation and
        translation, including a nonzero COM offset.

  \item \textbf{(Programming.)} Using motion capture data (or simulated data),
        compute chart angles from segment orientations with the provided listing.
        Document calibration, units, invalid-frame treatment and branch handling.
        Plot a clinical flexion trace only after declaring and checking its
        anatomical mapping; identify simulated data as simulated.
\end{enumerate}
