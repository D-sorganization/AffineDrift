A biological chain combines several kinds of coupling. Inertia couples
accelerations; a muscle path distributes tension across coordinates; a contact
can constrain motion; and muscle states limit how neural excitation changes
force. These mechanisms must be distinguished before interpreting the golf
swing as coordinated or low-dimensional.

\section{Open-Chain vs.\ Closed-Chain Biomechanics}

A tree of rigid segments is a useful starting model, with a floating pelvis
and articulated limbs. Connecting both hands to the same rigid club can close a
loop through the arms and trunk. A fixed foot constrains motion relative to the
world, but one contact does not automatically create the same internal loop as
a two-hand grip. Multiple supports can introduce additional loop constraints.
The result depends on which bodies, joints and contact freedoms the model includes.

For a stationary holonomic constraint $\phi(q)=0$, permissible velocities obey
$J_c(q)v=0$, and accelerations obey
$J_c\dot v+\dot J_c v=0$ in the chosen velocity coordinates.
Ideal constraint reactions do no work on those permissible velocities.
A frictional ground contact also requires unilateral normal force and an
appropriate friction law: solving a bilateral constraint equation does not
prove that the foot remains in contact or does not slip.
Grip compliance and club flexibility likewise change the rigid-loop approximation.

\subsection{Equations of Motion for Biological Chains}

Let $q\in\mathbb R^{n_q}$ describe configuration locally or through a constrained
representation, and let $v\in\mathbb R^{n_v}$ contain generalized speeds.
Write $\dot q=N(q)v$; for independent local angle and translation coordinates,
$N=I$ and $v=\dot q$. A quaternion orientation uses four entries for three
rotational freedoms, so $n_q$ and $n_v$ need not match~\cite{SimbodyMobilitiesBiological}.
A declared rigid-segment model has the form
\begin{equation}
 M(q)\dot v+h(q,v)=R_v(q)F_T+Q_{\mathrm{other}}+J_c(q)^T\lambda,
 \label{eq:ch5:eom-bio}
\end{equation}
where $M$ is $n_v\times n_v$, $h$ includes inertial velocity terms and gravity,
$F_T\in\mathbb R^p$ contains tendon tensions, and $Q_{\mathrm{other}}$ contains
additional modeled loads. These can include passive ligaments, damping or
external actuators; count each contribution once. The reaction vector $\lambda$
and Jacobian must be power-conjugate: $\lambda^TJ_cv$ is contact power.
For a spatial wrench, its force, moment, point and frame must all be declared.

If $\ell_i^{MT}(q)$ is a differentiable musculotendon path length and tension
$F_{T,i}\geq0$ pulls along that path, define
\[
 R_{q,ji}=-\frac{\partial\ell_i^{MT}}{\partial q_j},\qquad
 R_v=N^TR_q,\qquad \dot\ell^{MT}=-R_v^Tv.
\]
Then the muscle contribution and its power are
\begin{equation}
 Q_M=R_vF_T,\qquad Q_M^Tv=-F_T^T\dot\ell^{MT}.
 \label{eq:ch5:net-torque}
\end{equation}
These are generalized loads: angular rows give moments, while translational
rows give forces. Tendon tension need not equal fiber force when pennation or
other transmission geometry intervenes (Chapter~3).
For a free-body description using overall pose and internal joint coordinates,
internal muscle path lengths are invariant to overall rigid motion; muscle
loads cannot supply an independent external base wrench.

Force is an admissible idealized input if the model explicitly assumes direct
force actuation. In a muscle-driven model it instead follows from activation,
contraction and tendon states and their constitutive equations~\cite{millard2013flexing}.
Excitation, activation, fiber force and tendon tension are different quantities.
Prescribing activation bypasses activation dynamics; it does not automatically
specify tendon force or remove the need for contraction assumptions.

\subsection{Bi-Articular Muscles}

A muscle crossing two joints contributes one tension to two generalized loads.
Engineering tendon drives can do this too. Sharing an actuator does not impose
a relation $\phi(q)=0$ between the joint angles; ordinary compliant muscle
coupling therefore does not reduce the configuration dimension.
An imposed fixed musculotendon length would be an additional idealized constraint.
The rank and feasible image of $R_v$ describe instantaneous force allocation,
not the number of kinematic freedoms or nonlinear controllability by themselves.

\begin{principle}{Power Accounting Across Two Joints}{biarticular}
For two local joint angles with moment arms $r_1,r_2$, the coordinate powers are
$P_1=r_1F_T\dot q_1$ and $P_2=r_2F_T\dot q_2$.
Their sum is $-F_T\dot\ell^{MT}$.
Opposite coordinate powers can cancel while tension remains nonzero.
Cancellation establishes a skeletal coordinate-power balance, not zero
metabolic cost or a uniquely identified energy path between physical segments.
If the musculotendon unit shortens, lengthens or stores elastic energy, its
net mechanical exchange must also enter the ledger.
\end{principle}

For a manufactured example, $r_1=-0.03$\,m, $r_2=0.04$\,m,
$F_T=500$\,N and $(\dot q_1,\dot q_2)=(2,1.5)$\,rad/s give
$P_1=-30$\,W, $P_2=30$\,W and $\dot\ell^{MT}=0$.
Changing the second velocity to $1$\,rad/s gives $\dot\ell^{MT}=0.02$\,m/s
and net skeletal power $-10$\,W.
These numbers illustrate the signs; they are not gastrocnemius measurements.
For the swing, the analogous analysis must distinguish joint-coordinate powers,
segment boundary wrench powers and muscle/tendon storage before claiming
proximal-to-distal energy transfer.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Mapping Tendon Tension to Generalized Muscle Loads}]
import numpy as np


def compute_joint_torques(
    moment_arms: np.ndarray,
    muscle_forces: np.ndarray,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Map nonnegative tendon tensions to generalized muscle loads.

    R has shape (n_coordinates, n_muscles), and R[j, i] is
    -d(length_i)/d(q_j). Rotational rows give moments in N*m;
    translational rows give forces in N for meter coordinates.
    This map does not determine tension from neural excitation.
    """
    if np.iscomplexobj(moment_arms) or np.iscomplexobj(muscle_forces):
        raise ValueError("Inputs must be real")
    r = np.asarray(moment_arms, dtype=float)
    force = np.asarray(muscle_forces, dtype=float)
    if r.ndim != 2 or force.ndim != 1 or r.shape[1] != force.size:
        raise ValueError("Require R:(n,p) and tension:(p,)")
    if not np.isfinite(r).all() or not np.isfinite(force).all():
        raise ValueError("Inputs must be finite")
    if np.any(force < 0):
        raise ValueError("Tension must be nonnegative")
    return r @ force


# Manufactured two-angle, four-tendon example; not anatomy data.
R = np.array([[0.05, 0.03, -0.025, 0.0],
              [0.0, 0.04, -0.020, 0.03]])
force = np.array([200.0, 150.0, 100.0, 120.0])
torque = compute_joint_torques(R, force)  # [12.0, 7.6] N*m
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Co-Contraction and Joint Stiffness}

Define restoring tangent stiffness in independent local coordinates as
$K=-\partial Q_M/\partial q$, under stated activation, velocity and internal-state
or equilibrium conditions. In a quasistatic model with
$F_T=F_T(\ell^{MT})$ and $K_{MT}=\partial F_T/\partial\ell^{MT}$,
\begin{equation}
 K=R_qK_{MT}R_q^T-\sum_i F_{T,i}\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial q},
 \label{eq:ch5:joint-stiffness}
\end{equation}
where $r_i$ is column $i$ of $R_q$.
The first term is the material contribution; the second reflects changing
moment arms. A scalar constant-arm model reduces to $K=\sum_i r_i^2k_i$.
A fiber force-length slope is not automatically the effective musculotendon
stiffness or the experimentally measured short-range stiffness.
For aligned, nonpennate linear muscle and tendon springs in series,
$k_{MT}=(k_M^{-1}+k_T^{-1})^{-1}$; pennation, state dependence and history
require their own derivation.

Matched antagonists can increase positive material stiffness while their net
moment cancels. For constant moment arms $+0.03$ and $-0.03$\,m, equal tensions
cancel, and two effective stiffnesses of $1000$\,N/m give
$K=1.8$\,N\,m/rad. Unequal forces or changing geometry need not preserve that
balance. Muscle force-length branches and geometric terms can also change the
sign of tangent stiffness. Neither a universal increase nor independent control
of every impedance component follows from co-contraction alone.
Impedance additionally involves inertia, damping, feedback and timescale;
static tangent stiffness is only one part of it (Chapter~3).

\section{The Mass Matrix of the Human Body}

The composite rigid body algorithm applies after selecting the tree, velocity
coordinates and physically admissible segment inertias. Product-of-exponentials
kinematics is one compatible representation, not a compulsory preprocessing step.
The kinetic energy is $T=\tfrac12v^TM(q)v$.
Positive definiteness requires that every nonzero modeled speed produce positive
kinetic energy; redundant coordinates, singular parameterizations or massless
unregularized freedoms can violate this condition.
A model with 20 independent speeds has a $20\times20$ mass matrix, but naming
body regions does not establish that count.

For constraints represented by $v=Z(q)w$, where $Z$ spans admissible velocities,
the reduced kinetic matrix is $Z^TMZ$. Constraint multipliers and reaction-force
indeterminacy are separate from the positive definiteness of this reduced matrix.
This distinction matters in a two-hand club model: closing the loop changes
admissible motion and reactions without making the underlying segment inertias
nonphysical.
