\chapter{Experimental Methods}
\label{ch:experimental}

\begin{gomdraftnotice}
This chapter is a provisional methods reference.

A biomechanical inference links measurements to a model of the body and its environment.
Camera calibration, marker-to-bone motion, filtering, force frames and clock alignment enter that link together.
A small position residual does not by itself validate joint acceleration, muscle force or tissue loading.
This chapter distinguishes the sensor observation from the inferred physical quantity and identifies checks needed before combining measurements.

\subsection{Marker-Based Systems}

Optoelectronic systems estimate marker positions from calibrated camera images.
Camera arrangement, exposure, sampling, marker identification and calibration volume determine the observation conditions~\cite{chiari2005human}.
Marker counts, diameters and nominal submillimeter instrument tolerances are configuration specifications, not universal biomechanical accuracy bounds.
A protocol must state its anatomical landmarks, tracking clusters, occlusion handling and segment-frame construction.
An instrument's sampling rate also does not determine its end-to-end latency or its ability to resolve a short impact event.

A camera tracks the visible marker rather than the underlying bone.
Anatomical landmark placement, skin motion and the assumed joint geometry therefore enter the estimated bone pose~\cite{leardini2005human}.
For golf, the same distinction applies to a torso marker cluster, a club-mounted marker and the particular point whose velocity is reported.
A calibration check should include independent positions and motions over the intended working volume, with results expressed in the units and directions relevant to the task.

\subsection{Markerless Systems}

Video methods can estimate image keypoints, triangulated landmarks or model-based body states, depending on the camera configuration and estimator.
These outputs are different objects: a visible image feature is not automatically an anatomical joint center in three dimensions.
Accuracy depends on task, viewpoint, training domain, occlusion, clothing and the reference used for evaluation.
A universal 10--30\,mm error band or a blanket clinical-suitability judgment cannot be assigned to all markerless approaches.
Validation must address the intended downstream quantity; position agreement alone does not validate differentiated accelerations or inverse-dynamics moments.

\subsection{An Offline Processing Example}

The following listing processes finite, uniformly sampled marker positions in meters; timestamps and gap handling must be established before calling it.
It uses second-order filter sections for numerical stability and filters along the time axis~\cite{SciPyButterExperimental,SciPySosFiltFiltExperimental}.
The caller supplies a cutoff rather than inheriting a universal 6\,Hz choice.
This is an offline forward--backward operation that uses future samples, not a causal real-time controller.
At the specified single-pass Butterworth cutoff, ideal forward--backward amplitude is $1/2$ (approximately $-6$\,dB), since the single-pass magnitude is squared.
The returned velocities and accelerations are finite-difference estimates, with unavailable endpoint derivatives marked as NaN rather than copied from neighbors.
Padding can still affect nearby samples; record length and a justified edge-exclusion interval must be checked for each application.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Offline Marker Processing With Declared Frequency and Boundary Limits}]
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import butter, sosfiltfilt


def process_mocap_data(
    marker_data: np.ndarray,
    sample_rate: float,
    cutoff_freq: float,
    filter_order: int = 4,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Offline example for finite, uniformly sampled positions in meters.

    Input shape is (frames, markers, 3). cutoff_freq is the
    single-pass Butterworth -3 dB frequency, not the two-pass
    -3 dB frequency. Returned positions, velocities and accelerations
    have the input shape and floating dtype. Endpoint derivatives
    are NaN; additional edge exclusion needs task-specific checking.
    Missing markers must be handled explicitly before this function.
    """
    if np.iscomplexobj(marker_data):
        raise ValueError("Positions must be real-valued")
    data = np.asarray(marker_data, dtype=float)
    if data.ndim != 3 or data.shape[1] == 0 or data.shape[2] != 3:
        raise ValueError("Expected shape (frames, markers, 3)")
    if not np.isfinite(data).all():
        raise ValueError("Resolve missing/nonfinite positions first")
    sample_rate, cutoff_freq = float(sample_rate), float(cutoff_freq)
    if not np.isfinite(sample_rate) or sample_rate <= 0:
        raise ValueError("sample_rate must be positive and finite")
    if not np.isfinite(cutoff_freq) or not 0 < cutoff_freq < sample_rate / 2:
        raise ValueError("Require 0 < cutoff_freq < Nyquist frequency")
    if (
        isinstance(filter_order, (bool, np.bool_))
        or not isinstance(filter_order, (int, np.integer))
        or filter_order < 1
    ):
        raise ValueError("filter_order must be a positive integer")

    padlen = 3 * (int(filter_order) + 1)
    if data.shape[0] <= padlen + 1:
        raise ValueError("Record too short for the declared padding")
    sos = butter(filter_order, cutoff_freq, fs=sample_rate, output="sos")
    positions = sosfiltfilt(sos, data, axis=0, padlen=padlen)
    dt = 1.0 / sample_rate
    velocities = np.full_like(positions, np.nan)
    accelerations = np.full_like(positions, np.nan)
    velocities[1:-1] = (positions[2:] - positions[:-2]) / (2 * dt)
    accelerations[1:-1] = (
        positions[2:] - 2 * positions[1:-1] + positions[:-2]
    ) / dt**2
    return positions, velocities, accelerations
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Soft Tissue Artifact}
\label{sec:sta}

Soft tissue artifact (STA) is motion of skin-mounted markers relative to bone.
It can be a major source of error even when the cameras localize each marker precisely~\cite{leardini2005human}.
Peters and colleagues' review found reported magnitudes exceeding 30\,mm on the thigh and reaching 15\,mm on the tibia, with substantial variation and limitations in the included populations~\cite{peters2010quantification}.
These are findings from lower-limb studies, not a universal interval for every marker, subject or swing.

STA can contain task-correlated motion within the frequency band of the biological signal; low-pass filtering therefore cannot generally separate it from bone movement.
Cluster fitting can reduce sensitivity to some local errors but cannot remove a coherent displacement of the whole cluster relative to bone.
Likewise, constrained inverse kinematics enforces a model and redistributes residuals; a smaller residual is not proof of more accurate bone motion.
Report both translational and orientation uncertainty and test sensitivity of the inferred loads to plausible marker and joint-frame errors.
Bone-referenced methods provide a different validation route, subject to their own calibration, field-of-view and measurement limitations.

\subsection{Low-Pass Filter Cutoff Selection}
\label{sec:filter-cutoff}

A low cutoff can remove genuine rapid motion, while a high cutoff retains noise amplified by differentiation.
In the frequency domain, differentiation multiplies a component by $i2\pi f$, and a second derivative by $-(2\pi f)^2$.
It changes spectral weighting, not the frequency of a sinusoid.
The need for acceleration therefore does not automatically prescribe a higher cutoff.

Winter's residual-analysis construction compares samples $x_i$ of one scalar coordinate trajectory with filtered samples $\hat{x}_i(f_c)$~\cite[Section 3.4.4.3]{Winter2009}:
\[
R(f_c)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\bigl(x_i-\hat{x}_i(f_c)\bigr)^2}.
\]
Here $N$ is the number of samples and $f_c$ is the candidate cutoff frequency.
If a suitable high-frequency portion of the residual curve is approximately linear, extrapolate that fitted portion to obtain an ordinate intercept $R_0$.
That intercept has the signal's units, such as meters; it is not a frequency.
The described compromise chooses the frequency where the residual curve intersects the horizontal level $R=R_0$.
This is a conditional signal/noise heuristic, not a guaranteed global optimum or a universal walking, running or golf cutoff.
State the fitted interval, filter response, endpoint treatment and sensitivity to plausible alternative choices.

Filtering must also be considered across modalities.
Kristianslund and colleagues showed that changing the relationship between force and marker filtering changed inferred joint moments and participant rankings in a sidestep-cutting study~\cite{Kristianslund2012Filtering}.
A sharp force transient combined with strongly smoothed kinematics can leave incompatible terms in the equations of motion.
That result motivates task-specific joint assessment of force and kinematic bandwidth; it does not establish one cutoff for every experiment.
Preserve raw data and inspect event timing, derivatives and dynamic residuals after processing.

\section{Electromyography (EMG)}

EMG records electrical potentials associated with muscle-fiber activity through a particular electrode and recording arrangement.
Surface EMG is affected by electrode geometry, placement, tissue conduction, crosstalk and movement artifact; it is not a direct readout of a unique neural command or muscle force.
Intramuscular recordings sample differently and should not inherit a surface-electrode processing protocol without justification.
An EMG-informed force estimate needs additional activation, contraction and tendon assumptions; agreement with kinematics alone does not identify those assumptions.

\subsection{EMG Processing and Interpretation}

Specify the recording bandwidth, sample rate and analog anti-aliasing before selecting digital filters.
A common envelope workflow is band-pass filtering, full-wave rectification and low-pass smoothing, but each frequency setting must match the recording and question.
Filter transients and smoothing alter onset and peak timing; a smooth envelope is not an instantaneous excitation measurement.
Any downsampling requires its own anti-aliasing justification.

Normalization expresses an amplitude relative to a stated reference measurement, not automatically as a fraction of maximal force.
The CEDE consensus distinguishes several normalization approaches, including task-specific maximal contractions, standardized contractions and within-task references~\cite{Besomi2020Normalization}.
Report the protocol, reference statistic, repeatability and any reason the reference effort may not represent the task.
A dynamic amplitude exceeding a recorded MVC reference is not in itself a mathematical impossibility.
Interpretation of comparisons between muscles, sessions or participants depends on what the chosen normalization preserves and removes.

\section{Force Plates}

A six-component force plate reports a calibrated force and moment wrench about a specified origin.
Rated load, sample rate and nominal CoP accuracy must come from the actual device and calibration, including loading position and magnitude.
Before combining it with motion capture, transform both force and moment into the declared laboratory frame and account for the change of moment origin.

For an explicit example, choose a right-handed frame with $+z$ upward, origin $O$ at the wrench-reference point and contact plane $z=h$.
Let $(\bm F,\bm M_O)$ denote the ground-on-person wrench in that frame, with $F_z>0$ during loaded stance.
The signed height $h$ is positive when the reference origin lies below the surface.
At the CoP, $\bm r=(x_{\mathrm{CoP}},y_{\mathrm{CoP}},h)$, assume the remaining contact couple is normal to the plane:
\[
\bm M_O=\bm r\times\bm F+(0,0,T_z)^\top.
\]
Expanding this identity yields
\begin{equation}
  x_{\mathrm{CoP}}=\frac{hF_x-M_y}{F_z},\qquad
  y_{\mathrm{CoP}}=\frac{M_x+hF_y}{F_z},
  \label{eq:ch7:cop}
\end{equation}
and $T_z=M_z-x_{\mathrm{CoP}}F_y+y_{\mathrm{CoP}}F_x$.
For instance, $h=0.05$\,m, $\bm F=(20,-10,500)$\,N and $\bm M_O=(-19.5,-49,1.8)$\,N\,m give CoP $(0.10,-0.04)$\,m and free moment $T_z=2$\,N\,m.
A different plate convention requires a consistent transformation, not a memorized sign change.
In the same frame and about the same origin, negating both force and moment for action/reaction leaves the CoP coordinates unchanged and reverses the free moment.

As $F_z$ approaches zero, these ratios become ill-conditioned; reject unloaded intervals using a threshold justified by noise and the task.
CoP is not center of mass, and a shared plate under both feet does not independently identify the wrench under each foot.
A valid whole-body force record also does not by itself determine individual muscle forces or joint contact loads.

\section{Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs)}

An IMU contains accelerometers and gyroscopes; a magnetometer may be added but is not intrinsic to every unit.
In a local inertial approximation, let $Q$ map sensor-frame vectors to world coordinates, and let $\bm a_s$ be the world acceleration of the sensor location.
A calibrated accelerometer observation has the form
\[
\bm f_m=Q^\top(\bm a_s-\bm g)+\bm b_a+\bm\eta_a,
\]
where $\bm g=(0,0,-g)^\top$ in the upward world frame, with $g>0$ the gravitational acceleration magnitude, $\bm b_a$ is sensor-frame bias and $\bm\eta_a$ is noise.
Thus the observation is specific force, not kinematic acceleration~\cite{AnalogDevicesInertialExperimental}.
Ignoring noise, recovery requires $\bm a_s=Q(\bm f_m-\bm b_a)+\bm g$.
A stationary upward-aligned accelerometer reads approximately $+g$ vertically, whereas ideal free fall has zero specific force.
Orientation error consequently leaks gravity into the recovered movement acceleration.

Gyroscope bias accumulates in integrated orientation; magnetometer heading can be disturbed by the local magnetic environment.
An extended Kalman or complementary filter estimates state only under its observation and disturbance assumptions.
Treating accelerometer direction as gravity requires negligible or separately modeled translational acceleration, a condition that can fail in rapid swings.
If the required acceleration is at a different rigid-body point, angular acceleration and centripetal lever-arm terms must also be included.
Double integration needs initial conditions and drift control; the sensor equations alone do not make absolute position observable.

\section{Data Synchronization}

A common trigger identifies an event but does not necessarily establish a shared sampling clock or remove transport and processing latency.
Estimate clock offset and drift, preserve timestamps, and verify synchronization using a physical event observable in the relevant channels.
Resampling into a common time base does not correct an unknown offset.
Report the residual timing uncertainty together with each channel's filtering and event-detection method.

Physiological electromechanical delay is distinct from instrument delay.
An EMG-to-force lag depends on the task and physiological processes and should not be removed as if it were merely a clock error.
Cross-correlation can support alignment when signals share an appropriate physical event, but mechanically maximizing correlation can erase the biological lag being studied or align unrelated features.
There is no universal EMG-to-motion shift for every muscle and movement.

\section{Connecting Measurements to Swing Inference}

In a swing analysis, marker geometry establishes segment frames; their differentiated motion contributes inertial terms; force-plate wrenches supply external loading; and EMG or IMUs contribute additional, differently conditioned observations.
Timing and filtering determine whether those terms describe the same event and bandwidth.
A mismatch can resemble a change in sequencing, joint moment or apparent energy transfer.
Assess uncertainty through the full inference chain and distinguish observed movement, model-dependent net joint loads and less identifiable internal muscle or tissue forces.

\section*{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Filter Selection.}
  Generate a known trajectory plus declared noise at a specified sample rate.
  Compare several cutoffs below Nyquist using position, velocity and acceleration errors; exclude and report an edge interval.
  Confirm the two-pass amplitude at the declared single-pass cutoff and distinguish synthetic truth from an unobservable experimental trajectory.
  \item \textbf{CoP Computation.}
  Reconstruct the wrench from the numerical contact example above, then recover CoP and free moment.
  Add declared force and moment noise and examine the ratios as normal force decreases.
  Explain why a peak vertical force alone cannot determine a CoP trajectory.
  \item \textbf{EMG Processing.}
  Process a synthetic amplitude-modulated signal through a declared envelope pipeline.
  Vary smoothing and normalization, and assess their effects on amplitude and timing without interpreting the synthetic envelope as physiological force.
  \item \textbf{IMU Interpretation.}
  Simulate stationary, free-fall and accelerating sensor states with known orientation.
  Recover acceleration from specific force, then introduce orientation and bias errors and quantify the resulting drift.
\end{enumerate}
