\chapter[How Biology Differs From Engineering]{How Biology Differs\\From Engineering}
\label{ch:bio-vs-eng}

The golf swing connects neural excitation, muscle and tendon dynamics, joint
motion, ground contact, and club deformation. A model can represent selected
parts of this chain accurately while leaving others unresolved. Reproducing
clubhead motion does not, by itself, establish which muscles generated it;
reproducing net joint moments does not establish local tissue stress. This
chapter develops the assumptions needed for a particular question and the
corresponding tests of those assumptions.

The mathematical tools of the preceding volumes remain useful. Their
application depends on the chosen state, input, constraints, and output.
Biology changes those choices; it does not suspend mechanics. Numerical
examples in this chapter are manufactured checks of equations, not measured
human parameters or recommendations for a golfer.

\section{The Rigid-Body Assumption and Its Limits}

A rigid body has a fixed mass distribution in its own frame. A revolute joint
permits one relative rotation and constrains the other five relative motions.
Both are idealizations useful in biological and engineered systems. Bones
deform, but their deformation need not be resolved for every movement question.
Engineered shafts, transmissions, and sensors can also have compliance,
backlash, noise, delay, and temperature dependence. The relevant distinction
is between a declared approximation and the behavior it must explain.

For a golf model, declare the segment inventory, coordinate frames, joint
constraints, and external contacts before selecting actuators. A shoulder
rotation model may suffice for an endpoint-kinematics question while being
inadequate for joint-contact loading. A hand--club constraint can transmit
forces and moments even when its constrained coordinates are not actuated.
Calling an anatomical joint ``six degree of freedom'' also requires care:
relative pose has six components, but constraints and tissue mechanics may
couple or strongly resist some motions. Coordinate count is not a count of
independently controllable directions.

\begin{comparison}{Modeling Assumptions to Test}{rigid-vs-bio}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.6em,style=nextline]
 \item[Structure] Choose rigid segments, compliant modes, or a continuum
 description according to the deformation that affects the target output.
 \item[Actuation] Specify actuator states and force, velocity, power, delay,
 and saturation limits where they matter.
 \item[Sensing] Identify observed quantities, error, sampling, filtering,
 and latency. A reconstructed state is not itself a sensor reading.
 \item[Adaptation] State the time horizon over which parameters are fixed.
 Fatigue, learning, and material changes require additional hypotheses.
 \item[Redundancy] Establish the rank of the relevant map and its feasible
 domain before claiming alternative solutions or compensation.
\end{description}
\end{comparison}

Motor torque can depend on speed, current dynamics, and thermal limits;
muscle force can depend on activation, fibre length, velocity, and history.
An ideal torque source deliberately suppresses these dependencies. It can
be a useful first model of either actuator, provided conclusions remain about
net loads and motion. A richer biological model should earn its added
complexity through improved, independently tested predictions.

\section{Compliance, Timescales, and the Chosen Output}
\subsection{Adding Deformation Requires Adding Dynamics}

A free rigid club has six pose coordinates locally, not one grip angle.
A one-angle club is a constrained planar abstraction. Flexible modes add
coordinates to the selected rigid model. Write $\bm z=(\bm q,\bm\eta)$,
where $\bm q\in\mathbb R^n$ describes the rigid coordinates and
$\bm\eta\in\mathbb R^r$ the retained deformation amplitudes.
The selector $E=[0_{r\times n}\ I_r]$ gives $E\bm z=\bm\eta$.
In a local chart with $\dot{\bm z}$ as
its generalized velocity, an illustrative model is
\begin{equation}
 M(\bm z)\ddot{\bm z}+h(\bm z,\dot{\bm z})
 +E^T(K\bm\eta+D\dot{\bm\eta})
 =B(\bm z)\bm\tau+J_c(\bm z)^T\bm\lambda .
 \label{eq:ch1:flexible-eom}
\end{equation}
All terms are generalized forces in the same augmented coordinate space.
$M$ includes rigid--flexible inertial coupling; $h$ contains remaining
inertial and gravitational terms. Constant $K,D$ describe a local linear
elastic and viscous approximation, not arbitrary tissue behavior.
$B$ maps actuator loads $\bm\tau$ into the augmented coordinates, while
$J_c$ is the constraint Jacobian and $\bm\lambda$ its conjugate reaction
loads. Contact equations complete the model. Adding an unprojected modal spring force
to a rigid-body equation would omit both dimensions and coupling.

The shaft's relevance depends on the output: small deformation can matter
for clubface orientation while contributing little to whole-body motion.
Ground contact and grip loads determine modal excitation; controller
bandwidth alone does not describe that loading spectrum.

\subsection{A Frequency Comparison With a Checkable Meaning}

For a linear mode, let
\[
 m\ddot\eta+d\dot\eta+k\eta=F(t),\qquad
 \omega_n=\sqrt{k/m},\qquad\zeta=\frac{d}{2\sqrt{km}}.
\]
For a harmonic load of angular frequency $\Omega$, steady-state displacement
per unit force is
\[
 H(\Omega)=\frac{1}{k-m\Omega^2+\mathrm i d\Omega},\qquad
 kH(\Omega)=\frac{1}{1-(\Omega/\omega_n)^2+2\mathrm i\zeta\Omega/\omega_n}.
\]
At low frequency, $H\simeq1/k$: quasi-static compliance can still produce
important deflection. Near resonance, damping and coupling matter. Far above
the mode's frequency, inertia dominates the forced response; that does not
establish rigidity during a transient with initial deformation and broadband
loads.

\begin{principle}{The Timescale Separation Principle}{timescale}
Compare the relevant load spectrum and initial transients with modal
frequencies and damping, then propagate omitted deformation to the output.
Quasi-static reduction requires negligible dynamic error over that spectrum;
a rigid approximation additionally requires negligible deformation error for
the task. Neither follows from controller bandwidth alone.
\end{principle}

For a manufactured translational mode with $m=1$~kg, $k=100$~N/m, and
$d=1$~N\,s/m, $\omega_n=10$~rad/s and $\zeta=0.05$.
At $\Omega=1,10,100$~rad/s, the amplitude relative to static compliance,
$|kH|$, is approximately $1.010$, $10$, and $0.0101$. The high-frequency
response is nearly opposite in phase to the force. A constant static gain
cannot reproduce all three regimes. These are illustrative parameters,
not measurements of a golf shaft.

\section{From Excitation to Joint Power}
\subsection{Force Definitions and Units}

A common Hill-type idealization separates activation, normalized active
force--length and force--velocity curves, and parallel passive force
\cite{millard2013flexing}. With positive fibre velocity denoting lengthening,
write the illustrative undamped fibre force as
\begin{equation}
 F_M=F_0\left[a f_L(\ell_M/\ell_0)f_V(\dot\ell_M/v_0)
                         +f_{PE}(\ell_M/\ell_0)\right].
 \label{eq:ch1:hill-overview}
\end{equation}
$\ell_M$ is fibre length, $\ell_0$ its optimal reference length, and
$a\in[0,1]$ the activation state. $F_0$ is the maximum active isometric
fibre-force scale at optimal length; the curve values are dimensionless.
$v_0>0$ is a declared velocity scale, not a universal human shortening speed.
The parallel-elastic term $f_{PE}$ is scaled by the same outside factor $F_0$.
Other models
add damping or history dependence and use different normalizations.

Fibre force is not automatically tendon force. In a massless, ideal pennated
equilibrium model, $F_T=F_M\cos\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the pennation angle
between fibres and tendon. This relation requires a tendon constitutive
law and compatible fibre--tendon geometry. A compliant tendon can change
length while the whole musculotendon path length changes little. Joint
angular velocity therefore does not by itself determine fibre shortening
velocity. Model-specific equilibrium, slack, and regularization conditions
must be checked before solving for fibre velocity.

\subsection{Moment Arms Must Preserve Power}

For differentiable, time-independent musculotendon routing
$\bm\ell_{MT}(\bm q)$, define
\begin{equation}
 R_{ji}=-\frac{\partial\ell_{MT,i}}{\partial q_j},\qquad
 \bm\tau_M=R(\bm q)\bm F_T .
 \label{eq:ch1:muscle-torque}
\end{equation}
For rotational coordinates, $R_{ji}$ has length units and the conjugate
load is a moment. Translational coordinates instead have force as the
conjugate load. Virtual work gives the sign-sensitive check
\[
 \dot{\bm\ell}_{MT}=-R^T\dot{\bm q},\qquad
 \bm\tau_M^T\dot{\bm q}=-\bm F_T^T\dot{\bm\ell}_{MT}.
\]
A positive tensile force delivers positive skeletal power when its path
shortens. Fibre power can differ from path power because tendon energy storage
and other internal processes intervene. For constrained coordinates or speeds
distinct from $\dot{\bm q}$, apply the velocity map before comparing power.

For a numerical check, take rotational coordinates and
\[
 R=\begin{bmatrix}0.03&-0.03&0\\0&0.02&-0.02\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm m,
 \quad\bm F_T=\begin{bmatrix}100\\80\\60\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm N,
 \quad\dot{\bm q}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}.
\]
Then $\bm\tau_M=(0.6,0.4)^T$~N\,m and
$\dot{\bm\ell}_{MT}=(-0.06,0.08,-0.02)^T$~m/s. Both expressions for
skeletal power give $0.8$~W. A moment-arm sign error can look plausible in
a torque plot while failing energy accounting immediately.

\begin{principle}{Force-Velocity Asymmetry in Multi-Joint Tasks}{fv-asymmetry-task}
Force capacity depends on each muscle's fibre state, activation, parameters,
and constitutive curves. A biarticular muscle couples multiple joint motions
through its routing and tendon dynamics. Evaluate those states before
classifying its contraction or constructing the feasible joint-load set;
joint rotation alone does not specify eccentric or concentric fibre contraction.
\end{principle}

In a downswing, the same visible wrist motion can be compatible with different
tendon strains, activation histories, and antagonist forces. Muscle modeling
makes those alternatives calculable under assumptions; it does not make video
sufficient to identify which alternative occurred.

\section{Two Different Kinds of Redundancy}

Let the task be $\bm y=\phi(\bm q)$, with
Jacobian $J_y=\partial\phi/\partial\bm q$. For $n$
independent local configuration coordinates, infinitesimal motions preserving
$\bm y$ satisfy $J_y\delta\bm q=0$, with dimension
$n-\operatorname{rank}J_y$. A three-joint planar arm controlling a
2D endpoint position has a one-dimensional local nullspace only where the
Jacobian has rank two. At singular configurations the rank changes. A finite
displacement requires following the nonlinear level set, not extrapolating
one tangent direction indefinitely.

Muscle-force redundancy concerns a different map. At fixed geometry, an
instantaneous force-allocation model solves
$R\bm F_T=\bm\tau_{\mathrm{req}}$, with a linear equality solution space
of dimension $p-\operatorname{rank}R$ when nonempty, for $p$ muscle paths.
Nonnegative tensile forces and state-dependent capacities restrict this space.
In the dimensionless algebraic example
\[
 R=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
 \bm\tau_{\mathrm{req}}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\3\end{bmatrix},
\]
all equality solutions are $(3-t,3-t,t)^T$. Bounds $0\leq F_i\leq3$
leave $0\leq t\leq3$. Other loads or capacities can leave a point or no
solution. This is an allocation example, not a human moment-arm model. It
shows why a nullspace count cannot guarantee compensation for a fatigued muscle.
These candidate forces must still satisfy muscle dynamics: if every internal
state is already fixed, a constitutive model may fix tendon force as well.
An allocation equality alone does not supply a realizable excitation history.

Even equal nullity does not identify equal physical freedom. The task map
$J_y=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$
has null vector $(1,-1,1)^T$, whereas the example $R$ has null vector
$(-1,-1,1)^T$. Their domains also represent different quantities:
configuration changes and muscle forces. Matching hand position does not
guarantee matching velocity, joint loading, energy use, or future motion.

The uncontrolled manifold hypothesis tests whether variability is preferentially
organized along directions preserving a selected task variable
\cite{Scholz1999}. Its original sit-to-stand analysis supports a
task-dependent interpretation of joint variability. It is not a direct
measurement of a neural projection operator or proof that all task-null
directions are left uncontrolled. Coordinate scaling, task choice, noise,
and dynamics influence interpretation. In golf, variability preserving
clubhead position at one instant can still change face orientation, impact
timing, or the subsequent response to a perturbation.

\section{Choosing the Model by the Question}
\subsection{Rigid Segments With Generalized-Load Actuators}

This model examines motion, power, momentum, and net loads without resolving
individual muscles. Inverse dynamics requires kinematics, inertial parameters,
external loads, and constraints; it does not identify individual muscle forces
from motion alone. Add actuator bounds when testing trajectory feasibility.
An unconstrained optimal torque history is a mathematical result, not evidence
that a golfer can execute it.

For clubhead speed, this level can clarify how mass distribution, segment
motion, and external forces interact. A sensitivity calculation can ask
whether plausible parameter changes or shaft deformation alter the conclusion.
If they do, examine the omitted physics before interpreting the result as a
mechanism of a human swing.

\subsection{Rigid Segments With Muscle and Tendon States}

This adds routing, activation, and constitutive assumptions. A forward
simulation requires excitations and initial states. Estimating excitation
from observed motion requires further data, a control hypothesis, or an
optimization criterion. Multiple solutions may fit the same motion. Report
residual forces, reserve actuators, parameter sensitivity, and objective
sensitivity rather than presenting one optimum as an observed neural strategy.

OpenSim supports musculoskeletal modeling and simulation; a typical model
combines rigid bodies, joints, and force elements \cite{Delp2007}. Such a
model is not automatically a finite-element model of deforming bone or soft
tissue. Joint reactions are resultant loads conditional on modeled actuators
and constraints, not local stress fields.

\subsection{Deformation or Tissue Stress as an Output}

Flexible modes, continuum models, or coupled multibody--finite-element models
resolve deformation when the question requires it. Local stress requires
geometry, constitutive properties, boundary conditions, and loading; numerical
resolution and constitutive validation are separate checks. A resultant force
and moment constrain integrals of traction without uniquely determining its
spatial distribution. A calculated stress field is not an injury probability
without a validated injury or failure model.

These choices are not a universal fidelity ranking. Extra parameters can
increase uncertainty when measurements cannot identify them. Real-time
feasibility depends on model, algorithm, hardware, accuracy, and update rate;
a fixed degree-of-freedom threshold cannot select the method. State an output
tolerance, compare simpler and richer models against independent observations,
and retain the least complexity that supports the claim.

\section{Mass Properties Need Accounting and Frames}
\subsection{A Complete Segment Inventory}

Anthropometric coefficients require a population, segmentation scheme,
landmarks, and parameter conventions. De~Leva's study adjusts landmark
references of Zatsiorsky--Seluyanov segment parameters \cite{DeLeva1996};
it is not a generic update of Dempster cadaver coefficients. Mixing
incompatible segmentations can double-count or omit mass. Scaling population
estimates also does not make them subject-specific measurements.

An inconsistent inventory illustrates the danger: central fractions $0.081$
and $0.497$ combined with six
unilateral fractions $0.0271$, $0.0162$, $0.0061$, $0.1416$, $0.0433$, and
$0.0137$. Their one-row sum is $0.826$; counting both sides gives $1.074$.
Neither accounts for one body. Renormalizing conceals the provenance problem.
No empirical replacement table is asserted here without verification of its
source, landmarks, and segment definitions.

For a synthetic accounting exercise, use central fractions $0.10,0.40$ and
six paired fractions $(0.05,0.03,0.02,0.09,0.04,0.02)$ per side. Their
bilateral sum is one. For $75$~kg, the central masses are $7.5,30$~kg and
each side has $(3.75,2.25,1.50,6.75,3.00,1.50)$~kg. This tests bookkeeping
only; it provides neither human lengths nor centres of mass or inertias.

\subsection{Centre of Mass and Spatial Inertia}

Let a segment's local centre of mass be $\bm c_i$, its local-to-world rotation
matrix $Q_i\in SO(3)$, and its world origin $\bm p_i$. Then
\[
 \bm r_i=\bm p_i+Q_i\bm c_i,\qquad
 \bm r_{COM}=\frac{\sum_i m_i\bm r_i}{\sum_i m_i}.
\]
Body mass and height alone cannot specify these world positions. A pose and
kinematic model are needed, with separate left and right instances. For
masses $2,1$~kg at $(0.2,0,0),(0.4,0.15,0)$~m, respectively, the centre
is $(4/15,0.05,0)$~m. Translating both positions translates the centre by
the same amount, a useful frame-consistency check.

A scalar $I=m(\rho L)^2$ describes inertia about a specified axis when
$\rho L$ is its radius of gyration. General 3D dynamics needs a symmetric
tensor $I_C$ about the centre of mass in declared axes. For an angular-first
twist $V=(\bm\omega,\bm v_O)$, all expressed in one frame, let $\bm c$
point from origin $O$ to the centre and $C=[\bm c]_\times$. Then
\[
 \mathcal I_O=\begin{bmatrix}I_C-mCC&mC\\-mC&mI_3\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The signs follow from
$\bm v_C=\bm v_O+\bm\omega\times\bm c=\bm v_O-C\bm\omega$, giving
\[
 \tfrac12 V^T\mathcal I_OV
 =\tfrac12\bm\omega^TI_C\bm\omega
  +\tfrac12m\|\bm v_O+\bm\omega\times\bm c\|^2.
\]
Positive mass and positive-definite $I_C$ make this form positive definite.
Physical inertia tensors also satisfy principal moment triangle inequalities;
positive eigenvalues alone are not a complete realizability test. Point or
ideal line masses can have singular rotational inertia. Declare which case
an implementation accepts.

\section{When the Control-Affine Framework Applies}
\subsection{Choose the Input Before Splitting Drift and Control}

Torque-driven and excitation-driven models describe different interventions.
For a smooth fixed-contact model in independent coordinates, let $\bm s$
collect muscle states, with composite state $\state=(\bm q,\bm v,\bm s)$.
Assume $M$ is invertible on the domain under analysis. A possible description is
\[
 \dot{\bm q}=N(\bm q)\bm v,\qquad
 M(\bm q)\dot{\bm v}=\bm f_{\mathrm{mech}}(\bm q,\bm v,\bm s),
 \qquad\dot{\bm s}=h_s(\bm q,\bm v,\bm s,\bm u).
\]
$N$ accounts for representations whose coordinate derivatives differ from
generalized speeds. Reactions must be solved consistently or eliminated on
the constraint manifold. Here excitation acts through muscle states, with
no separate direct excitation term in mechanics. Input affinity depends on
$h_s$ and any other input-dependent equations.

\begin{principle}{The Extended Affine Structure}{affine-bio}
A declared model is control-affine in its chosen input if
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\state}=f(\state)+G(\state)\bm u.
 \label{eq:ch1:affine-bio}
\end{equation}
Nonlinear state dependence in $f,G$ is permitted. For example,
$\dot a=(u-a)/T$ with constant $T>0$ is affine in excitation. If remaining
muscle-state equations depend on activation and mechanics but not directly
on excitation, their nonlinearity does not invalidate this structure.
Establish these assumptions for the selected model; do not infer them from
the word ``musculoskeletal.''
\end{principle}

Consider instead the branch-dependent law documented for OpenSim's
\href{https://opensim-org.github.io/opensim-moco-site/docs/1.3.0/html_user/classOpenSim_1_1MuscleFirstOrderActivationDynamicModel.html}{first-order activation model}:
\[
 \dot a=\frac{u-a}{T(u,a)},\qquad
 T(u,a)=\begin{cases}
 T_{act}(0.5+1.5a),&u>a,\\
 T_{deact}/(0.5+1.5a),&u\leq a.
 \end{cases}
\]
For a manufactured evaluation without an activation floor, choose $a=0.5$,
$T_{act}=0.01$~s, and $T_{deact}=0.04$~s. The rates at $u=0,0.5,1$ are
$-15.625,0,40$~s$^{-1}$. An affine input map would give the midpoint rate
$(-15.625+40)/2=12.1875$~s$^{-1}$, not zero. Each branch is affine in
$u$ for fixed state, but the full map is not. The rate is continuous at $u=a$,
with generally unequal one-sided slopes. Activation floors and clamping in
an implementation need their own treatment; this arithmetic does not
reproduce every software variant.

\begin{remark}{Dimensional Expansion of the State Space}{expanded_manifold}
For $n$ independent coordinates, $p$ activations, and $p$ independent fibre
length states, a local state dimension is $2n+2p$. A rigid-tendon model may
omit fibre-length states; fatigue or history states increase the count.
Algebraic constraints and redundant pose representations change the distinction
between stored variables and independent states. Derive dimension from the
equations rather than a universal number of states per muscle.
\end{remark}

Lie-bracket calculations require appropriate smooth vector fields and a
specified admissible input set. Contraction arguments also require a suitable
metric, invariant domain, and the theorem's differential conditions. Input
bounds, contact transitions, and nonsmooth force laws can limit conclusions.
Smooth analysis within a contact mode is not a proof across impacts or
changes in contact. Dimensionality is a computational challenge; validity of
the mathematical hypotheses comes first.

Zero excitation does not instantly erase activation or tendon strain. With
constant $T$, activation subsequently decays as $a(t)=a(0)e^{-t/T}$ while
other states evolve. Zero-input drift can thus include force associated with
prior excitation. It is not synonymous with anatomically passive force,
zero joint torque, or zero power. A golf counterfactual must specify what is
removed and which initial states, contacts, and feedback rules are retained.

\section{Connecting the Parts of the Swing Argument}

The choices form a chain of inference. Kinematics and external loads, with
mass properties and contacts, constrain net dynamics. Routing and constitutive
assumptions map candidate muscle states into those loads. Activation dynamics
connect states to candidate excitations. Compliance changes both transmission
and the output being controlled. Redundancy leaves alternatives that an
objective or further measurement may restrict. Every added explanation
introduces a corresponding test.

A claim that a golfer increases clubhead speed through a particular muscle
action requires more than a faster clubhead trajectory. Test whether the
conclusion persists across plausible inertial parameters, grip constraints,
tendon properties, allocation objectives, and shaft models. Separate an
instantaneous power identity from a finite-time causal intervention:
removing one torque or excitation changes later states and may change loads
supplied by feedback. Report the intervention and its uncertainty alongside
the predicted effect.

The resulting explanation should distinguish measured quantities, mechanical
calculations, biologically conditioned inferences, and hypotheses about
control or coaching. That distinction lets a simple model provide strong
insight while identifying what remains unresolved about the swing.

\section*{Exercises With Verification Targets}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{Mass Accounting.} Reproduce both erroneous inventory sums and
 the synthetic $75$-kg inventory. Explain why a unit sum cannot validate
 anatomical provenance or supply missing centre-of-mass locations.
 \item \textbf{Compliance.} Reproduce the three values of $|kH|$ above.
 A hypothetical $5$-Hz mode completes $5(0.25)=1.25$ cycles in $0.25$~s.
 Explain why this count supplies neither excitation amplitude nor output
 error and cannot decide whether shaft deformation is negligible.
 \item \textbf{Power.} Verify the $0.8$-W example by both routes. Identify
 the energy-storage terms needed to equate skeletal and contractile-element
 power for a compliant tendon.
 \item \textbf{Redundancy.} Derive both null vectors and the interval
 $0\leq t\leq3$. For three joints and six muscle paths, state the ranks
 needed for task nullity one and force nullity three. Explain why neither
 number counts identifiable neural excitation histories.
 \item \textbf{Mass Frames.} Reproduce the two-mass centre of mass and its
 translation covariance. With upward $y$ and gravity magnitude $g$, compute
 $U=\sum_i m_i g y_i=0.15g$. State the reference level and why mass
 fractions alone cannot determine potential energy.
 \item \textbf{Spatial Inertia.} Use $m=2$~kg and
 \[
 \bm c=(0.1,-0.2,0.3)\ \mathrm m,\qquad
 I_C=\operatorname{diag}(0.02,0.03,0.04)\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.
 \]
 Check symmetry,
 positivity, principal moment inequalities, and both energy expressions for
 $\bm\omega=(1.5,-2,0.5)$~s$^{-1}$ and $\bm v_O=(-1.2,0.8,-0.4)$~m/s.
 Nonzero translation tests the cross-block signs.
 \item \textbf{Input Affinity.} Compare the branch-dependent example with
 the constant-$T$ model, $T=0.02$~s, giving rates $(-25,0,25)$~s$^{-1}$
 at the same inputs and activation. Explain
 why nonlinear activation dependence alone does not disprove input affinity,
 and why zero excitation is not an instantaneous relaxed state.
 \item \textbf{Model Selection.} Specify separate evidence requirements for
 clubface pose, net wrist moment, individual tendon force, and local stress.
 For a force--velocity calculation, identify the missing curve, normalization,
 fibre state, activation, and tendon assumptions before assigning an
 eccentric-to-isometric force ratio.
\end{enumerate}
