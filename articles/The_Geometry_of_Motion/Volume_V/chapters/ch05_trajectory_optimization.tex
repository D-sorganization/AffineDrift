\chapter[Trajectory Optimization in Practice]{Trajectory Optimization\\in Practice}
\label{ch:traj-opt}

A simulated trajectory answers a conditional question about a model and input.
Optimization adds a declared objective and feasible set; it does not infer the
objective of a human golfer from the resulting motion. This chapter derives and
tests the quadratic minimization at the heart of iLQR, then specifies the checks
needed to turn those local steps into a nonlinear trajectory optimizer.

\section{The Discrete Problem and Its Approximation}

For a chosen discrete dynamics map $F_k$, solve
\[
 \min_{u_0,\ldots,u_{N-1}}\quad
 J=\phi(x_N)+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\ell_k(x_k,u_k),\qquad
 x_{k+1}=F_k(x_k,u_k),\quad x_0=x_{\mathrm{initial}}.
\]
State/input bounds and terminal constraints are additional parts of the problem.
If $\ell$ represents a continuous running cost, its discretization must include
the time interval or a stated quadrature rule. Derivatives must describe the
same discrete map used for rollout, including its integration and input-hold
conventions. Differentiating a continuous vector field is not automatically
differentiating its numerical flow map.

iLQR uses first derivatives $A=F_x$, $B=F_u$ and quadratic cost/value models.
Full differential dynamic programming (DDP) also retains contractions of the
next value gradient with second derivatives of $F$. Both are local methods;
neither name certifies a global optimum~\cite{Mayne1966,jacobson1970dpp,TedrakeTrajectoryFoundations}.

At a dynamically feasible nominal trajectory, let $V_x^+,V_{xx}^+$ describe
the next value approximation. The iLQR coefficients are
\begin{align*}
 Q_x&=\ell_x+A^TV_x^+,& Q_u&=\ell_u+B^TV_x^+,\\
 Q_{xx}&=\ell_{xx}+A^TV_{xx}^+A,&
 Q_{uu}&=\ell_{uu}+B^TV_{xx}^+B,\\
 Q_{ux}&=\ell_{ux}+B^TV_{xx}^+A.&
\end{align*}
For example, DDP adds $\sum_i(V_x^+)_i\nabla_{uu}^2(F_k)_i$
to $Q_{uu}$, and analogous terms to the other Hessian blocks.

\section{Deriving the Backward Step}

With $z=(\delta x,\delta u)$, expand
\[
 Q(z)=Q_0+Q_x^T\delta x+Q_u^T\delta u+
 \tfrac12\delta x^TQ_{xx}\delta x+
 \delta u^TQ_{ux}\delta x+\tfrac12\delta u^TQ_{uu}\delta u.
\]
If $Q_{uu}\succ0$, stationarity gives
\[
 \delta u=k+K\delta x,\qquad
 k=-Q_{uu}^{-1}Q_u,\quad K=-Q_{uu}^{-1}Q_{ux}.
\]
Substitution, including the cross terms, yields the Schur complement
\begin{align*}
 V_0-Q_0&=\tfrac12Q_u^Tk,\\
 V_x&=Q_x+Q_{xu}k,\\
 V_{xx}&=Q_{xx}+Q_{xu}K
          =Q_{xx}-Q_{xu}Q_{uu}^{-1}Q_{ux}.
\end{align*}
Minimization subtracts a positive-semidefinite term from the state curvature.
The minus sign therefore matters. The entire Hessian of
$Q$ need not be positive definite for this conditional control minimization.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Exact Minimization of a Local Control Quadratic}]
import numpy as np


def quadratic_step(gradient: np.ndarray, hessian: np.ndarray,
                   state_size: int) -> dict:
    """Minimize in control variables ordered after state variables."""
    gradient = np.asarray(gradient, dtype=float)
    hessian = np.asarray(hessian, dtype=float)
    if (gradient.ndim != 1 or gradient.size < 2
            or not np.all(np.isfinite(gradient))):
        raise ValueError("Gradient must be a finite vector")
    if (isinstance(state_size, bool)
            or not isinstance(state_size, (int, np.integer))
            or not 1 <= state_size < gradient.size):
        raise ValueError("State and control blocks must be nonempty")
    if (hessian.shape != (gradient.size, gradient.size)
            or not np.all(np.isfinite(hessian))
            or not np.allclose(hessian, hessian.T,
                               rtol=1e-12, atol=1e-12)):
        raise ValueError("Hessian must be finite and symmetric")
    hessian = (hessian + hessian.T) / 2
    state = slice(0, state_size)
    control = slice(state_size, None)
    huu = hessian[control, control]
    hux = hessian[control, state]
    try:
        np.linalg.cholesky(huu)
    except np.linalg.LinAlgError as error:
        raise ValueError("Control Hessian must be positive definite") from error
    step = -np.linalg.solve(huu, gradient[control])
    gain = -np.linalg.solve(huu, hux)
    value_hessian = hessian[state, state] + hux.T @ gain
    return {
        "k": step, "K": gain,
        "value_gradient": gradient[state] + hux.T @ step,
        "value_hessian": (value_hessian + value_hessian.T) / 2,
        "value_offset": float(0.5 * gradient[control] @ step),
    }
\end{lstlisting}

This executable core is not a complete iLQR solver. It has no nonlinear rollout,
constraint handling or convergence claim. For the scalar-state/scalar-control
case $\nabla Q=(3,4)$ and $\nabla^2Q=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$,
it returns $k=-2$, $K=-1/2$, $V_x=1$, $V_{xx}=3/2$ and $V_0-Q_0=-4$.
The minimized polynomial is therefore $-4+\delta x+3\delta x^2/4$.

If $Q_{uu}$ is indefinite, adding a fixed small number to its diagonal does not
guarantee positive definiteness. A solver must adjust regularization and check
factorization. When gains minimize a modified quadratic, the simplified Schur
formulas above describe that modified quadratic. To evaluate an original
quadratic under arbitrary gains, retain all terms:
\begin{align*}
 V_x&=Q_x+Q_{xu}k+K^TQ_u+K^TQ_{uu}k,\\
 V_{xx}&=Q_{xx}+Q_{xu}K+K^TQ_{ux}+K^TQ_{uu}K.
\end{align*}
Mixing regularized gains with unjustified cancellations changes the backward
model and its predicted improvement.

\section{A Real Acceptance Loop}

Starting from the fixed initial state, roll out each candidate using
\[
 u_k^{\mathrm{trial}}=\bar u_k+\alpha k_k+
 K_k(x_k^{\mathrm{trial}}-\bar x_k),\qquad
 x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{trial}}=F_k(x_k^{\mathrm{trial}},u_k^{\mathrm{trial}}).
\]
Try decreasing positive step lengths $\alpha$. Evaluate the actual nonlinear
objective and feasibility for each candidate. Accept a finite, feasible candidate
only if it satisfies the chosen sufficient-decrease rule; otherwise retain the
nominal trajectory and adjust regularization or report failure. Assigning
$\alpha=1$ once is not a line search. Clipping a control after the backward pass
also changes the subproblem; bounded-control methods must account for active
constraints.

Record the accepted trajectory's cost, including the final returned trajectory.
Small cost change alone may mean a stalled search, numerical noise or a rejected
step. Report iteration limits and termination reason, feasibility residuals,
gradient or appropriate constrained stationarity residuals, and discretization
sensitivity. Initialization and local minima matter; a successful rollout is
not an optimality certificate.

\section{Pendulum and Golf Objectives}

For a pendulum angle measured from downward vertical, swing-up targets
$q_N=\pi$ modulo $2\pi$ and $\dot q_N=0$. A smooth periodic terminal penalty
$w_q(1+\cos q_N)+w_v\dot q_N^2/2$ respects angle wrapping but does not impose an
exact terminal equality. At the exactly downward, motionless, zero-input
initialization, this penalty's gradient can vanish; a local method may need a
nontrivial initial guess. A finite torque bound and specified duration complete
the physical question.

The running penalty $\int\tau^TR\tau\,\mathrm dt$ is a weighted squared-torque
effort measure. It is not mechanical work $\int\tau^T\dot q\,\mathrm dt$, positive
work, electrical consumption or metabolic expenditure. Mechanical net work can
cancel between positive and negative phases; different objectives can therefore
favor different motions even with identical dynamics.

For golf, terminal clubhead speed must be paired with a strike location, clubface
orientation, approach direction, admissible joint motion and actuator limits.
Specify whether the terminal time is fixed or is a state-dependent impact event.
Pre-impact speed does not by itself determine ball launch, which also depends on
contact geometry and the impact model. Muscle excitation requires activation
states and force limits; replacing excitation with unconstrained joint torque
changes the feasible set. A ground reaction or second-hand constraint also
changes the dynamics differentiated by the optimizer.

The coupling is consequential: an inertia or frame error alters both forward
predictions and their derivatives, so optimization can exploit the error. The
resulting trajectory is a hypothesis to test with the simulation and feedback
checks in the adjacent chapters, not evidence that a golfer uses this objective.
