\label{ch:configuration}

\epigraph{%
  To command a body to move, we must first establish exactly where it is. We do not use the raw atoms of the universe to map a body; we use its independent coordinates—its configuration.
}{}

\begin{laymansbox}{Context \& Use Case: Mapping the Possible}{context_box}
\textbf{The Math You Will See:} We define the \textbf{Configuration Space ($\mathcal{Q}$)}, count Degrees of Freedom (DOF), and define \textbf{Forward Kinematics} functions mapping joint angles ($\mathbf{q}$) to Cartesian workspace coordinates ($\mathbf{p} \in \Reals^3$).
\textbf{Why We Need It:} If you want a robot arm to pick up a coffee cup, you know where the cup is in physical 3D space ($X, Y, Z$). But you cannot command the robot's $X, Y, Z$ position directly! You can only command the motors at its joints (its "configuration"). We must build mathematical maps that translate between the physical space where the coffee cup lives, and the abstract angular "configuration space" where the robot's motors live.
\textbf{All Variables Defined:} $\mathbf{q}$ represents joint angles, $\mathcal{Q}$ is the configuration manifold, and $\mathbf{p}_{end}$ is the end-effector position.
\end{laymansbox}

\section{Degrees of Freedom (DOF)}

In Chapter 2, we learned that the State Space $\mathcal{X}$ consists of both the position coordinates ($\mathbf{q}$) and the velocity coordinates ($\dot{\mathbf{q}}$). But how many position coordinates does our system actually have? And why?

Consider a single speck of dust floating completely unconstrained in a room. To perfectly define where it is, you need 3 coordinates: $x, y, z$. If it is instead a rigid block of steel floating in the room, knowing $x, y, z$ only fixes its center of mass. It could be pitched entirely backward, or rolled on its side. It therefore requires three additional \textbf{angles} (roll, pitch, yaw) defining its rotation. 

This unconstrained rigid block has exactly 6 \textbf{Degrees of Freedom (DOF)}. It requires a minimum of 6 independent parameters (or coordinates) to fully define its posture in 3D Euclidean space.

\begin{definition}{Degrees of Freedom (DOF)}{dof}
  The Degrees of Freedom of a system represents the absolute minimum number of independent scalar coordinates required to perfectly and unambiguously define the position and orientation of every single particle in that system.
\end{definition}

If you connect two floating, rigid steel blocks together with a perfectly rigid bar (welded so they cannot move relative to each other), you do not have 12 DOF. Since the distance and orientation between them are perfectly locked by the weld, you still only have 6 DOF for the combined assembly. The weld acts as a geometric \emph{constraint}. Every independent constraint you add mathematically subtracts a DOF. A hinge joint locking two 6-DOF bodies together removes 5 DOF, leaving exactly 1 DOF of relative rotation between them.

\section{The Configuration Space $\mathcal{Q}$}

The collection of all valid configurations a system can possibly achieve—accounting for all of its physical constraints, linkages, pins, and bearings—is called its \textbf{Configuration Space}, mathematically denoted by the symbol $\mathcal{Q}$.

Like state space, configuration space is an abstract, multi-dimensional geometric surface. It is, strictly speaking, a mathematical \textbf{manifold} (which we explore deeply in Volume I). The number of dimensions of this space is exactly equal to the system's Degrees of Freedom. 

\begin{laymansbox}{Configuration Space vs. State Space\index{State Space}}{config_state}
  A system with $n$ Degrees of Freedom has a Configuration Space $\mathcal{Q}$ of dimension $n$, defined completely by the vector $\mathbf{q} = [q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n]^T$. 

  The corresponding \textbf{State Space} $\mathcal{X}$ (from Chapter 2) is the configuration space multiplied by the velocity of those coordinates, meaning state space always has $2n$ dimensions.
  
  $$ \text{State} \ \mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{q}^T, \dot{\mathbf{q}}^T]^T \in \mathcal{X} = T\mathcal{Q} $$
  (Where $T\mathcal{Q}$ represents the Tangent Bundle of the configuration manifold).
\end{laymansbox}

\section{Differential Geometry Primer: Manifolds and Tangent Spaces}

If the Configuration Space $\mathcal{Q}$ is not a flat plane, what is it? Mathematically, it is a \textbf{Differentiable Manifold}. Differential geometry provides the rigorous tools required to do calculus on these curved surfaces.

\begin{definition}{Differentiable Manifold}{manifold}
A \emph{manifold} is a topological space that locally resembles flat Euclidean space $\Reals^n$ near every point. If you zoom in closely enough on any point of a manifold, the neighborhood looks completely flat, allowing you to establish a local coordinate grid (a \emph{chart}). A collection of these overlapping charts that covers the entire manifold is called an \emph{atlas}.
\end{definition}

This is analogous to the Earth: globally it is a sphere (a 2-dimensional manifold, $S^2$), but locally, a street map of your neighborhood looks perfectly flat. 

\subsection{The Tangent Space $T_q\mathcal{Q}$}

While the manifold $\mathcal{Q}$ represents all valid positions (configurations), motion requires us to talk about velocities. Because the manifold is curved, a velocity vector cannot "live" on the surface itself---it would point off the surface tangentially!

Instead, velocity lives in the \textbf{Tangent Space} attached to that specific point.

\begin{definition}{Tangent Space $T_q \mathcal{Q}$}{tangent_space}
At any specific configuration $q \in \mathcal{Q}$, the \emph{Tangent Space} $T_q\mathcal{Q}$ is the perfectly flat, $n$-dimensional Euclidean vector space containing all possible velocity vectors passing through $q$. 
\end{definition}

Crucially, the tangent space is \emph{local}. The tangent plane touching a sphere at the North Pole is a completely different vector space from the tangent plane touching it at the Equator. You cannot directly add a velocity vector from the North Pole to a velocity vector from the Equator. This fundamental geometric fact breaks standard linear control theory when applied to highly articulated robots or spacecraft. 

\subsection{The Tangent Bundle $T\mathcal{Q}$}
If you take the manifold $\mathcal{Q}$ and attach every single local tangent space $T_q\mathcal{Q}$ to every single point $q$, the resulting composite object is called the \textbf{Tangent Bundle}, denoted $T\mathcal{Q}$. This bundle is exactly what defines the \textbf{State Space} $\mathcal{X}$. It contains every position and every possible velocity at that position.

\begin{laymansbox}{Mathematical Reminder: Tangent Bundle vs. State Space}{tangent_bundle_reminder}
Whenever you see the "State Vector" $\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{q}^T, \dot{\mathbf{q}}^T]^T$, recall its geometric meaning. The upper half ($\mathbf{q}$) is a point on the curved manifold $\mathcal{Q}$. The lower half ($\dot{\mathbf{q}}$) is a vector living in the flat tangent space $T_q\mathcal{Q}$ attached specifically to that point $\mathbf{q}$. Together, they coordinate the Tangent Bundle.
\end{laymansbox}

\section{Generalized Coordinates}

When we model a complex physical system (such as the 15-DOF human golfer in Volume III), we do not track the 3D $(x,y,z)$ position of the golfer's wrist, elbow, and shoulder separately, and then painstakingly write huge algebraic constraint equations ensuring their bones don't stretch or compress during simulation.

Instead, we use \textbf{Generalized Coordinates}. Since an elbow is a pin joint allowing only 1 DOF of rotation, we abandon $(x,y,z)$ mapping entirely and instead just track the single angle $\theta_\text{elbow}$. If we define the angle of the shoulder $\theta_\text{shoulder}$, and the fixed length of the humerus bone $L_1$, the Cartesian position of the elbow is instantly known.

Generalized coordinates are the minimal set of independent variables $q_1, q_2, \dots q_n$ that perfectly span the Configuration Space. They are the most mathematically efficient map of the mechanism's geometry. In robotics, $\mathbf{q}$ almost always refers to the physical joint angles of the motors. 

\subsection{Forward Kinematics\index{Kinematics}}

The mathematical function that translates our abstract Generalized Coordinates $\mathbf{q}$ (the angles in our configuration space) back into the physical 3D Euclidean space of the real world is called \textbf{Forward Kinematics}.

If $\mathbf{p}_{end} = [x, y, z]^T \in \Reals^3$ is the Cartesian workspace position of the end of our robotic arm (or a golf clubhead), Forward Kinematics is the generally nonlinear function $f_{kin}$:

\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{p}_{end} = f_{kin}(\mathbf{q})
\end{equation}

By using trigonometry, we stack the lengths of the links and the angles of the joints $\mathbf{q}$ to compute exactly where the end-effector sits in physical space. For a simple 2-link robotic arm rotating on a flat 2D plane (with fixed link lengths $L_1, L_2$ and variable joint angles $q_1, q_2$):

\begin{align}
  x &= L_1 \cos(q_1) + L_2 \cos(q_1 + q_2) \\
  y &= L_1 \sin(q_1) + L_2 \sin(q_1 + q_2)
\end{align}

Notice that to know where the physical end of the arm is in the real world, we only needed the configuration vector $\mathbf{q} \in \mathcal{Q}$. The inverse problem---finding the required joint angles to reach a desired target $(x,y)$---is called Inverse Kinematics (IK), and is significantly harder because multiple joint configurations might reach the same physical target (e.g., reaching overhand vs. underhand to grab a cup).

\section{Holonomic vs. Non-Holonomic Constraints}

It is critical to distinguish between constraints that restrict position versus those that restrict velocity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Holonomic Constraints:} These restrict the actual \emph{position} of the system (e.g., a train locked to a physical track, or two robot links welded together). They permanently reduce the dimension of the Configuration Space $\mathcal{Q}$. 
    \item \textbf{Non-Holonomic Constraints:} These restrict the \emph{velocity} of the system, but not its reachable positions. The classic example is a car: a car cannot move perfectly sideways (its velocity $v_y = 0$ in its local frame). However, by parallel parking (going forward, turning, reversing), a car can eventually reach \emph{any} $(X, Y)$ position and orientation on the 2D plane. It has 3 DOF (X, Y, Heading), but only 2 inputs (Gas, Steering). Thus, its Configuration Space is not reduced, but its instantaneous control is severely constrained.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}

When controlling physical motion, you are fundamentally commanding a point to traverse through Configuration Space $\mathcal{Q}$. 

However, mapping $\mathcal{Q}$ is vastly more complex than simple $(x,y)$ geometry. Consider a simple drone spinning freely. In physical space, it can rotate horizontally 360 degrees, and keep rotating 720 degrees, and 1080 degrees, looping back over itself physically but extending its generalized coordinate to infinity. This infinite looping implies that $\mathcal{Q}$ is NOT a simple flat plane. It curves recursively onto itself like a circle or a sphere. 

To govern angular movement and rotations formally—and escape the mathematical nightmares of "Gimbal Lock"—we must upgrade from linear vectors and Euler angles to transformation matrices. We must transition to the Special Orthogonal Group $SO(3)$.
