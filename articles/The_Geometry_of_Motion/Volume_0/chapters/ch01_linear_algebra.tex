\section{Vectors, Matrices, and Vector Spaces}

Before mapping physics, we must understand the fundamental data structures of control theory: vectors and matrices.

A \textbf{vector} $\mathbf{v}$ is an ordered array of numbers. It can represent a position in space, a velocity profile, or a list of sensor readings. In physics, vectors implicitly assume a coordinate frame (e.g., $X, Y, Z$). A generic column vector in $n$-dimensional space is denoted:
\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} \in \Reals^n
\end{equation}

A \textbf{matrix} $\mathbf{M}$ is a 2D grid of numbers. If a vector represents a point, a matrix acts as a set of instructions on how to \emph{transform} that point---stretching it, rotating it, or moving it into an entirely different dimensional space. It maps vectors from one space to another ($\mathbf{M} : \Reals^m \to \Reals^n$).
\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} m_{11} & m_{12} & \cdots & m_{1m} \\ m_{21} & m_{22} & \cdots & m_{2m} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ m_{n1} & m_{n2} & \cdots & m_{nm} \end{bmatrix} \in \Reals^{n \times m}
\end{equation}

When we multiply a matrix by a vector ($\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}$), the matrix takes a linear combination of its columns using the elements of $\mathbf{v}$ as weights. Throughout this book, when we rotate a robot arm, we are physically applying a matrix to the vectors extending along its links.

\subsection{Rank and Null Space}
Not all transformations lose information, but some do. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rank:} The \emph{rank} of a matrix is the number of linearly independent columns it contains. If a $3 \times 3$ matrix has a rank of 2, it collapses full 3D space into a completely flat 2D plane. In robotics, the rank of the Jacobian matrix tells you how many directions your end-effector can actually move. If the rank drops, the robot is in a \emph{singularity} (stuck).
    \item \textbf{Null Space (Kernel):} The null space $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{M})$ is the set of all vectors $\mathbf{x}$ that get crushed to zero: $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. For a humanoid robot, the null space of the arm's Jacobian describes all the "internal motions" it can make (like wildly swinging its elbow) while keeping its hand completely still holding a cup of coffee.
    \item \textbf{The Determinant (Volumetric Interpretation):} For a square matrix, the determinant calculates the factor by which the matrix scales lengths, areas, or volumes. Imagine a theoretical $1 \times 1 \times 1$ cube. If you multiply every point in that cube by a $3 \times 3$ matrix $\mathbf{M}$, the cube warps into a parallelepiped. The newly formed volume is precisely $\det(\mathbf{M})$. 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If $\det(\mathbf{M}) = 1$, the volume is perfectly preserved (like a pure rotation in space).
        \item If $\det(\mathbf{M}) = 0$, the volume is crushed completely flat (the matrix loses rank and goes singular).
        \item If $\det(\mathbf{M}) < 0$, the space has been turned completely inside out (a mirror reflection). 
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{The Dot Product and Cross Product}

Two fundamental operations exist for combining vectors in 3-dimensional space:

\subsection{The Dot Product}
The dot product (or inner product) is a scalar (a single number). It measures how much two vectors "align" with each other. It is calculated by multiplying matching components and summing them.
\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{b} = a_x b_x + a_y b_y + a_z b_z = \norm{\mathbf{a}}\norm{\mathbf{b}}\cos(\theta)
\end{equation}
If two vectors are perfectly perpendicular ($90^\circ$), their dot product is exactly 0. In control theory, we use dot products to compute kinetic energy ($E = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{M} \mathbf{v}$) and to project forces mathematically onto specific mechanical joints. The inner product fundamentally generalizes the concept of an angle to high dimensions.

\subsection{The Cross Product and the Skew-Symmetric Matrix}
The cross product is a vector. It takes two vectors and generates a brand new vector that is perfectly perpendicular to both of them.
\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} a_y b_z - a_z b_y \\ a_z b_x - a_x b_z \\ a_x b_y - a_y b_x \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

In physics, the cross product is the mathematical engine of rotation. If you apply a force $\mathbf{F}$ at a distance $\mathbf{r}$ from a hinge, the resulting torque $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ twisting the hinge is exactly the cross product: $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$. 
Crucially, the cross product can be rewritten as a matrix multiplication. We define the \emph{skew-symmetric matrix} $[\mathbf{a}_\times]$:
\begin{equation}
    [\mathbf{a}_\times] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -a_z & a_y \\ a_z & 0 & -a_x \\ -a_y & a_x & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \implies \quad \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = [\mathbf{a}_\times] \mathbf{b}
\end{equation}
Notice that for a skew-symmetric matrix, $[\mathbf{a}_\times]^T = -[\mathbf{a}_\times]$. This matrix representation is absolutely pivotal for representing 3D rotations in Lie Algebra, which we cover heavily in Chapter 4.

\section{Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, and Quadratic Forms}

When you multiply a vector by a matrix, the vector usually changes direction. However, for almost every square matrix, there are a few special directions that do \emph{not} change their axis when the matrix hits them. They only stretch, shrink, or flip. 

\subsection{The Eigen-Decomposition}
These magical vectors are called \textbf{Eigenvectors} ($\mathbf{v}_i$), and the amount they stretch is called the \textbf{Eigenvalue} ($\lambda_i$). 
\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i
\end{equation}

Why do we care? 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $\mathbf{M}$ represents the mass and inertia tensor of a tumbling satellite in space, its eigenvectors tell us the "Principal Axes"---the natural axes where the satellite is easiest and hardest to spin stably. Think of spinning a book in the air: it spins cleanly along its shortest or longest axis (eigenvectors), but tumbles chaotically if you try to spin it along the intermediate axis.
    \item If $\mathbf{M}$ represents a control system's dynamics matrix ($A$), the eigenvalues completely dictate stability. If any eigenvalue has a positive real part ($Re(\lambda) > 0$), the system will explode exponentially. If all eigenvalues are negative ($Re(\lambda) < 0$), the system safely stabilizes back to rest. Eigenvalues are the fundamental heartbeat of linear dynamics.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}{Visualizing Eigenvectors: The Stretching Rubber Sheet}{visualize_eigenvectors}
Imagine printing a perfect circle onto a rubber square. You grab the edges of the rubber and stretch it wildly in various directions. The circle stretches into an ellipse. 

Most straight lines drawn on the circle have now been rotated and tilted. However, exactly two lines pointing across the circle did not rotate at all; they only got \emph{longer}. These non-rotating axes are your \textbf{Eigenvectors}. The factor by which those specific lines stretched (perhaps one stretched by $3\times$ and the other shrunk by $0.5\times$) are your \textbf{Eigenvalues}. The matrix $\mathbf{M}$ is simply the algebraic instruction set for that stretching operation.
\end{example}

\subsection{Symmetric Matrices and Quadratic Forms}
In optimization and energy mechanics, we frequently encounter \emph{quadratic forms}, written as $\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{M} \mathbf{x}$. 
\begin{equation}
    q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{M} \mathbf{x} = \sum_{i,j} M_{ij} x_i x_j
\end{equation}
Kinetic energy ($E = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{M} \mathbf{v}$), optimal control costs ($J = \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{R} \mathbf{u}$), and Lyapunov functions all rely on symmetric metrics. A symmetric matrix ($\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M}^T$) has a beautiful property: \emph{all of its eigenvalues are strictly real, and all of its eigenvectors are perfectly orthogonal to each other}.

If all eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix $\mathbf{M}$ are strictly positive ($\lambda_i > 0$), the matrix is called \textbf{Positive Definite} ($\mathbf{M} \succ 0$). This guarantees that $\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{M} \mathbf{x} > 0$ for any non-zero $\mathbf{x}$. In control, if you can prove your energy matrix is positive definite, you have mathematically proven that your system's energy has a strict "bottom" (a minimum), which is the cornerstone of stability proofs.

\section{The Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)}
What if a matrix isn't square? Or what if it doesn't have a full set of eigenvectors? The \textbf{Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)} is the ultimate, bulletproof factorization of \emph{any} matrix, regardless of size or rank.
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^T
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{V}$ (Right singular vectors): A rotation in the input space.
    \item $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ (Singular values, $\sigma_i$): Diagonal scaling factors (always positive).
    \item $\mathbf{U}$ (Left singular vectors): A rotation in the output space.
\end{itemize}

Geometrically, the SVD says that \emph{any} linear transformation can be broken down into three simple steps: Rotate the input ($\mathbf{V}^T$), stretch it along the axes ($\mathbf{\Sigma}$), and rotate the output ($\mathbf{U}$). 

\begin{example}{The SVD of the Jacobian (Mobility) Matrix}{svd_jacobian}
In robotics, the Jacobian matrix ($\mathbf{J}$) maps joint speeds to the physical cartesian velocity of the hand ($\mathbf{v}_{\text{hand}} = \mathbf{J} \dot{\mathbf{q}}$). Because the robot likely has more joints than spatial dimensions (e.g., 7 joints moving in 3D space), $\mathbf{J}$ is a non-square $3 \times 7$ matrix. You cannot calculate its eigenvectors. 

Instead, we take the SVD of the Jacobian ($\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^T$):
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{U} \in \Reals^{3\times3}$: The columns of $\mathbf{U}$ define the actual geometric 3D directions the hand can push in the physical room.
    \item $\mathbf{\Sigma} \in \Reals^{3\times7}$: The singular values ($\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$) form the axes of the \textbf{Velocity Manipulability Ellipsoid}. They tell us exactly how fast the robot can move in the corresponding $\mathbf{U}$ directions. If the arm is stretched perfectly straight, one singular value drops to $\sigma_k = 0$, the ellipsoid flattens into a pancake, and the robot loses a degree of freedom (it cannot pull straight back).
    \item $\mathbf{V}^T \in \Reals^{7\times7}$: These vectors define the specific combinations of joint motors required to move the hand along those lines.
\end{itemize}
 Conversely, the \textbf{Force Manipulability Ellipsoid} uses the exact same axes $\mathbf{U}$, but its scaling is defined by $1/\sigma_i$. Where a robot is fastest, it is mechanically weakest. Where it is slowest (near singularities), it can exert infinite mechanical leverage.
\end{example}
