\begin{laymansbox}{Context \& Use Case: The Problem with Spinning}{context_box}
\textbf{The Math You Will See:} We introduce \textbf{Rotation Matrices} ($\mathbf{R}$), the \textbf{Special Orthogonal Group} $\SO(3)$, and \textbf{Quaternions} ($\mathbf{q}$).
\textbf{Why We Need It:} Moving a robot arm up, down, left, or right is easy; you just add numbers to $X, Y, Z$. \index{rotation!history!Euler}
\index{rotation!history!Hamilton}
\section{Historical Context: From Euler to Hamilton}
\label{sec:rotation_history}

The mathematics of 3D rotation has been refined over centuries by some of history's greatest mathematicians. In \textbf{1775}, Leonhard Euler published his groundbreaking \emph{Euler's Rotation Theorem}, establishing that any finite rotation in 3D space can be described as a single rotation about a fixed axis by some angle—a foundational geometric insight that remains central to modern rotation calculus.

However, Euler's representation (axis-angle form) requires solving nonlinear equations and computing trigonometric functions. In \textbf{1843}, the Irish mathematician \textbf{William Rowan Hamilton} invented \textbf{Quaternions} ($\mathcal{H}$), an extension of complex numbers to four dimensions. Quaternions elegantly encode rotations as multiplication operations on 4-tuples, bypassing the singularities that plague other representations. Almost simultaneously, \textbf{Olinde Rodrigues} (1795–1853) derived the famous \textbf{Rodrigues rotation formula}, expressing how a vector rotates about an axis—a result that connects Euler's axis-angle theorem to matrix algebra.

These three frameworks—rotation matrices, quaternions, and axis-angle representations—are \emph{mathematically equivalent} but offer different computational and conceptual advantages. Modern robotics and computer graphics employ all three, often switching between them fluidly.

To know where the Body Frame $\{b\}$ is relative to the absolute stationary universe—the Spatial (or World) Frame ($\{s\}$)—we must define two mathematical properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The \textbf{translation} vector $\mathbf{p} \in \Reals^3$ pointing from the origin of $\{s\}$ to the origin of $\{b\}$.
    \item The \textbf{rotation} of the axes of $\{b\}$ relative to the axes of $\{s\}$.
\end{enumerate}

Unlike the straightforward $(X, Y, Z)$ translation vector, rotation is mathematically subtle and surprisingly difficult to parameterize.

\index{rotation matrix!definition}
\section{Rotation Matrices: From First Principles}
\label{sec:rotation_matrices}

\subsection{What is a Rotation Matrix?}

To represent a rotation, we examine the three orthonormal basis vectors (axes) of the body frame $\{b\}$: the unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_b, \hat{\mathbf{y}}_b,$ and $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_b$, each expressed in the coordinates of the spatial frame $\{s\}$. Stack these three column vectors side-by-side:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rotation_matrix_def}
   \mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & | \\ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_b & \hat{\mathbf{y}}_b & \hat{\mathbf{z}}_b \\ | & | & | \end{bmatrix} \in \Reals^{3 \times 3}
\end{equation}

This is a \textbf{Rotation Matrix}. Each column is a unit vector, and any two columns are orthogonal to each other. This orthonormality is the key property.

\subsection{Orthonormality Constraints}

\begin{theorem}{Orthonormality Conditions}{orthonormality}
A matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \Reals^{3 \times 3}$ is a valid rotation matrix if and only if:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Orthogonality:} $\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$, which means $\mathbf{R}^{-1} = \mathbf{R}^T$.
    \item \textbf{Determinant Constraint:} $\det(\mathbf{R}) = +1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\mathbf{R} = [\mathbf{c}_1 \mid \mathbf{c}_2 \mid \mathbf{c}_3]$, where $\mathbf{c}_i$ are the columns. The product $\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R}$ yields a $3 \times 3$ matrix whose $(i,j)$ entry is $\mathbf{c}_i^T \mathbf{c}_j$. For this to equal $\mathbf{I}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Diagonal: $\mathbf{c}_i^T \mathbf{c}_i = 1$ for each $i$ (each column is unit length).
    \item Off-diagonal: $\mathbf{c}_i^T \mathbf{c}_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$ (columns are mutually orthogonal).
\end{itemize}
These are exactly 6 independent constraints on the 9 matrix entries, leaving $9 - 6 = 3$ degrees of freedom.

The determinant condition $\det(\mathbf{R}) = 1$ distinguishes \emph{proper rotations} (right-handed orientation preserving) from reflections, which would have $\det(\mathbf{R}) = -1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Three Elementary Rotation Matrices}

Any rotation in 3D can be decomposed into rotations about the principal axes. We derive the three elementary rotation matrices.

\index{rotation matrix!Rx}
\begin{theorem}{Rotation About the \texorpdfstring{$x$}{x}-Axis}{rotation_x_axis}
A rotation by angle $\theta$ about the $x$-axis, with the rotation viewed as right-hand rule (counterclockwise when looking from positive $x$ toward the origin), is given by:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Rx}
\mathbf{R}_x(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Geometric Interpretation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Column 1:} $[1, 0, 0]^T$ is the $x$-axis, unchanged by rotation about itself.
    \item \textbf{Column 2:} $[0, \cos\theta, \sin\theta]^T$ is where the original $y$-axis rotates to.
    \item \textbf{Column 3:} $[0, -\sin\theta, \cos\theta]^T$ is where the original $z$-axis rotates to.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{derivation}
The $x$-axis unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_x = [1, 0, 0]^T$ is invariant. The $y$ and $z$ axes lie in the $yz$-plane, which undergoes a 2D rotation:
\[\begin{bmatrix} y' \\ z' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} y \\ z \end{bmatrix}.\]
Embedding this in 3D yields equation \eqref{eq:Rx}.
\end{derivation}

\index{rotation matrix!Ry}
\begin{theorem}{Rotation About the \texorpdfstring{$y$}{y}-Axis}{rotation_y_axis}
A rotation by angle $\theta$ about the $y$-axis is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Ry}
\mathbf{R}_y(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\index{rotation matrix!Rz}
\begin{theorem}{Rotation About the \texorpdfstring{$z$}{z}-Axis}{rotation_z_axis}
A rotation by angle $\theta$ about the $z$-axis is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Rz}
\mathbf{R}_z(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Each elementary matrix satisfies $\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$ and $\det(\mathbf{R}) = 1$ by construction (they encode orthonormal bases).

\index{SO(3)!definition}
\section{The Special Orthogonal Group: SO(3)}
\label{sec:SO3}

\subsection{SO(3) as a Group}

\begin{definition}{The Group \texorpdfstring{$\SO(3)$}{SO(3)}}{SO3_group}
The \textbf{Special Orthogonal Group} $\SO(3)$~\cite{Murray1994} is the set of all $3 \times 3$ real matrices satisfying:
\begin{equation}
\SO(3) = \{\mathbf{R} \in \Reals^{3 \times 3} : \mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}, \, \det(\mathbf{R}) = 1\}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{\texorpdfstring{$\SO(3)$}{SO(3)} is a Group}{SO3_is_group}
The set $\SO(3)$ forms a mathematical group under matrix multiplication.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We verify the four group axioms:

\textbf{Closure:} If $\mathbf{R}_1, \mathbf{R}_2 \in \SO(3)$, then:
\begin{align}
(\mathbf{R}_1 \mathbf{R}_2)^T (\mathbf{R}_1 \mathbf{R}_2) &= \mathbf{R}_2^T \mathbf{R}_1^T \mathbf{R}_1 \mathbf{R}_2 = \mathbf{R}_2^T \mathbf{I} \mathbf{R}_2 = \mathbf{I}, \\
\det(\mathbf{R}_1 \mathbf{R}_2) &= \det(\mathbf{R}_1) \det(\mathbf{R}_2) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1.
\end{align}
Thus $\mathbf{R}_1 \mathbf{R}_2 \in \SO(3)$.

\textbf{Associativity:} Matrix multiplication is associative: $(\mathbf{R}_1 \mathbf{R}_2) \mathbf{R}_3 = \mathbf{R}_1 (\mathbf{R}_2 \mathbf{R}_3)$.

\textbf{Identity:} The identity matrix $\mathbf{I}$ satisfies $\mathbf{I}^T \mathbf{I} = \mathbf{I}$ and $\det(\mathbf{I}) = 1$, so $\mathbf{I} \in \SO(3)$.

\textbf{Inverses:} For $\mathbf{R} \in \SO(3)$, we have $\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$, so $\mathbf{R}^{-1} = \mathbf{R}^T$. Moreover:
\begin{equation}
\det(\mathbf{R}^T) = \det(\mathbf{R}) = 1,
\end{equation}
so $\mathbf{R}^{-1} \in \SO(3)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{SO(3) as a Smooth Manifold}

\begin{theorem}{\texorpdfstring{$\SO(3)$}{SO(3)} is a 3-Dimensional Smooth Manifold}{SO3_manifold}
The set $\SO(3)$ is a smooth, compact manifold of dimension 3.
\end{theorem}

\begin{argument}
The orthogonality constraint $\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$ imposes 6 independent scalar equations (the symmetric matrix $\mathbf{I}$ has 6 independent entries) on the 9 entries of $\mathbf{R}$. Additionally, $\det(\mathbf{R}) = 1$ is one equation, but it is redundant: for orthogonal matrices with real entries, $\det(\mathbf{R}) = \pm 1$ automatically, and we select the component with determinant $+1$ by continuity and connectedness.

Thus, the effective number of independent constraints is 6, leaving:
\begin{equation}
\dim(\SO(3)) = 9 - 6 = 3.
\end{equation}

Each point in $\SO(3)$ admits a local parameterization by 3 parameters (e.g., Euler angles in a suitable domain), confirming that $\SO(3)$ is a smooth 3-dimensional manifold.
\end{argument}

\begin{principle}{The SO(3) Manifold}{so3_principle}
  The continuous, infinite geometric space of all possible valid $3 \times 3$ rotation matrices satisfying $\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$ and $\det(\mathbf{R}) = 1$ is a mathematical \textbf{manifold} called the \textbf{Special Orthogonal Group in 3 Dimensions}, denoted as $\SO(3)$.

  If a Configuration Space $\mathcal{Q}$ is simply a rigid body freely rotating around a fixed point, we say $\mathcal{Q} = \SO(3)$.
\end{principle}

\index{Euler angles}
\section{Euler Angles: Gimbal Lock and Beyond}
\label{sec:euler_angles}

\subsection{Definition of Euler Angles}

The most intuitive way for a human to describe the orientation of an aircraft or robotic arm is to split rotation into three successive rotations about primary axes. This is known as an \textbf{Euler Angle} representation: $\boldsymbol{\theta} = [\phi, \theta, \psi]^T$.

While highly intuitive for human pilots, Euler Angles harbor a fatal mathematical flaw known as \textbf{Gimbal Lock}.

\subsection{The ZYX Convention}

One common convention is \textbf{ZYX Euler angles} (also called \emph{yaw-pitch-roll}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rotate by $\psi$ (yaw) about the original $z$-axis.
    \item Rotate by $\theta$ (pitch) about the \emph{current} $y$-axis (after yaw).
    \item Rotate by $\phi$ (roll) about the \emph{current} $x$-axis (after yaw and pitch).
\end{enumerate}

The composite rotation matrix is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:euler_zyx}
\mathbf{R}(\phi, \theta, \psi) = \mathbf{R}_x(\phi) \mathbf{R}_y(\theta) \mathbf{R}_z(\psi).
\end{equation}

Computing this product explicitly:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:euler_zyx_full}
\mathbf{R}(\phi, \theta, \psi) = \begin{bmatrix}
\cos\psi \cos\theta & -\sin\psi \cos\phi + \cos\psi \sin\theta \sin\phi & \sin\psi \sin\phi + \cos\psi \sin\theta \cos\phi \\
\sin\psi \cos\theta & \cos\psi \cos\phi + \sin\psi \sin\theta \sin\phi & -\cos\psi \sin\phi + \sin\psi \sin\theta \cos\phi \\
-\sin\theta & \cos\theta \sin\phi & \cos\theta \cos\phi
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The ZXZ Convention}

Another standard convention is \textbf{ZXZ Euler angles} (symmetric, used in physics and astronomy):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rotate by $\phi$ about the original $z$-axis.
    \item Rotate by $\theta$ about the \emph{current} $x$-axis.
    \item Rotate by $\psi$ about the \emph{current} $z$-axis (after the first two rotations).
\end{enumerate}

The composite rotation is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}(\phi, \theta, \psi) = \mathbf{R}_z(\psi) \mathbf{R}_x(\theta) \mathbf{R}_z(\phi).
\end{equation}

\index{gimbal lock}
\subsection{Gimbal Lock: Singularity and Proof}

\begin{laymansbox}{Gimbal Lock: Physical Intuition}{gimballock}
  If an airplane pitches perfectly $90^\circ$ straight up into the sky, its Roll axis (pointing out its nose) now perfectly aligns with its original Yaw axis (pointing perfectly down towards the Earth).

  Mathematically, two independent degrees of freedom have become linearly dependent—they merged into the identical physical rotation axis. A degree of freedom is literally lost during the calculation. If you attempt to control the plane passing through this singularity, the Jacobian mapping Euler angle rates to angular velocity becomes singular, producing undefined or numerically unstable results (NaN values, wildly large rate commands).
\end{laymansbox}

\begin{theorem}{Gimbal Lock at \texorpdfstring{$\theta = \pm 90^\circ$}{theta = +/- 90 degrees}}{gimbal_lock_proof}
For ZYX Euler angles, the Jacobian matrix relating angular velocity components to Euler angle rate components becomes singular when $\theta = \pm 90^\circ$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{derivation}
The relationship between angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega} = [\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z]^T$ in the spatial frame and Euler angle rates $\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = [\dot{\phi}, \dot{\theta}, \dot{\psi}]^T$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\omega} = \mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, \dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the Jacobian. For ZYX angles, it can be shown that:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}(\phi, \theta, \psi) = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & -\sin\psi & \cos\psi \cos\theta \\
0 & \cos\psi & \sin\psi \cos\theta \\
1 & 0 & -\sin\theta
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

When $\theta = 90^\circ$, we have $\cos\theta = 0$, so:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}\left(\phi, 90^\circ, \psi\right) = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & -\sin\psi & 0 \\
0 & \cos\psi & 0 \\
1 & 0 & -1
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The third column is linearly dependent on the first two, so $\text{rank}(\mathbf{J}) < 3$ and $\det(\mathbf{J}) = 0$. The Jacobian is singular, making it impossible to invert: given $\boldsymbol{\omega}$, we cannot solve uniquely for $\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$. Physically, the yaw and roll rates become indistinguishable.
\end{derivation}

Since our control theory and geometric motion algorithms fundamentally rely on continuous trajectories twisting through all possible spatial angles (like chaotic golf club arcs or throwing motions), we must completely abandon Euler angles for internal calculations. We cannot afford mathematical singularities.

\index{Lie group!definition}
\index{Lie algebra!definition}
\section{Differential Geometry Primer: Lie Groups and Lie Algebras}
\label{sec:lie_groups_algebras}

We have established that $\SO(3)$ is a manifold. However, it is not just any manifold. Because you can combine two rotations to get a third rotation (via matrix multiplication), and every rotation can be smoothly inverted, $\SO(3)$ possesses both algebraic structure (it is a mathematical \emph{group}) and geometric structure (it is a \emph{smooth manifold}).

\begin{definition}{Lie Group}{lie_group}
A \emph{Lie Group} is a smooth manifold that is also a mathematical group, where the group operations (multiplication and inversion) are smooth functions. As a result, we can perform calculus on them. $\SO(3)$ is the preeminent example of a Lie Group in mechanics.
\end{definition}

\subsection{The Tangent Space and Lie Algebra}

If $\SO(3)$ is a curved manifold of positions (rotations), what is its velocity space? By our earlier definition in Chapter 3, the velocities must live in the Tangent Space.

If we examine the tangent space at the identity rotation (the "zero" rotation, matrix $\mathbf{I}$), we discover an incredibly special mathematical object called the \textbf{Lie Algebra}, denoted by lowercase $\so(3)$.

\begin{definition}{Lie Algebra \texorpdfstring{$\so(3)$}{so(3)}}{lie_algebra}
The \emph{Lie Algebra} $\so(3)$ is the tangent space to the Lie Group $\SO(3)$ at the identity element. Physically, it represents the space of all possible angular velocities (spin rates) when the object is currently unrotated. Mathematically, it consists of all $3 \times 3$ \emph{skew-symmetric} matrices: matrices $[\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times$ satisfying $[\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times^T = -[\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times$.
\end{definition}

A skew-symmetric matrix representing a vector $\boldsymbol{\omega} = [\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z]^T$ is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:skew_symmetric}
[\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\omega_z & \omega_y \\ \omega_z & 0 & -\omega_x \\ -\omega_y & \omega_x & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\begin{laymansbox}{Mathematical Reminder: Group vs. Algebra}{group_vs_algebra_reminder}
Whenever you hear \textbf{Lie Group} (like $\SO(3)$ or $\SE(3)$), think of the curved, global manifold of \emph{Positions/Configurations}.
Whenever you hear \textbf{Lie Algebra} (like $\so(3)$ or $\se(3)$), think of the perfectly flat, linear tangent space of \emph{Velocities}. We can do standard linear control math in the Lie Algebra, but we map the results back onto the curved Lie Group using the \emph{Exponential Map} (covered next) to actually rotate the physical robot.
\end{laymansbox}

\section{Rodrigues' Rotation Formula: Full Derivation}
\label{sec:rodrigues}
\index{Rodrigues' rotation formula}
\index{matrix exponential!rotation}

We now prove the celebrated \textbf{Rodrigues' rotation formula}\index{Rodrigues' rotation formula}, which gives a closed-form expression for the matrix exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix. This result, first obtained by Olinde Rodrigues in 1840~\cite{rodrigues1840}, is the computational backbone of the exponential map for $\SO(3)$.

\subsection{The Key Identity: \texorpdfstring{$[\hat{\omega}]^3 = -[\hat{\omega}]$}{[omega-hat]\^{}3 = -[omega-hat]}}

Let $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \in \Reals^3$ be a unit vector ($\norm{\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}} = 1$) and $[\hat{\omega}] \in \so(3)$ the corresponding skew-symmetric matrix. We first establish a crucial algebraic identity.

\begin{theorem}{Cyclic Property of Skew-Symmetric Matrices}{skew_cubic}
For any unit vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ with skew-symmetric matrix $[\hat{\omega}]$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:skew_cubic}
[\hat{\omega}]^3 = -[\hat{\omega}].
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Recall that for a skew-symmetric matrix, the action $[\hat{\omega}]\mathbf{v} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{v}$. Thus:
\begin{equation}
[\hat{\omega}]^2 \mathbf{v} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times (\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{v}).
\end{equation}
By the vector triple product identity $\mathbf{a} \times (\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{a}(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{v}) - \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a})$ with $\norm{\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}} = 1$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:skew_squared}
[\hat{\omega}]^2 = \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^T - \mathbf{I}.
\end{equation}
Therefore:
\begin{equation}
[\hat{\omega}]^3 = [\hat{\omega}](\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^T - \mathbf{I}) = [\hat{\omega}]\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^T - [\hat{\omega}].
\end{equation}
Since $[\hat{\omega}]\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \mathbf{0}$, we obtain $[\hat{\omega}]^3 = -[\hat{\omega}]$.
\end{proof}

This identity implies that all higher powers of $[\hat{\omega}]$ reduce to either $[\hat{\omega}]$ or $[\hat{\omega}]^2$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:skew_power_cycle}
[\hat{\omega}]^4 = -[\hat{\omega}]^2, \quad [\hat{\omega}]^5 = [\hat{\omega}], \quad [\hat{\omega}]^6 = [\hat{\omega}]^2, \quad \ldots
\end{equation}

\subsection{Taylor Expansion and Regrouping}

The matrix exponential is defined by its Taylor series:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exp_taylor}
e^{[\hat{\omega}]\theta} = \mathbf{I} + \theta[\hat{\omega}] + \frac{\theta^2}{2!}[\hat{\omega}]^2 + \frac{\theta^3}{3!}[\hat{\omega}]^3 + \frac{\theta^4}{4!}[\hat{\omega}]^4 + \cdots
\end{equation}

Substituting $[\hat{\omega}]^3 = -[\hat{\omega}]$, $[\hat{\omega}]^4 = -[\hat{\omega}]^2$, $[\hat{\omega}]^5 = [\hat{\omega}]$, etc., and grouping by powers of $[\hat{\omega}]$:
\begin{equation}
e^{[\hat{\omega}]\theta} = \mathbf{I} + \underbrace{\left(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots\right)}_{\sin\theta}[\hat{\omega}] + \underbrace{\left(\frac{\theta^2}{2!} - \frac{\theta^4}{4!} + \frac{\theta^6}{6!} - \cdots\right)}_{1 - \cos\theta}[\hat{\omega}]^2.
\end{equation}

The odd-power series is exactly $\sin\theta$ and the even-power series is exactly $1 - \cos\theta$.

\begin{theorem}{Rodrigues' Rotation Formula}{rodrigues_formula}
\index{Rodrigues' rotation formula}
For a unit axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \in \Reals^3$ and angle $\theta \in \Reals$, the rotation matrix is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rodrigues}
\boxed{e^{[\hat{\omega}]\theta} = \mathbf{I} + \sin\theta\,[\hat{\omega}] + (1 - \cos\theta)\,[\hat{\omega}]^2.}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, using~\eqref{eq:skew_squared}, the action on a vector $\mathbf{v}$ is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rodrigues_vector}
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\cos\theta + (\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{v})\sin\theta + \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \mathbf{v})(1 - \cos\theta).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The vector form~\eqref{eq:rodrigues_vector} has a geometric interpretation: decompose $\mathbf{v}$ into a component along $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ (unchanged by rotation) and a component perpendicular to $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ (rotated by $\theta$ in the plane normal to $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$). See \cite{rodrigues1840} for the original derivation and \cite{Murray1994} for the modern Lie-theoretic perspective.

\section{Quaternion Algebra}
\label{sec:quaternions}

\subsection{Definition and Representation}

A \textbf{quaternion} $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{H}$ is a 4-tuple written as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:quaternion_def}
\mathbf{q} = \begin{bmatrix} q_w \\ q_x \\ q_y \\ q_z \end{bmatrix} = q_w + q_x \mathbf{i} + q_y \mathbf{j} + q_z \mathbf{k}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ are the three \emph{imaginary units} satisfying:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{i}^2 = \mathbf{j}^2 = \mathbf{k}^2 = \mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}\mathbf{k} = -1, \quad \mathbf{i}\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}, \quad \mathbf{j}\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i}, \quad \mathbf{k}\mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j}.
\end{equation}

Note that quaternion multiplication is \emph{non-commutative}: $\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}$ but $\mathbf{j}\mathbf{i} = -\mathbf{k}$.

\subsection{Quaternion Operations}

\subsubsection{Addition}

Quaternion addition is component-wise:
\begin{equation}
(q_w + q_x \mathbf{i} + q_y \mathbf{j} + q_z \mathbf{k}) + (r_w + r_x \mathbf{i} + r_y \mathbf{j} + r_z \mathbf{k}) = (q_w + r_w) + (q_x + r_x) \mathbf{i} + (q_y + r_y) \mathbf{j} + (q_z + r_z) \mathbf{k}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Hamilton Product (Multiplication)}

The product of two quaternions $\mathbf{q} = q_w + \mathbf{q}_v$ and $\mathbf{r} = r_w + \mathbf{r}_v$, where $\mathbf{q}_v = [q_x, q_y, q_z]^T$ and $\mathbf{r}_v = [r_x, r_y, r_z]^T$, is:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hamilton_product}
\mathbf{q} \otimes \mathbf{r} = \begin{bmatrix}
q_w r_w - \mathbf{q}_v^T \mathbf{r}_v \\
q_w \mathbf{r}_v + r_w \mathbf{q}_v + \mathbf{q}_v \times \mathbf{r}_v
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $\times$ denotes the 3D cross product. In component form:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{q} \otimes \mathbf{r} = \begin{bmatrix}
q_w r_w - q_x r_x - q_y r_y - q_z r_z \\
q_w r_x + q_x r_w + q_y r_z - q_z r_y \\
q_w r_y - q_x r_z + q_y r_w + q_z r_x \\
q_w r_z + q_x r_y - q_y r_x + q_z r_w
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Conjugate}

The \textbf{conjugate} of $\mathbf{q} = q_w + q_x \mathbf{i} + q_y \mathbf{j} + q_z \mathbf{k}$ is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{q}^* = q_w - q_x \mathbf{i} - q_y \mathbf{j} - q_z \mathbf{k} = \begin{bmatrix} q_w \\ -q_x \\ -q_y \\ -q_z \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Norm and Magnitude}

The \textbf{norm} (or magnitude) of a quaternion is:
\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{q}\| = \sqrt{q_w^2 + q_x^2 + q_y^2 + q_z^2}
\end{equation}

A \textbf{unit quaternion} satisfies $\|\mathbf{q}\| = 1$.

\subsubsection{Inverse}

For a unit quaternion $\mathbf{q}$, the inverse is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{q}^{-1} = \mathbf{q}^*
\end{equation}

since $\mathbf{q} \otimes \mathbf{q}^* = \mathbf{q}^* \otimes \mathbf{q} = 1$ (the multiplicative identity quaternion).

For a non-unit quaternion, the inverse is $\mathbf{q}^{-1} = \mathbf{q}^* / \|\mathbf{q}\|^2$.

\subsection{Unit Quaternions and Rotation}

\index{quaternion!unit quaternion}
\index{quaternion!rotation representation}
\begin{theorem}{Unit Quaternions Represent Rotations}{quaternion_rotation}
Every unit quaternion $\mathbf{q}$ can be written as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:quaternion_rotation}
\mathbf{q} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta/2) \\ \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \sin(\theta/2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta/2) \\ \hat{\omega}_x \sin(\theta/2) \\ \hat{\omega}_y \sin(\theta/2) \\ \hat{\omega}_z \sin(\theta/2) \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ is a unit vector (the rotation axis) and $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ is the rotation angle. This quaternion represents a rotation of angle $\theta$ about the axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{laymansbox}{Why the Half-Angles?}{quaternion_half_angle}
  You might notice $\theta/2$ in the formula instead of just $\theta$. This is because applying a quaternion rotation to a vector $\mathbf{v}$ requires multiplying it from the front \emph{and} the back ($\mathbf{q} \otimes \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{q}^{-1}$, where $\mathbf{v}$ is viewed as the quaternion $[0, v_x, v_y, v_z]^T$). The half-angles combine during this double-multiplication to give the perfect full-angle $\theta$ rotation!

  Note also that the quaternions $\mathbf{q}$ and $-\mathbf{q}$ represent the exact same physical rotation in 3D space.
\end{laymansbox}

\index{quaternion!double cover}
\subsection{Double Cover: $q$ and $-q$ Represent the Same Rotation}

\begin{theorem}{Quaternion Double Cover}{quaternion_double_cover}
The unit quaternions $\mathbf{q}$ and $-\mathbf{q}$ represent the same rotation in 3D space. Thus, the map from unit quaternions to $\SO(3)$ is a 2-to-1 covering map, and we say that $S^3$ (the unit sphere in $\mathbb{H}$) is a \emph{double cover} of $\SO(3)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The rotation action of a unit quaternion $\mathbf{q}$ on a pure-imaginary quaternion $\mathbf{v} = [0, \vec{v}]$ is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{q} \otimes \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{q}^*.
\end{equation}

For $-\mathbf{q}$, we compute:
\begin{align}
(-\mathbf{q}) \otimes \mathbf{v} \otimes (-\mathbf{q})^*
  &= (-\mathbf{q}) \otimes \mathbf{v} \otimes (-\mathbf{q}^*) \\
  &= (-1)(\mathbf{q}) \otimes \mathbf{v} \otimes (-1)(\mathbf{q}^*) \\
  &= (-1)(-1)\,\mathbf{q} \otimes \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{q}^* \\
  &= \mathbf{q} \otimes \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{q}^*,
\end{align}
where we used the fact that $(-\mathbf{q})^* = -\mathbf{q}^*$ and that the scalar factor $(-1)$ commutes with quaternion multiplication and appears twice, yielding $(-1)^2 = 1$.

Thus $\mathbf{q}$ and $-\mathbf{q}$ induce the identical rotation on every vector $\vec{v} \in \Reals^3$.
\end{proof}

\index{rotation matrix!quaternion conversion}
\section{Conversion Between Quaternions and Rotation Matrices}
\label{sec:quat_to_matrix}

\subsection{Quaternion to Rotation Matrix}

\begin{theorem}{Quaternion-to-Matrix Conversion}{quat_to_matrix}
A unit quaternion $\mathbf{q} = [q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z]^T$ with $q_w^2 + q_x^2 + q_y^2 + q_z^2 = 1$ corresponds to the rotation matrix:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:quat_to_matrix}
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{q}) = \begin{bmatrix}
1 - 2(q_y^2 + q_z^2) & 2(q_x q_y - q_w q_z) & 2(q_x q_z + q_w q_y) \\
2(q_x q_y + q_w q_z) & 1 - 2(q_x^2 + q_z^2) & 2(q_y q_z - q_w q_x) \\
2(q_x q_z - q_w q_y) & 2(q_y q_z + q_w q_x) & 1 - 2(q_x^2 + q_y^2)
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{derivation}
A rotation by angle $\theta$ about unit axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = [\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z]^T$ can be expressed via the Rodrigues formula~\cite{rodrigues1840}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} + \sin\theta \, [\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}]_\times + (1 - \cos\theta) \, [\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}]_\times^2.
\end{equation}

With $q_w = \cos(\theta/2)$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \sin(\theta/2) = [q_x, q_y, q_z]^T$, one can expand using the identities $\sin\theta = 2\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2) = 2 q_w \sqrt{q_x^2 + q_y^2 + q_z^2}$ and $(1 - \cos\theta) = 2\sin^2(\theta/2) = 2(q_x^2 + q_y^2 + q_z^2)$ to obtain equation \eqref{eq:quat_to_matrix}.
\end{derivation}

\subsection{Rotation Matrix to Quaternion}

\begin{theorem}{Matrix-to-Quaternion Conversion}{matrix_to_quat}
From a rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}$, the unit quaternion can be recovered (up to sign) via:
\begin{equation}
q_w = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1 + R_{11} + R_{22} + R_{33}},
\end{equation}
with $q_x, q_y, q_z$ determined by:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
q_x &= \frac{R_{32} - R_{23}}{4 q_w}, \\
q_y &= \frac{R_{13} - R_{31}}{4 q_w}, \\
q_z &= \frac{R_{21} - R_{12}}{4 q_w}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

For numerical stability, a more robust algorithm selects the largest diagonal element to compute first, then back-solves for the others.

\subsection{Rotation Representation Comparison}
\label{sec:rotation_comparison}
\index{rotation representations!comparison}

Having introduced four rotation representations, we now compare their properties systematically. The choice of representation has significant practical consequences for storage, computation, and numerical stability.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Comparison of rotation representations in $\Reals^3$.}
\label{tab:rotation_comparison}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Property} & \textbf{Rotation Matrix} & \textbf{Euler Angles} & \textbf{Axis-Angle} & \textbf{Quaternion} \\
\midrule
Parameters & 9 & 3 & 3 & 4 \\
Constraints & 6 (orthonormality) & 0 & 0 & 1 (unit norm) \\
Singularities & None & Gimbal lock & $\theta = \pi$ & None \\
Composition & Matrix multiply & Complex & Via exp map & Quaternion multiply \\
Interpolation & Difficult & Nonuniform & Via exp map & SLERP \\
Storage cost & High & Low & Low & Low \\
Unique & Yes & No (periodic) & No ($\theta > \pi$) & No ($\pm\mathbf{q}$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{When to use each representation:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rotation matrices} are the natural choice for \emph{composing} transformations (chain of rigid-body transforms) and for direct application to vectors. Their $9$-parameter redundancy is the cost of a singularity-free, algebraically closed representation.
  \item \textbf{Euler angles} remain useful for \emph{human interpretation} (roll, pitch, yaw) and low-DOF joints, but gimbal lock (Section~\ref{sec:gimbal_lock}) makes them unsuitable for general-purpose computation.
  \item \textbf{Axis-angle} (equivalently, exponential coordinates) is the natural parameterisation of the Lie algebra $\so(3)$ and is essential for optimisation on $\SO(3)$, perturbation analysis, and control.
  \item \textbf{Quaternions} offer the best trade-off for \emph{interpolation} (via SLERP, Section~\ref{sec:slerp}), \emph{storage} (4 parameters, 1 constraint), and \emph{numerical stability} (no singularities, cheap renormalisation). Modern simulation frameworks---including MuJoCo, Drake, and Bullet---typically store orientations as unit quaternions internally and convert to rotation matrices only when matrix operations are required.
\end{itemize}

\index{SLERP}
\section{Spherical Linear Interpolation (SLERP)}
\label{sec:slerp}

In computer graphics and animation, we often need to smoothly interpolate between two rotations. For quaternions, the natural interpolation is \textbf{Spherical Linear Interpolation (SLERP)}.

\begin{definition}{SLERP Formula}{slerp_def}
The SLERP between two unit quaternions $\mathbf{q}_0$ and $\mathbf{q}_1$ at parameter $t \in [0, 1]$ is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:slerp}
\text{SLERP}(\mathbf{q}_0, \mathbf{q}_1; t) = \frac{\sin((1-t) \Omega)}{\sin \Omega} \, \mathbf{q}_0 + \frac{\sin(t \Omega)}{\sin \Omega} \, \mathbf{q}_1
\end{equation}
where $\Omega = \arccos(|\mathbf{q}_0 \cdot \mathbf{q}_1|)$ is the (acute) angle between the quaternions on the unit sphere.
\end{definition}

\begin{derivation}
Two unit quaternions $\mathbf{q}_0$ and $\mathbf{q}_1$ lie on the unit sphere $S^3 \subset \mathbb{H}$. The angle between them is $\Omega = \arccos(\mathbf{q}_0 \cdot \mathbf{q}_1)$ (taking absolute value ensures we pick the shorter arc).

A geodesic (great circle arc) on $S^3$ is the shortest path between two points. Parameterizing the geodesic by arc length with $t \in [0,1]$ (where $t=0$ at $\mathbf{q}_0$ and $t=1$ at $\mathbf{q}_1$) gives the SLERP formula.

If $\Omega \approx 0$ (very close quaternions), use linear interpolation $\text{LERP}(\mathbf{q}_0, \mathbf{q}_1; t) = (1-t) \mathbf{q}_0 + t \mathbf{q}_1$ followed by normalization to avoid numerical division by zero.
\end{derivation}

SLERP is constant-speed interpolation and is the standard for smooth rotational animations in robotics and graphics.

\index{angular velocity}
\section{Angular Velocity and the Matrix Exponential}
\label{sec:angular_velocity}

\subsection{Angular Velocity in the Spatial Frame}

\index{angular velocity!spatial frame}
When a rigid body rotates, its instantaneous rotational motion is characterized by the \textbf{angular velocity} vector $\boldsymbol{\omega} \in \Reals^3$. The magnitude $\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|$ is the rate of rotation (rad/s), and the direction of $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is the instantaneous axis of rotation.

The rate of change of the rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}(t)$ with respect to time is related to angular velocity by:

\begin{theorem}{Matrix Derivative in Spatial Frame}{matrix_derivative_spatial}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:R_dot_spatial}
\dot{\mathbf{R}}(t) = [\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times \, \mathbf{R}(t)
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is expressed in the spatial frame and $[\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times$ is the skew-symmetric matrix defined in equation \eqref{eq:skew_symmetric}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating the orthogonality constraint $\mathbf{R}^T(t) \mathbf{R}(t) = \mathbf{I}$ with respect to time:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}[\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R}] = \dot{\mathbf{R}}^T \mathbf{R} + \mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{0}.
\end{equation}

Rearranging:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}} = -(\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}})^T,
\end{equation}

so $\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}}$ is skew-symmetric. Define $[\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times := \mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}}$. Then:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{R} [\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times.
\end{equation}

However, by convention in robotics, we often write $\dot{\mathbf{R}} = [\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times \mathbf{R}$ where $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is the spatial frame angular velocity. Both are correct; the difference is in the choice of frame.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Angular Velocity in the Body Frame}

\index{angular velocity!body frame}
When the angular velocity is expressed in the body-fixed frame, the differential equation becomes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:R_dot_body}
\dot{\mathbf{R}}(t) = \mathbf{R}(t) [\boldsymbol{\omega}_b]_\times
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\omega}_b$ is the angular velocity in the body frame.

\subsection{Proof that Angular Velocity Lives in $\so(3)$}

\begin{theorem}{Angular Velocity is a Lie Algebra Element}{angular_velocity_so3}
For any rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}(t) \in \SO(3)$, the quantity $\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}} \in \so(3)$ is a skew-symmetric matrix, i.e., an element of the Lie algebra $\so(3)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From $\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$, differentiate:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{R}}^T \mathbf{R} + \mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{0}.
\end{equation}

Taking the transpose of the second term:
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}})^T = \dot{\mathbf{R}}^T \mathbf{R}.
\end{equation}

From the differentiated constraint, $\dot{\mathbf{R}}^T \mathbf{R} = -\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}}$, so:
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}})^T = -\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}}.
\end{equation}

Thus $\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}}$ is skew-symmetric, confirming it belongs to $\so(3)$.
\end{proof}

\index{axis-angle representation}
\section{Axis-Angle Representation and Euler's Rotation Theorem}
\label{sec:axis_angle}

\begin{theorem}{Euler's Rotation Theorem}{euler_rotation_theorem}
Every rotation in 3D can be expressed as a rotation about a single axis by some angle. That is, for any $\mathbf{R} \in \SO(3)$, there exist a unit vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \in \Reals^3$ (the rotation axis) and an angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ such that the rotation is completely described by this pair $(\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}, \theta)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The rotation axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ is an eigenvector of $\mathbf{R}$ with eigenvalue 1:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R} \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}.
\end{equation}

Such an eigenvector exists because $\det(\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}) = 0$ for any rotation (a rotation always leaves one axis invariant). The angle $\theta$ can be recovered from $\text{trace}(\mathbf{R}) = 1 + 2\cos\theta$.
\end{proof}

The axis-angle representation is compact and intuitive but computationally less efficient than quaternions for repeated operations.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:ch4_summary}

This chapter has developed the complete mathematical framework for 3D rotations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rotation Matrices ($\SO(3)$):} We defined the Special Orthogonal Group as the set of $3 \times 3$ orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$, proved it is both a group and a 3-dimensional smooth manifold, and derived the three elementary rotations $\mathbf{R}_x(\theta), \mathbf{R}_y(\theta), \mathbf{R}_z(\theta)$.

    \item \textbf{Euler Angles and Gimbal Lock:} We presented the ZYX and ZXZ conventions and rigorously proved that the Jacobian relating angular velocities to Euler angle rates becomes singular at $\theta = \pm 90^\circ$, making Euler angles unsuitable for continuous control.

    \item \textbf{Lie Groups and Algebras:} We identified $\SO(3)$ as a Lie Group (smooth manifold + group structure) and its Lie algebra $\so(3)$ as the space of skew-symmetric matrices, which represent angular velocities.

    \item \textbf{Quaternion Algebra:} We introduced the quaternion representation of rotations, defined all quaternion operations (addition, Hamilton product, conjugate, norm, inverse), and proved that $\mathbf{q}$ and $-\mathbf{q}$ represent the same rotation (the double cover property).

    \item \textbf{Conversions:} We derived explicit formulas for converting between quaternions and rotation matrices in both directions.

    \item \textbf{SLERP:} We presented spherical linear interpolation as the natural way to smoothly interpolate between rotations.

    \item \textbf{Angular Velocity:} We connected the time-derivative of rotation matrices to angular velocity vectors, showing that $\dot{\mathbf{R}} = [\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times \mathbf{R}$ and that $\mathbf{R}^T \dot{\mathbf{R}} \in \so(3)$.

    \item \textbf{Euler's Theorem:} We stated and sketched the proof that every 3D rotation can be expressed as a rotation about a single axis by a single angle.
\end{itemize}

These tools eliminate the singularities of Euler angles while providing efficient computational representations. The next chapter combines rotation matrices with translation vectors into the full rigid body transformation group $\SE(3)$.

\section{Further Reading}
\label{sec:ch4_further_reading}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The rotation group $\SO(3)$ and its extension to rigid-body transformations
$\SE(3)$ are treated in depth by Murray, Li, and Sastry~\cite{Murray1994},
whose Chapter~2 develops the group-theoretic and differential-geometric
foundations that underpin this chapter. Lynch and Park~\cite{Lynch2017},
Chapters~3--4, provide a complementary modern treatment with numerous worked
examples of rotation matrices, homogeneous transformation matrices, and
their application to serial kinematic chains.

The Lie group structure of $\SO(3)$ and its Lie algebra $\so(3)$ are part of
a much larger mathematical framework. Hall~\cite{hall2015} offers a
self-contained introduction to Lie groups and Lie algebras accessible to
engineers with the linear algebra background developed in Chapter~1 of this
text. For the geometric and symplectic perspective on rotation groups and
their role in mechanics, Marsden and Ratiu~\cite{marsden1999} provide the
definitive reference.

The Rodrigues rotation formula derived in this chapter has a distinguished
history: it was first published by Olinde Rodrigues in
1840~\cite{rodrigues1840}, predating the modern matrix formalism by nearly a
century. Rodrigues' original derivation used purely geometric reasoning; the
matrix exponential form $e^{[\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}]_\times \theta}$
that we derive in Chapter~6 provides the modern algebraic counterpart.

Finally, readers implementing rotation representations in software should be
aware that quaternion arithmetic is the \emph{de facto} standard in modern
robotics and game-engine frameworks (e.g., Eigen, ROS~tf2, Unity) precisely
because it avoids the gimbal-lock singularity and requires only four
parameters with a single unit-norm constraint. The SLERP algorithm
presented in this chapter is the core interpolation primitive in all of
these libraries.

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:quaternion_lie_algebra}
Show that the set of pure quaternions $\{\mathbf{q} : q_w = 0\}$ forms a 3-dimensional vector space that is isomorphic to $\so(3)$. Under what operation does this isomorphism hold?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:matrix_exponential}
For small $t$, the matrix exponential provides $\mathbf{R}(t) = e^{t [\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times}$ where $\boldsymbol{\omega} = [0, 0, 1]^T$ (unit rotation about $z$). Expand the series $e^{t [\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times} = \mathbf{I} + t[\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times + \frac{t^2}{2}[\boldsymbol{\omega}]_\times^2 + \cdots$ and verify it matches $\mathbf{R}_z(t)$ to second order in $t$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:rodrigues_derivation}
Derive the Rodrigues rotation formula $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} + \sin\theta \, [\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}]_\times + (1 - \cos\theta) \, [\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}]_\times^2$ by expanding the rotation of an arbitrary vector about an axis, using the decomposition $\mathbf{v} = (\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \mathbf{v}) \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + \mathbf{v}_\perp$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:quaternion_interpolation_constant_speed}
Prove that SLERP produces constant-speed interpolation: the quaternion at parameter $t$ moves along the geodesic at constant angular velocity. Hint: compute the derivative $\frac{d}{dt}\text{SLERP}(\mathbf{q}_0, \mathbf{q}_1; t)$ and show its norm is constant.
\end{exercise}
