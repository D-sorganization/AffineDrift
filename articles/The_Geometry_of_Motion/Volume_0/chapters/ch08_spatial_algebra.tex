\begin{laymansbox}{Context \& Use Case: Unifying Translation and Rotation}{context_box}
\textbf{The Math You Will See:} We introduce \textbf{Spatial Vector Algebra}, defining the $6 \times 6$ \textbf{Spatial Cross Product} ($[\mathcal{V} \times]$), the \textbf{Spatial Inertia Matrix} ($\mathcal{I}$), and their role in transforming forces and accelerations across frames.

\textbf{Why We Need It:} In classical 3D mechanics, linear motion ($F = ma$) and angular motion ($\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\omega}$) are computed separately, leading to a tangled web of cross products and messy offsets when bodies are connected together. Spatial Algebra fuses them. A "Spatial Vector" describes pushing and turning simultaneously. By doing math in 6D, the complex gyroscopic and Coriolis forces of a multi-joint robot swinging through space are resolved with a single matrix multiplication.

\textbf{All Variables Defined:} $\mathcal{V}$ is a spatial velocity (Twist), $\mathcal{F}$ is a spatial force (Wrench), and $\mathcal{I}$ is the combined 6D mass/inertia tensor.
\end{laymansbox}

\section{Historical Context: From Plücker to Featherstone}
\label{sec:history_spatial_algebra}

The roots of spatial algebra reach deep into classical geometry. In the 1860s, \textbf{Julius Plücker} studied lines in 3D space as geometric objects, discovering that a line can be represented as a 6-dimensional vector satisfying certain constraints. This \textbf{Plücker coordinate} representation remained mostly a curiosity in algebraic geometry for over a century.

In 1900, Sir \textbf{Robert Stawell Ball} \cite{ball1900} developed the theory of ``screws''—geometric objects unifying rotation and translation—and showed that Plücker's coordinates naturally describe instantaneous screw motions. Ball's work connected to mechanics through the wrench (force + torque) dual to the twist (angular + linear velocity).

However, the true synthesis came when \textbf{Roy Featherstone}, in the 1980s and 1990s, recognized that Ball's geometric screws and Plücker's algebraic coordinates could be married with Lie algebra to create a unified computational framework for multi-body dynamics. Featherstone's \textbf{Spatial Vector Algebra} \cite{Featherstone2008} standardized the representation, showed how to compose inertias of linked chains (composite rigid body algorithm), and proved that careful use of spatial algebra enables $O(N)$ inverse dynamics.

Modern robotics and physics engines (MuJoCo \cite{todorov2012}, Drake, Pinocchio) are built entirely on Featherstone's spatial framework. The companion platform accompanying this textbook builds on the same foundation.

\section{Plücker Coordinates and Lines in 3D}
\label{sec:plucker}

Before we discuss spatial vectors as physical quantities, it helps to understand their geometric origin: the Plücker representation of lines.

\begin{definition}{Plücker Coordinates of a Line}{plucker_def}
A line $\ell$ in 3D can be represented as a 6-vector in \textbf{Plücker coordinates}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{d} \\ \mathbf{m} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{P}^5
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathbf{d}$ is the direction vector of the line (unit vector).
  \item $\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{d}$ is the moment (or Plücker moment), where $\mathbf{r}$ is any point on the line.
\end{itemize}

The Plücker coordinates satisfy the \textbf{Plücker relation}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{d}^T \mathbf{m} = 0
\end{equation}
This constraint encodes the fact that the direction and moment are related geometrically.

\textbf{Geometric Insight}: The direction $\mathbf{d}$ specifies the "orientation" of the line; the moment $\mathbf{m}$ encodes the line's location in space. Two lines are identical if and only if they have the same Plücker coordinates.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Connection to Spatial Vectors}

A spatial velocity (Twist) $\mathcal{V} = (\boldsymbol{\omega}, \mathbf{v})$ can be viewed as the instantaneous motion of a rigid body. The axis of this motion is a screw (a line with a pitch), and the Plücker coordinates of that screw are exactly $(\boldsymbol{\omega}, \mathbf{v})$ (up to scaling).

Similarly, a spatial force (Wrench) $\mathcal{F} = (\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{f})$ is the dual concept: it represents a force $\mathbf{f}$ applied at some point in space, creating a torque $\mathbf{m}$ about the origin. The Plücker relationship $\mathbf{f}^T \mathbf{m} = 0$ holds when the force and moment arise from a single applied force (they lie on the same screw axis).

\section{Spatial Vectors: Twists and Wrenches}
\label{sec:spatial_vectors}

In Chapter \ref{ch:lie_groups}, we introduced spatial vectors as elements of the Lie algebras $\se(3)$ and $\se^*(3)$. Here we formalize them as vectors and define their algebra.

\begin{definition}{Spatial Velocity (Twist)}{twist_def}
A spatial velocity or Twist is a 6D vector:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{V} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\omega} \\ \mathbf{v} \end{bmatrix} \in \se(3) \cong \Reals^6
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\boldsymbol{\omega} \in \Reals^3$ is the angular velocity.
  \item $\mathbf{v} \in \Reals^3$ is the linear velocity of the origin of the reference frame.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Physical Interpretation}: A point at position $\mathbf{r}$ relative to the origin has absolute velocity $\mathbf{v} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Spatial Force (Wrench)}{wrench_def}
A spatial force or Wrench is a 6D vector:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{m} \\ \mathbf{f} \end{bmatrix} \in \se^*(3) \cong \Reals^6
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathbf{m} \in \Reals^3$ is the moment (torque) about the origin.
  \item $\mathbf{f} \in \Reals^3$ is the linear force.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Physical Interpretation}: A force $\mathbf{f}$ applied at a point $\mathbf{r}$ creates a moment $\mathbf{m} + \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{f}$ about the origin.
\end{definition}

\section{The Spatial Cross Product}
\label{sec:spatial_cross}

In classical 3D mechanics, cross products appear everywhere. When a body rotates with angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$, the velocity of a point at offset $\mathbf{r}$ includes the term $\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}$. When a force $\mathbf{f}$ is applied at point $\mathbf{r}$, it creates a torque $\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{f}$.

Spatial Algebra extends the cross product to 6D, but because Twists and Wrenches live in dual spaces (with different transformation laws), there are actually \textit{two} distinct spatial cross products.

\subsection{Motion Cross Product}

The \textbf{motion cross product} acts on Twists and related vectors (velocities, accelerations). If a frame has spatial velocity $\mathcal{V} = (\boldsymbol{\omega}, \mathbf{v})$, a point with position offset $\mathbf{r}$ has velocity $\mathbf{v} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}$.

We represent this as a matrix operator:

\begin{definition}{Motion Cross Product Operator}{motion_cross_def}
For a spatial velocity Twist $\mathcal{V} = (\boldsymbol{\omega}, \mathbf{v})$, the motion cross product operator is:
\begin{equation}
    [\mathcal{V} \times] = \begin{bmatrix} [\boldsymbol{\omega}] & \mathbf{0}_{3 \times 3} \\ [\mathbf{v}] & [\boldsymbol{\omega}] \end{bmatrix} \in \Reals^{6 \times 6}
    \label{eq:motion_cross_matrix}
\end{equation}
where $[\boldsymbol{\omega}]$ and $[\mathbf{v}]$ are the standard $3 \times 3$ skew-symmetric cross product matrices.

\textbf{Action on a spatial velocity}: If $\mathcal{W} = (\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \mathbf{a})$ is another spatial quantity, then:
\begin{equation}
    [\mathcal{V} \times] \mathcal{W} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \boldsymbol{\alpha} \\ \mathbf{v} \times \boldsymbol{\alpha} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{a} \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:motion_cross_action}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Force Cross Product}

Wrenches are the dual to Twists. Because of this duality, when we apply the adjoint transformation to a wrench, it uses a different matrix. Correspondingly, the force cross product is defined differently:

\begin{definition}{Force Cross Product Operator}{force_cross_def}
The force cross product (coadjoint cross product) for a spatial velocity $\mathcal{V} = (\boldsymbol{\omega}, \mathbf{v})$ acting on Wrenches is:
\begin{equation}
    [\mathcal{V} \times^*] = -[\mathcal{V} \times]^T = \begin{bmatrix} [\boldsymbol{\omega}] & [\mathbf{v}] \\ \mathbf{0}_{3 \times 3} & [\boldsymbol{\omega}] \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:force_cross_matrix}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Action on a spatial force}: If $\mathcal{G} = (\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{f})$ is a Wrench, then:
\begin{equation}
    [\mathcal{V} \times^*] \mathcal{G} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{m} + \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{f} \\ \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{f} \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:force_cross_action}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Why Two Cross Products?}

The reason for the asymmetry is that power (work rate) must be invariant:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Power} = \mathcal{F}^T \mathcal{V}
\end{equation}

Under a coordinate frame change, if Twists transform via $[\text{Ad}_{\mathbf{T}}]$ and Wrenches transform via $[\text{Ad}_{\mathbf{T}}]^{-T}$ (the coadjoint), then the pairing $\mathcal{F}^T \mathcal{V}$ is preserved. The cross product operators must respect this dual structure.

\begin{principle}{Duality of Motion and Force Cross Products}{duality_principle}
The motion and force cross products are dual in the sense that:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}^T ([\mathcal{V} \times] \mathcal{W}) = ([\mathcal{V} \times^*] \mathcal{G})^T \mathcal{W}
\end{equation}
for any Twist $\mathcal{V}$, Twist-like vector $\mathcal{W}$, and Wrench $\mathcal{G}$. This ensures that the bilinear form (power) is preserved under transformations.
\end{principle}

\begin{principle}{$\text{Ad}$ vs.\ $\text{ad}$: Two Distinct Operators}{ad_vs_Ad}
The notation in spatial algebra distinguishes two fundamentally different operators that are easily confused:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $[\text{Ad}_{\mathbf{T}}] \in \Reals^{6 \times 6}$: The \textbf{Adjoint} of a rigid body transform $\mathbf{T} \in \SE(3)$. This is a coordinate transformation that re-expresses a spatial velocity (or acceleration) measured in one frame into another frame. It depends on a \emph{pose} (position and orientation).
  \item $[\text{ad}_{\mathcal{V}}] = [\mathcal{V} \times] \in \Reals^{6 \times 6}$: The \textbf{adjoint} (lowercase), also called the \emph{Lie bracket} or \emph{spatial motion cross product}. This maps a spatial velocity $\mathcal{V}$ to its cross-product operator, which computes Coriolis and centrifugal accelerations. It depends on a \emph{velocity}, not a pose.
\end{itemize}
In recursive dynamics algorithms such as the RNEA, both operators appear: $[\text{Ad}_{\mathbf{T}}]$ propagates velocities across frames (a coordinate change), while $[\text{ad}_{\mathcal{V}}] = [\mathcal{V} \times]$ computes the velocity-dependent acceleration terms (Coriolis). Confusing the two produces incorrect dynamics \cite{Featherstone2008, Murray1994}.
\end{principle}

\section{The Spatial Inertia Matrix}
\label{sec:spatial_inertia}

Just as scalar mass $m$ resists linear acceleration ($F = ma$), and a $3 \times 3$ inertia tensor $\mathbf{I}_c$ resists angular acceleration ($\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{I}_c \boldsymbol{\alpha}$), Spatial Algebra unifies them into a single $6 \times 6$ \textbf{Spatial Inertia Matrix} $\mathcal{I}$.

\subsection{Definition and Properties}

\begin{definition}{Spatial Inertia Matrix}{spatial_inertia_matrix_def}
For a rigid body with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Mass $m$,
  \item Center of mass (CoM) at position $\mathbf{c}$ relative to the reference frame origin,
  \item Rotational inertia tensor $\mathbf{I}_c$ (about the CoM),
\end{itemize}
the Spatial Inertia Matrix expressed at the frame origin is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I}_c - m[\mathbf{c}][\mathbf{c}] & m[\mathbf{c}] \\ -m[\mathbf{c}] & m\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3} \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:spatial_inertia_full}
\end{equation}

Using the identity $[\mathbf{c}][\mathbf{c}] = -\mathbf{c}\mathbf{c}^T + (\mathbf{c}^T\mathbf{c})\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3}$, this can be rewritten as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I}_c + m(\mathbf{c}\mathbf{c}^T - |\mathbf{c}|^2\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3}) & m[\mathbf{c}] \\ -m[\mathbf{c}] & m\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3} \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:spatial_inertia_alt}
\end{equation}

When the reference frame origin is \emph{at} the center of mass ($\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$), this simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_{\text{CoM}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I}_c & \mathbf{0}_{3 \times 3} \\ \mathbf{0}_{3 \times 3} & m\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3} \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:spatial_inertia_cocom}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Symmetric and Positive Definite}

\begin{theorem}{Spatial Inertia is SPD}{spatial_inertia_spd}
The Spatial Inertia Matrix $\mathcal{I}$ is symmetric and positive definite.

\textbf{Proof (Symmetry)}:
Write the spatial inertia in standard form:
\[
  \mathcal{I} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I}_c - m[\mathbf{c}][\mathbf{c}]^T & m[\mathbf{c}]^T \\ m[\mathbf{c}] & m\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3} \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Since $[\mathbf{c}]$ is skew-symmetric ($[\mathbf{c}]^T = -[\mathbf{c}]$), each block is symmetric or correctly transposed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Top-left block: $\mathbf{I}_c - m[\mathbf{c}][\mathbf{c}]^T$ is symmetric (both $\mathbf{I}_c$ and the product $[\mathbf{c}][\mathbf{c}]^T$ are symmetric).
  \item Off-diagonal blocks: The top-right block is $m[\mathbf{c}]^T = -m[\mathbf{c}]$, and the bottom-left block is $m[\mathbf{c}]$. Their transposes satisfy $(m[\mathbf{c}]^T)^T = m[\mathbf{c}]$, confirming $\mathcal{I}^T = \mathcal{I}$.
  \item Bottom-right block: $m\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3}$ is trivially symmetric.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Proof (Positive Definite)}:
For any non-zero spatial vector $\mathcal{V} = (\boldsymbol{\omega}, \mathbf{v})$, we compute:
\[
  \mathcal{V}^T \mathcal{I} \mathcal{V} = \boldsymbol{\omega}^T (\mathbf{I}_c - m[\mathbf{c}][\mathbf{c}]^T) \boldsymbol{\omega} + 2m\boldsymbol{\omega}^T[\mathbf{c}]^T\mathbf{v} + m\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v}
\]

This can be rewritten as the rotational kinetic energy plus the translational kinetic energy:
\[
  \mathcal{V}^T \mathcal{I} \mathcal{V} = \boldsymbol{\omega}^T \mathbf{I}_c \boldsymbol{\omega} + m|\mathbf{v} + [\mathbf{c}]\boldsymbol{\omega}|^2
\]

Since $\mathbf{I}_c$ is positive definite (non-zero $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ implies $\boldsymbol{\omega}^T \mathbf{I}_c \boldsymbol{\omega} > 0$), the entire expression is positive whenever $\mathcal{V} \neq 0$.
\end{theorem}

\section{The Parallel Axis Theorem in 6D}
\label{sec:parallel_axis_6d}

\subsection{Derivation from First Principles}

The classical parallel axis theorem states that if the inertia tensor about the center of mass is $\mathbf{I}_c$, then the inertia tensor about a point at distance $\mathbf{c}$ from the CoM is:
\[
  \mathbf{I}_{\text{origin}} = \mathbf{I}_c + m(c^2 \mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3} - \mathbf{c}\mathbf{c}^T)
\]

In spatial form, the parallel axis theorem extends to both translational and rotational terms. If we define:
\[
  \mathcal{I}_0 = \text{Spatial inertia at origin}
\]
\[
  \mathcal{I}_c = \text{Spatial inertia at CoM}
\]

then the relationship is:

\begin{theorem}{Parallel Axis Theorem in 6D}{parallel_axis_6d}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R} & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{R} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I}_c & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} & m\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R}^T & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{R}^T \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -m[\mathbf{c}][\mathbf{c}]^T & m[\mathbf{c}]^T \\ m[\mathbf{c}] & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:parallel_axis_6d}
\end{equation}

When $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$ (same orientation):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I}_c - m[\mathbf{c}][\mathbf{c}]^T & m[\mathbf{c}]^T \\ m[\mathbf{c}] & m\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3} \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Interpretation}: The top-left block contains the parallel axis correction to rotational inertia; the off-diagonals encode the coupling between angular and linear motion due to the offset CoM.
\end{theorem}

\section{Composite Rigid Body Algorithm}
\label{sec:composite_rigid_body}

In Chapter 7, we computed inverse dynamics using the RNEA. An alternative and equally important algorithm is the \textbf{Composite Rigid Body Algorithm (CRB)}, which builds up the spatial inertia of linked chains recursively.

\subsection{Concept: Bottom-Up Inertia Assembly}

Instead of computing torques directly, the CRB first assembles the inertia of composite structures: e.g., the forearm link is rigidly attached to the hand, so the hand's inertia is added to the forearm's. By recursively building composite inertias from tips to base, we can pre-compute the total mass matrix $\mathbf{M}(q)$ in $O(N)$ time.

\begin{algorithm}{Composite Rigid Body Algorithm}{alg_crb}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=pseudocode]
function CompositeRigidBody(links, joints, lambda):
    // Input: link inertias, joint structure
    // Output: composite inertias I_c for each link

    I_c = [None] * (N+1)         // Will store composite inertia of link i and all descendants

    for i <-N down to 1:         // Traverse from tips to base
        I_c[i] <-I[i]            // Start with link i's own inertia

        for j in children[i]:
            // Add inertia of child j to link i
            T_j_to_i <-relative transform from j to i
            Ad_j_to_i <-Adjoint of T_j_to_i
            I_c[i] <-I_c[i] + Ad_j_to_i^T * I_c[j] * Ad_j_to_i

    return I_c
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Use in Computing the Mass Matrix}

Once the composite inertias are assembled, the mass matrix can be extracted via:
\[
  \mathbf{M}_{ij}(q) = \mathcal{S}_i^T [\text{Ad}_{\mathbf{T}_{i,j}}]^{-T} \mathcal{I}_{c,j}(q) [\text{Ad}_{\mathbf{T}_{i,j}}]^{-1} \mathcal{S}_j
\]

where $\mathcal{I}_{c,j}$ is the composite inertia of link $j$ and all its descendants.

\begin{theorem}{CRB Complexity}{crb_complexity}
The Composite Rigid Body Algorithm computes the mass matrix $\mathbf{M}(q)$ in $O(N^2)$ time (because there are $N^2$ entries), but the inertia assembly itself (bottom-up recursion) is $O(N)$. Once assembled, forward-dynamics calculations (solving for accelerations given forces) are $O(N)$ via matrix multiplication.
\end{theorem}

\section{Spatial Algebra for Planar Systems}
\label{sec:planar_spatial}

For systems constrained to a plane (e.g., 2D manipulation, golf swings), we can work with reduced spatial representations.

\subsection{3D Vectors for 2D Motion}

A planar motion has:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Angular velocity $\omega$ (a scalar, rotation about the $z$-axis).
  \item Linear velocity $\mathbf{v} = (v_x, v_y) \in \Reals^2$.
\end{itemize}

We can embed this in a 3D twist:
\[
  \mathcal{V}_{\text{planar}} = (\omega, v_x, v_y)^T \in \Reals^3
\]

The motion cross product for planar motion simplifies:
\[
  [\mathcal{V} \times]_{\text{planar}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -v_y & v_x \\ v_y & 0 & \omega \\ -v_x & -\omega & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\]

A planar spatial inertia matrix is $3 \times 3$:
\[
  \mathcal{I}_{\text{planar}} = \begin{bmatrix} I_z & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & m & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & m \end{bmatrix}
\]
when the CoM is at the origin. With offset $\mathbf{c} = (c_x, c_y)$:
\[
  \mathcal{I}_{\text{planar}} = \begin{bmatrix} I_z + m(c_x^2 + c_y^2) & mc_y & -mc_x \\ mc_y & m & 0 \\ -mc_x & 0 & m \end{bmatrix}
\]

\subsection{Computational Advantage}

Working in 3D (instead of 6D) reduces memory and computation by a factor of 4, making planar mechanisms extremely fast to simulate.

\section{Newton-Euler Equations in Spatial Form}
\label{sec:spatial_newton_euler_full}

The fundamental equations of dynamics in spatial form are elegant and reveal the unity of linear and angular motion.

\subsection{Spatial Momentum and the Equation of Motion}

The spatial momentum of a rigid body with spatial inertia $\mathcal{I}$ and spatial velocity $\mathcal{V}$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P} = \mathcal{I} \mathcal{V}
    \label{eq:spatial_momentum}
\end{equation}

The equation of motion (Newton-Euler in spatial form) states that the time derivative of spatial momentum equals the applied wrench:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\mathcal{P}}{dt} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{ext}}
    \label{eq:spatial_newton_euler_basic}
\end{equation}

However, because $\mathcal{I}$ itself may be time-varying (when expressed in a rotating frame), the full equation includes a bias term:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}(\mathcal{I} \mathcal{V}) = \mathcal{I} \dot{\mathcal{V}} + \dot{\mathcal{I}} \mathcal{V}
    \label{eq:spatial_momentum_full}
\end{equation}

In a \emph{body-fixed frame} (rotating with the body), $\mathcal{I}$ is constant, but velocities are measured in the rotating frame, introducing the bias term $-[\mathcal{V} \times]^* (\mathcal{I} \mathcal{V})$:

\begin{theorem}{Spatial Newton-Euler Equation}{spatial_ne_theorem}
For a rigid body in a body-fixed frame, the equation of motion is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{I} \dot{\mathcal{V}} - [\mathcal{V} \times]^* (\mathcal{I} \mathcal{V})
    \label{eq:spatial_newton_euler_final}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Interpretation}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item First term: the inertial wrench (like $F = ma$ in 6D).
  \item Second term: the "bias" or "gyroscopic" wrench, capturing Coriolis and centrifugal effects.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Derivation}: In an inertial frame:
\[
  \mathcal{F}_{\text{inertial}} = \frac{d}{dt}(\mathcal{I}_{\text{inertial}} \mathcal{V}_{\text{inertial}})
\]

Transforming to a body-fixed frame using the adjoint, the time derivative picks up the Lie bracket (commutator) of spatial velocities, which manifests as $[\mathcal{V} \times]^*$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Connection to Dual Quaternions}
\label{sec:dual_quaternions}

\subsection{Brief Overview}

Dual quaternions extend quaternions (Chapter \ref{ch:quaternions}) by using dual numbers instead of real numbers. A dual quaternion is:
\[
  \mathbf{q}_d = q_0 + q_1 \mathbf{i} + q_2 \mathbf{j} + q_3 \mathbf{k} + \epsilon(q_4 + q_5 \mathbf{i} + q_6 \mathbf{j} + q_7 \mathbf{k})
\]
where $\epsilon$ is the dual unit satisfying $\epsilon^2 = 0$ (and $\epsilon \neq 0$).

Dual quaternions are isomorphic to spatial vectors and can represent both rotation and translation in a single object. Some robotics frameworks use dual quaternions for inertia and screw calculations, though the $6 \times 6$ matrix representation is more standard for computation.

\textbf{Key property}: The spatial inertia can be represented as a dual-quaternion-valued linear form, and Featherstone's spatial algorithms can be rewritten using dual quaternions, often with elegant geometric interpretations.

For this course, we continue with the matrix representation, as it is more straightforward for algorithmic implementation.

\section{Worked Example: Spatial Inertia of a Multi-Link System}
\label{sec:worked_example_multi_inertia}

Consider a two-link arm where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Link 1 (upper arm): mass $m_1 = 2$ kg, CoM at $(0.3, 0, 0)$ m from joint 1, rotational inertia $\mathbf{I}_c^{(1)} = \text{diag}(0.05, 0.05, 0.01)$ kg$\cdot$m$^2$.
  \item Link 2 (forearm): mass $m_2 = 1$ kg, CoM at $(0.25, 0, 0)$ m from joint 2, rotational inertia $\mathbf{I}_c^{(2)} = \text{diag}(0.02, 0.02, 0.005)$ kg$\cdot$m$^2$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Building Link Spatial Inertias}

For link 1, with CoM offset $\mathbf{c}_1 = (0.3, 0, 0)^T$:
\[
  [\mathbf{c}_1] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.3 \\ 0 & 0.3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\]

\[
  [\mathbf{c}_1][\mathbf{c}_1]^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.09 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.09 \end{bmatrix}
\]

\[
  \mathcal{I}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I}_c^{(1)} - m_1[\mathbf{c}_1][\mathbf{c}_1]^T & m_1[\mathbf{c}_1]^T \\ m_1[\mathbf{c}_1] & m_1\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3} \end{bmatrix}
\]

\[
  \mathcal{I}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.05-2(0.09) & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.05-2(0) & 0 & 0 & 0 & -0.6 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.01-2(0) & 0 & 0.6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.6 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -0.6 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
\]

\[
  \mathcal{I}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.13 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.05 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -0.6 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.01 & 0 & 0.6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.6 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -0.6 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
\]

(The negative value in the top-left reflects that the CoM is offset by more than the gyradius; this is still valid.)

Similarly, $\mathcal{I}^{(2)}$ is computed with $\mathbf{c}_2 = (0.25, 0, 0)$ and $m_2 = 1$ kg.

\subsection{Composite Inertia}

If link 2 is rigidly attached to link 1 (e.g., they are the same rigid body or perfectly welded), the composite inertia is:
\[
  \mathcal{I}_{\text{composite}} = \mathcal{I}^{(1)} + \mathcal{I}^{(2)}
\]
This is simple summation when expressed in the same reference frame.

If the links are separated by a relative transform $\mathbf{T}_{1 \to 2}$, we must first transform $\mathcal{I}^{(2)}$ to the frame of link 1:
\[
  \mathcal{I}_{\text{composite}} = \mathcal{I}^{(1)} + [\text{Ad}_{\mathbf{T}_{1 \to 2}}]^{-T} \mathcal{I}^{(2)} [\text{Ad}_{\mathbf{T}_{1 \to 2}}]^{-1}
\]

\section{Physical Interpretation of Spatial Inertia Elements}
\label{sec:inertia_interpretation}

The $6 \times 6$ spatial inertia matrix has a clear physical meaning for each block:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Top-left} ($3 \times 3$): Rotational inertia (analogous to the $3 \times 3$ inertia tensor). It relates angular acceleration to the torque required.
  \item \textbf{Top-right} ($3 \times 3$): Coupling between linear and rotational motion due to CoM offset. If the CoM is not at the origin, accelerating the origin in one direction produces a torque around it.
  \item \textbf{Bottom-left} ($3 \times 3$): Transpose of top-right (due to symmetry).
  \item \textbf{Bottom-right} ($3 \times 3$): Mass matrix ($m\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3}$). It relates linear acceleration of the origin to the force required.
\end{itemize}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Kinetic Energy and the Origin of Spatial Inertia}
\label{sec:kinetic_energy_spatial_inertia}
\index{kinetic energy!spatial form}
\index{spatial inertia!kinetic energy derivation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The $6 \times 6$ spatial inertia matrix is not an arbitrary packaging of
mass properties.  It is the \emph{unique} object that makes kinetic
energy a quadratic form in spatial velocity.  We now prove this from
first principles.

\textbf{Classical kinetic energy.}
For a rigid body with mass $m$, centre-of-mass velocity
$\mathbf{v}_{cm}$, angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$, and
rotational inertia $\mathbf{I}_c$ about the centre of mass:
\[
  T = \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\norm{\mathbf{v}_{cm}}^2
    + \frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}^T \mathbf{I}_c\,\boldsymbol{\omega}.
\]

\textbf{Re-express about a body-fixed frame origin.}
Let the body frame origin $O$ be offset from the centre of mass by
$\mathbf{c} \in \Reals^3$.  Then the centre-of-mass velocity is
\[
  \mathbf{v}_{cm}
  = \mathbf{v}_O + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{c},
\]
where $\mathbf{v}_O$ is the linear velocity of $O$.  Substituting into
$T$ and expanding:
\begin{align*}
  T &= \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\norm{\mathbf{v}_O + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{c}}^2
     + \frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}^T \mathbf{I}_c\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \\
    &= \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\mathbf{v}_O^T \mathbf{v}_O
     + m\,\mathbf{v}_O^T (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{c})
     + \frac{1}{2}\,m\,(\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{c})^T
       (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{c})
     + \frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}^T \mathbf{I}_c\,\boldsymbol{\omega}.
\end{align*}

Using the skew-symmetric matrix $[\mathbf{c}]_\times$ so that
$\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{c} = [\mathbf{c}]_\times^T \boldsymbol{\omega}$,
and collecting the angular and linear components into the spatial
velocity $\mathcal{V} = (\boldsymbol{\omega},\, \mathbf{v}_O)^T$, we
obtain:

\begin{theorem}{Kinetic Energy as a Spatial Quadratic Form}{ke_spatial_quadratic}
\index{spatial inertia!quadratic form}
The kinetic energy of a rigid body equals
\[
  T = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{V}^T \mathcal{I}\, \mathcal{V},
\]
where the $6 \times 6$ \textbf{spatial inertia matrix}\index{spatial inertia} is
\[
  \mathcal{I}
  = \begin{pmatrix}
      \mathbf{I}_c + m\,[\mathbf{c}]_\times [\mathbf{c}]_\times^T
        & m\,[\mathbf{c}]_\times \\[4pt]
      m\,[\mathbf{c}]_\times^T
        & m\,\mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3}
    \end{pmatrix}.
\]
\end{theorem}

This derivation proves that $\mathcal{I}$ is the \emph{correct} inertial
object for spatial dynamics: it is determined entirely by the requirement
that kinetic energy be a quadratic form in $\mathcal{V}$.

The \textbf{off-diagonal blocks} $m[\mathbf{c}]_\times$ encode the
cross-coupling between rotation and translation that arises whenever the
centre of mass does not coincide with the frame origin ($\mathbf{c} \neq
\mathbf{0}$).  When the frame is placed at the centre of mass
($\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$), these blocks vanish and the spatial inertia
becomes block-diagonal---rotational and translational energies
decouple~\cite{Featherstone2008}.

\section{Python Implementation}
\label{sec:python_spatial_algebra}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Spatial Algebra and Inertia Computation in Python]
import numpy as np

def skew(v):
    """Convert 3D vector to skew-symmetric matrix."""
    return np.array([
        [0, -v[2], v[1]],
        [v[2], 0, -v[0]],
        [-v[1], v[0], 0]
    ])

def motion_cross(V):
    """
    Spatial cross product for motion (Twists).
    V: 6D spatial velocity (omega, v)
    Returns: 6x6 operator [Vx]
    """
    omega = V[:3]
    v = V[3:]
    Vx = np.zeros((6, 6))
    Vx[:3, :3] = skew(omega)
    Vx[3:, :3] = skew(v)
    Vx[3:, 3:] = skew(omega)
    return Vx

def force_cross(V):
    """
    Spatial cross product for forces (Wrenches).
    Returns: 6x6 operator [Vx*] = -[Vx]^T
    """
    return -motion_cross(V).T

def spatial_inertia(m, c, I_c):
    """
    Construct 6x6 spatial inertia matrix.
    Args:
        m: scalar mass
        c: 3D center of mass offset from origin
        I_c: 3x3 rotational inertia about CoM
    Returns:
        I: 6x6 spatial inertia matrix
    """
    I_spatial = np.zeros((6, 6))
    c_skew = skew(c)

    # Top-left: I_c - m[c][c]^T (parallel axis theorem)
    I_spatial[:3, :3] = I_c - m * (c_skew @ c_skew.T)

    # Top-right: m[c]^T
    I_spatial[:3, 3:] = m * c_skew.T

    # Bottom-left: m[c]
    I_spatial[3:, :3] = m * c_skew

    # Bottom-right: m * I_3
    I_spatial[3:, 3:] = m * np.eye(3)

    return I_spatial

def transform_inertia(I, T):
    """
    Transform spatial inertia from one frame to another.
    I: 6x6 spatial inertia in source frame
    T: 4x4 transformation matrix (to target frame)
    Returns: transformed 6x6 spatial inertia
    """
    R = T[:3, :3]
    p = T[:3, 3]

    # Construct transformation for inertia
    # I' = [Ad_T]^-T I [Ad_T]^-1
    Ad = np.zeros((6, 6))
    Ad[:3, :3] = R
    Ad[3:, :3] = skew(p) @ R
    Ad[3:, 3:] = R

    Ad_inv_T = np.linalg.inv(Ad).T
    return Ad_inv_T @ I @ np.linalg.inv(Ad)

def composite_rigid_body(link_inertias, link_transforms, parent_array):
    """
    Assemble composite rigid body inertias.
    Args:
        link_inertias: list of 6x6 inertia matrices
        link_transforms: list of 4x4 transforms to parent frames
        parent_array: parent[i] = index of parent of link i (or -1 for root)
    Returns:
        composite_inertias: list of 6x6 composite inertias
    """
    N = len(link_inertias)
    composite_inertias = [np.copy(I) for I in link_inertias]

    # Process from leaves to root (reverse order)
    for i in range(N - 1, -1, -1):
        parent = parent_array[i]
        if parent >= 0:
            # Transform this link's composite inertia to parent frame
            I_in_parent = transform_inertia(
                composite_inertias[i],
                np.linalg.inv(link_transforms[i])
            )
            # Add to parent's inertia
            composite_inertias[parent] += I_in_parent

    return composite_inertias

# Example: Two-link arm
if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Link 1: upper arm
    m1 = 2.0
    c1 = np.array([0.3, 0.0, 0.0])

    # Link 2: forearm

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch8_summary}

In this chapter, we developed Spatial Vector Algebra—the mathematical language for unified 6D mechanics:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Historical Context}: Plücker's 19th-century line geometry, Ball's screws, and Featherstone's modern synthesis created a computationally elegant framework.

  \item \textbf{Plücker Coordinates}: Lines in 3D are represented as 6D vectors satisfying a constraint, naturally connecting to spatial twists and wrenches.

  \item \textbf{Spatial Vectors}: Twists (velocity) and Wrenches (force) are the fundamental 6D objects, living in dual spaces $\se(3)$ and $\se^*(3)$.

  \item \textbf{Motion and Force Cross Products}: Two distinct $6 \times 6$ cross product operators capture the geometry of relative motion and force application; they are dual to preserve power.

  \item \textbf{Spatial Inertia}: A single symmetric, positive-definite $6 \times 6$ matrix unifies mass and rotational inertia, including the parallel axis theorem as a block structure.

  \item \textbf{Parallel Axis Theorem in 6D}: The full theorem includes coupling terms between angular and linear motion due to CoM offset.

  \item \textbf{Composite Rigid Body Algorithm}: Inertias of linked chains are assembled bottom-up in $O(N)$ time via adjoint transformations.

  \item \textbf{Planar Reduction}: 2D systems use 3D spatial algebra, reducing computation by a factor of 4.

  \item \textbf{Newton-Euler in Spatial Form}: A single matrix equation $\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{I}\dot{\mathcal{V}} - [\mathcal{V} \times]^*(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{V})$ unifies linear and angular dynamics.

  \item \textbf{Dual Quaternions}: Brief connection to an alternative 8D representation with elegant geometric properties.

  \item \textbf{Physical Interpretation}: Each block of the spatial inertia matrix has clear mechanical meaning.

  \item \textbf{Python Implementation}: Full code for inertia computation, transformation, and composite body assembly.
\end{enumerate}

With Spatial Vector Algebra in hand, the Recursive Newton-Euler Algorithm of Chapter \ref{ch:recursive_algorithms} becomes a straightforward loop of matrix multiplications, without a single transcendental function or explicit cross product in sight. This unity—where complex nonlinear dynamics reduce to structured linear algebra—is the deep beauty of modern computational mechanics.

\section{Further Reading}
\label{sec:further_reading_ch8}

The primary reference for spatial vector algebra is Featherstone \cite{Featherstone2008}, whose monograph develops the full $6 \times 6$ framework from first principles and demonstrates its application to recursive multi-body dynamics algorithms. Featherstone's treatment of spatial inertia, the motion and force cross products, and the composite rigid body algorithm is definitive, and readers seeking to implement spatial algebra in production code should consult his detailed pseudocode and numerical recipes.

The mathematical foundations connecting spatial vectors to the Lie algebra $\se(3)$ and its dual $\se^*(3)$ are developed rigorously by Murray, Li, and Sastry \cite{Murray1994}. Their perspective clarifies why twists and wrenches live in dual vector spaces and why the adjoint and coadjoint representations have the specific transformation properties used throughout this chapter.

For the classical treatment of rigid body dynamics, inertia tensors, and the parallel axis theorem in their traditional $3 \times 3$ form, Goldstein, Poole, and Safko \cite{Goldstein2002} remains the standard graduate-level reference. Comparing their development with the $6 \times 6$ spatial inertia formulation of this chapter illuminates how spatial algebra unifies and streamlines results that classically require separate angular and translational analyses.

Marsden and Ratiu \cite{marsden1999} place spatial algebra within the broader framework of geometric mechanics, connecting spatial inertia to momentum maps and showing how the Euler equations for rigid body motion arise naturally from reduction on $\SE(3)$. Their treatment is more abstract but reveals the deep geometric structures underlying the computational framework.

It is worth emphasizing that spatial algebra is the computational backbone of modern physics engines. MuJoCo \cite{todorov2012}, in particular, implements spatial vector arithmetic as its core computational primitive, using the $6 \times 6$ framework to achieve real-time simulation of complex contact-rich robotic systems. Understanding the material in this chapter is therefore essential not only for theoretical mechanics but for practical work with any modern simulation platform.
