\epigraph{%

\section{Introduction: The Physics Abstraction}

\begin{laymansbox}{Context \& Use Case: Why We Banish 3D Physics}{context_box}
\textbf{The Math You Will See:} We will build the \textbf{State Vector} ($\mathbf{x}$), a vertical stack of numbers containing positions ($\mathbf{q}$) and velocities ($\dot{\mathbf{q}}$). We will formulate the universal control equation $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}, t)$, and its linear cousin $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$.

\textbf{Why We Need It:} When a robotic arm swings a bat, the shoulder, elbow, and wrist all move in weird, overlapping arcs in 3D physics space ($X, Y, Z$). This is impossibly hard to calculate directly. Instead, we map the entire robot into an abstract mathematical realm called "State Space". In State Space, the complex multi-joint swinging robot is represented by a \emph{single, infinitesimally small dot} moving along a single curved line. This abstraction makes solving incredibly complex control problems possible.

\textbf{All Variables Defined:} $\mathbf{x}$ is the state, $\mathbf{u}$ is the control input (e.g., motor torque), and $f$ is the system dynamics mapping. See the \textbf{Nomenclature} at the start of the book for a full reference.
\end{laymansbox}

When a layman pictures a robotic arm throwing a ball or a drone correcting its pitch in a crosswind, they visualize physical bodies acting in 3-dimensional Euclidean space.

But control theorists and kinematic engineers almost never look at the physical space. They work in a wholly abstract, higher-dimensional mathematical realm known as \textbf{State Space}\index{State Space}. State space is arguably the most fundamental translation required in control theory. If you do not understand state space, nothing else in the subsequent volumes of \textit{The Geometry of Motion} will make sense.

\section{Historical Context: The Kalman Revolution}
\label{sec:kalman_history}

\begin{laymansbox}{Historical Context: From Transfer Functions to State Space}{history_box}
In the 1950s, control engineers exclusively used \textbf{transfer functions}\index{Transfer Function}, which relate outputs to inputs in the frequency domain via Laplace transforms. Transfer functions are elegant for single-input single-output (SISO) systems and for analyzing stability via Nyquist diagrams and Bode plots. However, they fail catastrophically for multi-input multi-output (MIMO) systems and provide no insight into what is happening \emph{inside} the system.

In 1960, \textbf{Rudolf E. Kalman} published his seminal paper introducing state-space representations and the foundational concepts of \textbf{controllability}\index{Controllability} and \textbf{observability}\index{Observability}. This was revolutionary: it enabled the design of optimal controllers for complex systems, the treatment of MIMO systems, and the development of the Kalman filter—one of the most important algorithms in engineering. The state-space framework was so powerful that it became the universal language of control theory.
\end{laymansbox}

The fundamental advantage of state-space methods over transfer functions is \emph{transparency}. A transfer function $G(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)}$ tells you how the input drives the output, but it hides the internal structure. A state-space model $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$ exposes the entire flow of energy and information through the system. This transparency makes it possible to:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Design controllers that regulate internal state, not just final outputs
  \item Detect when a system is uncontrollable or unobservable
  \item Handle systems with multiple inputs and multiple outputs naturally
  \item Compute optimal control laws analytically
  \item Estimate hidden state variables from measurements (Kalman filter)
\end{itemize}

The rest of this chapter builds the state-space framework from first principles. By the end, you will understand why Kalman's insight was revolutionary and why state space is now the only way modern control engineers think.

\section{Constructing a State Vector}
\label{sec:state_vector}

Let us build a state space from the ground up using the simplest dynamical system possible: a 1-dimensional point mass (like a train on a straight track).

We want to predict what the train will do. To do this, we need to know its position, $q$. Is $q$ the "state" of the system?

No. If we only know that the train is at $q = 100$ meters, we cannot predict where it will be 1 second from now. It could be moving right, moving left, or stationary.

Therefore, to complete our understanding, we must also know its velocity, $\dot{q}$ (the time-derivative of position). If we know $(q, \dot{q})$, we know its exact location and its exact speed.

Do we need to know acceleration ($\ddot{q}$) as part of the state? According to Newton's Second Law: $F = m\ddot{q}$. Acceleration is not an independent property of the train; it is completely driven by the external forces applied at that instant. Therefore, if we know the forces (the Control Inputs, $\mathbf{u}$), we can immediately compute the acceleration. The state itself is fully defined by purely the position and velocity.

\begin{definition}{The State Vector $\mathbf{x}$}{statevector}
  The state vector $\mathbf{x}$ is the minimum set of mathematical variables completely describing the condition of a dynamic system at a specific point in time.

  For our train, it is a 2-dimensional column vector:
  \begin{equation}
      \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} q \\ \dot{q} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \text{position} \\ \text{velocity} \end{bmatrix}
  \end{equation}

  For a general system with $n$ degrees of freedom, the state vector lives in $n$-dimensional state space: $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X} \subset \Reals^n$.
\end{definition}

If you take a photograph of a system, you capture its position $\mathbf{q}$. But a state vector $\mathbf{x}$ is the "ultimate photograph"---it captures the position \emph{and} the velocity. With $\mathbf{x}$ and the laws of physics, the entire future of the system is deterministic.

\subsection{Worked Example 1: Simple Pendulum}
\label{ex:pendulum}

\begin{example}{State Vector for a Simple Pendulum}{pendulum_state}
A pendulum of length $L$ and mass $m$ is suspended from a pivot. Let $\theta$ denote the angle from vertical, and $\tau$ denote an applied torque at the pivot.

The equation of motion is:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pendulum_eom}
  m L^2 \ddot{\theta} + mg L \sin(\theta) = \tau
\end{equation}

To predict the pendulum's future, what do we need?
\begin{itemize}
  \item The angle $\theta$ (where is it pointing?)
  \item The angular velocity $\dot{\theta}$ (is it spinning?)
  \item The torque $\tau$ is the control input (provided at the moment we want to predict)
\end{itemize}

Thus the state vector is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \theta \\ \dot{\theta} \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

This is a 2-dimensional state space, even though the pendulum physically swings in a 1-dimensional arc in our visual space.
\end{example}

\subsection{Worked Example 2: DC Motor}
\label{ex:dc_motor}

\begin{example}{State Vector for a DC Motor}{dcmotor_state}
A DC motor is driven by input voltage $V_{\text{in}}$. Let $\theta$ be the rotor angle, and let $i$ be the current flowing through the motor windings.

The governing equations are:
\begin{align}
  L \frac{\dd i}{\dd t} &= V_{\text{in}} - R i - K_e \dot{\theta} \label{eq:motor_voltage} \\
  J \frac{\dd \dot{\theta}}{\dd t} &= K_t i - b \dot{\theta} \label{eq:motor_torque}
\end{align}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $L$ is the motor inductance, $R$ is resistance
  \item $K_e$ is the back-EMF constant, $K_t$ is the torque constant
  \item $J$ is the rotor inertia, $b$ is viscous friction
\end{itemize}

The state vector must include all variables whose time-derivatives depend on the inputs. These are:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} i \\ \theta \\ \dot{\theta} \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

This is a 3-dimensional state space. Notice we include $\theta$ (rotor position) even though it does not directly appear in the governing equations—we need it to fully specify the motor's configuration, and its time-derivative $\dot{\theta}$ does appear.
\end{example}

\subsection{Worked Example 3: Two-Tank Fluid System}
\label{ex:two_tank}

\begin{example}{State Vector for Two Coupled Tanks}{twotank_state}
Two tanks, each with cross-sectional area $A$, are connected by a valve. Let $h_1$ and $h_2$ be the fluid levels. Flow into tank 1 is $Q_{\text{in}}$, and flow between tanks is proportional to the height difference:
\begin{equation}
  Q_{1 \to 2} = C \sqrt{|h_1 - h_2|}
\end{equation}

where $C$ is a flow coefficient, and outflow from tank 2 is:
\begin{equation}
  Q_{\text{out}} = C_{\text{out}} \sqrt{h_2}
\end{equation}

The height rates of change are:
\begin{align}
  A \frac{\dd h_1}{\dd t} &= Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{1 \to 2} \\
  A \frac{\dd h_2}{\dd t} &= Q_{1 \to 2} - Q_{\text{out}}
\end{align}

To predict the system, we need the current heights of both tanks. The state vector is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} h_1 \\ h_2 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

This is a 2-dimensional state space. The control input is the inflow rate $Q_{\text{in}}$.
\end{example}

\section{Converting \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}-th Order ODEs to State Form}
\label{sec:ode_conversion}

\begin{theorem}{Conversion of $n$-th Order ODE to First-Order State Form}{nth_order_conversion}
Any $n$-th order differential equation of the form
\begin{equation}
  y^{(n)} + a_{n-1} y^{(n-1)} + \cdots + a_1 \dot{y} + a_0 y = u(t)
\end{equation}
can be converted to a system of $n$ first-order ODEs via state-vector substitution.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Define the state variables as consecutive derivatives of $y$:
\begin{align}
  x_1 &= y \\
  x_2 &= \dot{y} \\
  x_3 &= \ddot{y} \\
  &\vdots \\
  x_n &= y^{(n-1)}
\end{align}

Then:
\begin{align}
  \dot{x}_1 &= x_2 \\
  \dot{x}_2 &= x_3 \\
  &\vdots \\
  \dot{x}_{n-1} &= x_n \\
  \dot{x}_n &= u(t) - a_0 x_1 - a_1 x_2 - \cdots - a_{n-1} x_n
\end{align}

where the final equation is obtained by solving the original ODE for $y^{(n)} = \dot{x}_n$:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{x}_n = u(t) - a_0 x_1 - a_1 x_2 - \cdots - a_{n-1} x_n
\end{equation}

In matrix form, this is $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}u$ where $\mathbf{A}$ is the companion matrix:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \\ -a_0 & -a_1 & -a_2 & \cdots & -a_{n-1} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

This representation is unique up to change of state variables (i.e., state transformation).
\end{proof}

\section{State Space Representations}
\label{sec:state_space_rep}

Because the state vector $\mathbf{x}$ contains $n$ variables, it naturally lives in an $n$-dimensional geometry: the State Space, denoted as $\mathcal{X} \subset \Reals^n$.

An astonishing realization for beginners is that when a dynamic system (like an airplane or a robotic arm) moves through physical space over time, its entire sprawling motion is represented as a single, unified \textbf{1-dimensional curve} snaking through $n$-dimensional state space. This curve is called the system's \textbf{Trajectory}\index{Trajectory} $\mathcal{T}$.

\subsection{Standard Form Differential Equations}

The immense power of state space is that it allows us to convert complex, higher-order physical differential equations into simple, first-order vector math.

Consider a simple mass-spring-damper system governed by a second-order differential equation:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mass_spring_damper}
  m\ddot{q} + c\dot{q} + kq = u
\end{equation}
where $q$ is position, $m$ is mass, $c$ is damping, $k$ is stiffness, and $u$ is our control force (e.g., a motor pushing the mass).

By defining our state vector as $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2]^T = [q, \dot{q}]^T$, we can rewrite this physical equation as purely mathematical first-order derivatives. First, isolate the highest derivative ($\ddot{q}$):
\begin{equation}
  \ddot{q} = \frac{1}{m}(u - c\dot{q} - kq)
\end{equation}
Now, substitute the state variables ($x_1 = q, x_2 = \dot{q}$):
\begin{align}
  \dot{x}_1 &= x_2 \\
  \dot{x}_2 &= \frac{1}{m}(u - c x_2 - k x_1)
\end{align}

This is called the \textbf{State Space Representation}\index{State Space Representation}. It is universally written in nonlinear control literature as:

\begin{principle}{The Fundamental Equation of Control Theory}{fund_eq}
  Every autonomous control system can be written as a first-order vector differential equation mapping the current state and current control commands to the velocity of the state vector:
  \begin{equation}
      \dot{\mathbf{x}} = f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}, t)
  \end{equation}
  This vector field $f$ dictates how the system "flows" through the abstract geometry of the state space.
\end{principle}

\subsection{Linear State Space: The A and B Matrices}
\label{sec:linear_state_space}

\index{Linear State Space}\index{A Matrix}\index{B Matrix}

When the system is perfectly linear (like the mass-spring-damper above), the function $f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}, t)$ can be split into two matrices: the Dynamics Matrix ($\mathbf{A}$) and the Input Matrix ($\mathbf{B}$).
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:linear_state_space}
    \dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}
\end{equation}

If we package our mass-spring equations into matrices, we get:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mass_spring_matrices}
    \begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_1 \\ \dot{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} =
    \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\frac{k}{m} & -\frac{c}{m} \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} +
    \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \frac{1}{m} \end{bmatrix} u
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The A Matrix}: $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -k/m & -c/m \end{bmatrix}$. This matrix defines the "drift" of the system. If you turn off all motors ($u=0$), the $\mathbf{A}$ matrix dictates exactly how the system will coast to a stop, oscillate, or explode. Its eigenvalues completely define the system's natural stability!

    \item \textbf{The B Matrix}: $\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/m \end{bmatrix}$. This matrix defines how your control inputs $u$ actually inject energy into the state. Notice that the top value is $0$, meaning our motor cannot instantly teleport the position ($x_1$); it can only directly push the velocity ($x_2$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Output Equations and Observability}
\label{sec:output_equations}

Often, we cannot measure the entire state vector. For example, in a DC motor, we can measure the voltage and current flowing through it, but we may not directly measure the angular position $\theta$. The \textbf{output equation}\index{Output Equation} describes what we actually measure:

\begin{definition}{Output Equation}{output_eq}
  For a linear system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$, the output equation is:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:output_standard}
    \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}
  \end{equation}

  where:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{y} \in \Reals^p$ is the measurement vector (what we can observe)
    \item $\mathbf{C} \in \Reals^{p \times n}$ is the output matrix
    \item $\mathbf{D} \in \Reals^{p \times m}$ is the feedthrough matrix (often zero)
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The question "Can we infer the full state $\mathbf{x}$ from the measurements $\mathbf{y}$?" is precisely the question of \textbf{observability}, which we formalize later in Section~\ref{sec:observability}.

\section{Equilibrium Points and Linearization}
\label{sec:equilibrium}

\subsection{Equilibrium Point Definition}

\begin{definition}{Equilibrium Point}{equilibrium_def}
  An \textbf{equilibrium point}\index{Equilibrium Point} (or fixed point) $\mathbf{x}^* \in \Reals^n$ of the system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}^*)$ is a point where the vector field is zero:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:equilibrium_condition}
    f(\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{u}^*) = \mathbf{0}
  \end{equation}

  At an equilibrium, the state does not change; the system is in a "steady state."
\end{definition}

For linear systems $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$, if $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible and $\mathbf{u}^* = \mathbf{0}$, the unique equilibrium is $\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{0}$. For nonlinear systems, there may be multiple equilibria.

\subsection{Linearization via Taylor Expansion}
\label{subsec:linearization}

To understand the local behavior near an equilibrium, we linearize via Taylor expansion. Suppose we have an equilibrium $\mathbf{x}^*$ at control input $\mathbf{u}^*$. Consider a small perturbation $\boldsymbol{\delta x} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^*$ and $\boldsymbol{\delta u} = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{u}^*$.

Expand $f$ in a Taylor series around $(\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{u}^*)$:
\begin{equation}
  f(\mathbf{x}^* + \boldsymbol{\delta x}, \mathbf{u}^* + \boldsymbol{\delta u}) = f(\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{u}^*) + \mathbf{J}_f \boldsymbol{\delta x} + \mathbf{J}_u \boldsymbol{\delta u} + O(\|\boldsymbol{\delta x}\|^2, \|\boldsymbol{\delta u}\|^2)
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{J}_f$ is the \textbf{Jacobian matrix}\index{Jacobian Matrix} of $f$ with respect to the state:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:jacobian_def}
  \mathbf{J}_f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\bigg|_{(\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{u}^*)} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\ \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}_{(\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{u}^*)}
\end{equation}

and similarly:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{J}_u = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{u}}\bigg|_{(\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{u}^*)}
\end{equation}

Since $f(\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{u}^*) = \mathbf{0}$, the linearized dynamics are:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:linearized_dynamics}
  \dot{\boldsymbol{\delta x}} \approx \mathbf{A}_c \boldsymbol{\delta x} + \mathbf{B}_c \boldsymbol{\delta u}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{A}_c = \mathbf{J}_f$ and $\mathbf{B}_c = \mathbf{J}_u$. This is a linear system describing the small-signal behavior around the equilibrium.

\begin{example}{Pendulum Linearization}{pendulum_linearization}
Consider the pendulum from Example~\ref{ex:pendulum} with $\tau = 0$ (no control). The equation is:
\begin{equation}
  \ddot{\theta} + \frac{g}{L} \sin(\theta) = 0
\end{equation}

State vector: $\mathbf{x} = [\theta, \dot{\theta}]^T$. The vector field is:
\begin{equation}
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ -\frac{g}{L} \sin(x_1) \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Equilibria: $f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$ when $x_2 = 0$ and $\sin(x_1) = 0$, so $\mathbf{x}^* = [k\pi, 0]^T$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

**Bottom equilibrium** ($\theta = 0$): The Jacobian is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{J}_f = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\frac{g}{L}\cos(\theta) & 0 \end{bmatrix}\bigg|_{\theta=0} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\frac{g}{L} & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

The linearized system near the bottom is $\ddot{\theta} = -\frac{g}{L}\theta$, a harmonic oscillator.

**Top equilibrium** ($\theta = \pi$):
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{J}_f\bigg|_{\theta=\pi} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \frac{g}{L} & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

The linearized system is $\ddot{\theta} = \frac{g}{L}\theta$, which has exponentially growing solutions—an unstable saddle.
\end{example}

\section{Phase Portraits and Stability Classification}
\label{sec:phase_portraits}

\index{Phase Portrait}\index{Stability}

For a 2D system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$, the phase portrait is the collection of all trajectories in the $(x_1, x_2)$ plane. The behavior depends entirely on the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$.

\begin{theorem}{Stability Classification from Eigenvalues}{stability_classification}
  \label{thm:stability}
  For a 2D linear system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$:

  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stable Node}\index{Stable Node}: Both eigenvalues are real and negative ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2 < 0$). Trajectories spiral into the origin monotonically.

    \item \textbf{Unstable Node}\index{Unstable Node}: Both eigenvalues are real and positive ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2 > 0$). Trajectories spiral away from the origin.

    \item \textbf{Saddle Point}\index{Saddle Point}: One eigenvalue is positive, one is negative. Trajectories approach along the stable manifold (eigenvector of $\lambda < 0$) and diverge along the unstable manifold (eigenvector of $\lambda > 0$).

    \item \textbf{Stable Spiral (Focus)}\index{Spiral, Stable}\index{Focus}: Eigenvalues are complex conjugates with negative real part ($\lambda = \alpha \pm i\beta$, $\alpha < 0$). Trajectories spiral inward.

    \item \textbf{Unstable Spiral}\index{Spiral, Unstable}: Eigenvalues are complex conjugates with positive real part. Trajectories spiral outward.

    \item \textbf{Center}\index{Center}: Eigenvalues are purely imaginary ($\lambda = \pm i\omega$). Trajectories form closed orbits (periodic motion).
  \end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

The origin is \textbf{asymptotically stable}\index{Asymptotic Stability} if and only if all eigenvalues have negative real part. The origin is \textbf{marginally stable}\index{Marginal Stability} if the largest real part is zero. The origin is \textbf{unstable}\index{Instability} if any eigenvalue has positive real part.

\section{Solution of Linear Systems: The Matrix Exponential}
\label{sec:matrix_exponential}

\index{Matrix Exponential}

For a linear time-invariant (LTI) system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ with initial condition $\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_0$, the solution is:

\begin{theorem}{Solution via Matrix Exponential}{matrix_exponential_solution}
  \label{thm:matrix_exponential}
  The unique solution is:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:matrix_exp_solution}
    \mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0
  \end{equation}

  where $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ is the \textbf{matrix exponential}\index{Matrix Exponential}, defined as:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:matrix_exp_series}
    e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^k}{k!} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{A}t + \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^2}{2!} + \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^3}{3!} + \cdots
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Consider the scalar ODE $\dot{x} = ax$ with initial condition $x(0) = x_0$. The solution is $x(t) = e^{at} x_0$.

  By analogy, define $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ as the solution to:
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{\dd}{\dd t} e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{A} e^{\mathbf{A}t}, \quad e^{\mathbf{A} \cdot 0} = \mathbf{I}
  \end{equation}

  The Taylor series ansatz:
  \begin{equation}
    e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^k}{k!}
  \end{equation}

  satisfies this differential equation because:
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{\dd}{\dd t} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^k}{k!} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k (\mathbf{A}t)^{k-1} \mathbf{A}}{k!} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^{k-1} \mathbf{A}}{(k-1)!} = \mathbf{A} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^j}{j!} = \mathbf{A} e^{\mathbf{A}t}
  \end{equation}

  with $e^{\mathbf{A} \cdot 0} = \mathbf{I}$. Now verify that $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0$ solves the original system:
  \begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0\right) = \mathbf{A} e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t) \quad \checkmark
  \end{equation}
\end{proof}

**Practical Computation:** For small-dimensional systems, the matrix exponential can be computed via diagonalization. If $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{P} \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \mathbf{P}^{-1}$ where $\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$, then:
\begin{equation}
  e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{P} e^{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}t} \mathbf{P}^{-1} = \mathbf{P} \text{diag}(e^{\lambda_1 t}, \ldots, e^{\lambda_n t}) \mathbf{P}^{-1}
\end{equation}

For systems with input, $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:forced_solution}
  \mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \int_0^t e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(\tau) \dd \tau
\end{equation}

This is the superposition of free response (first term) and forced response (second term).

\section{Controllability}
\label{sec:controllability}

\index{Controllability}

The key question in control is: \emph{Can we steer the system from any initial state to any desired final state using our inputs?}

\begin{definition}{Controllability}{controllability_def}
  A linear system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$ is \textbf{controllable}\index{Controllable System} if for any initial state $\mathbf{x}_0$ and any final state $\mathbf{x}_f$, there exists a finite time $T > 0$ and an input sequence $\mathbf{u}(t)$ for $t \in [0, T]$ that drives the system from $\mathbf{x}_0$ to $\mathbf{x}_f$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Kalman Controllability Rank Condition}{kalman_controllability}
  \label{thm:controllability_rank}
  The system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$ is controllable if and only if the \textbf{controllability matrix}
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controllability_matrix}
    \mathbf{C}_{\text{rank}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} & \mathbf{A}^2\mathbf{B} & \cdots & \mathbf{A}^{n-1}\mathbf{B} \end{bmatrix}
  \end{equation}
  has full rank: $\text{rank}(\mathbf{C}_{\text{rank}}) = n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
  The controllability matrix $\mathbf{C}_{\text{rank}}$ has dimensions $n \times nm$. Each column $\mathbf{A}^k \mathbf{B}$ represents the "reach" of the input after $k$ applications of the dynamics. The rank condition ensures that the span of these columns covers all $n$ dimensions of state space.

  The intuition is from the Cayley-Hamilton theorem: any power $\mathbf{A}^k$ for $k \geq n$ can be expressed as a linear combination of $\mathbf{A}^0, \mathbf{A}^1, \ldots, \mathbf{A}^{n-1}$. Thus, checking columns up to $\mathbf{A}^{n-1}\mathbf{B}$ is sufficient.

  A rigorous proof uses the Gram matrix approach: controllability is equivalent to the controllability Gram matrix
  \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W}_c = \int_0^T e^{\mathbf{A}\tau} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{B}^T e^{\mathbf{A}^T \tau} \dd \tau
  \end{equation}
  being positive definite, which is equivalent to $\mathbf{C}_{\text{rank}}$ having full rank.
\end{proof}

**Physical Interpretation:** If the system is not controllable, it means some mode or degree of freedom cannot be directly influenced by the available inputs. For a DC motor whose position is $\theta$ and current is $i$, if the only measurement is current and there is no direct control over electrical resistance, the position might be uncontrollable.

\section{Observability}
\label{sec:observability}

\index{Observability}

Observability is the dual of controllability: \emph{Can we infer the full state from measurements alone?}

\begin{definition}{Observability}{observability_def}
  A linear system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}$ is \textbf{observable}\index{Observable System} if the initial state $\mathbf{x}(0)$ can be uniquely determined from knowledge of the input $\mathbf{u}(t)$ and the output $\mathbf{y}(t)$ over a finite time interval $[0, T]$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Kalman Observability Rank Condition}{kalman_observability}
  \label{thm:observability_rank}
  The system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}$ is observable if and only if the \textbf{observability matrix}
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:observability_matrix}
    \mathbf{O} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{C} \\ \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A} \\ \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^2 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}
  \end{equation}
  has full rank: $\text{rank}(\mathbf{O}) = n$.
\end{theorem}

The observability matrix has dimensions $np \times n$ where $p$ is the number of measurements. Each row $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^k$ represents the $k$-th time derivative of the measurement, weighted by the dynamics. If these rows span all $n$ dimensions, we can reconstruct the state.

\section{The Mass-Spring-Damper System: Complete Analysis}
\label{sec:mass_spring_complete}

\index{Mass-Spring-Damper}

Let us apply the concepts from this chapter to the mass-spring-damper system with stiffness $k$, damping $c$, and mass $m$:
\begin{equation}
  m\ddot{q} + c\dot{q} + kq = u
\end{equation}

\textbf{State vector:} $\mathbf{x} = [q, \dot{q}]^T$.

\textbf{Matrices:}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -k/m & -c/m \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/m \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Characteristic polynomial:}
\begin{equation}
  \det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I}) = \det\begin{bmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -k/m & -c/m - \lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 + \frac{c}{m}\lambda + \frac{k}{m}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Eigenvalues:}
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{1,2} = -\frac{c}{2m} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{c}{2m}\right)^2 - \frac{k}{m}} = -\frac{c}{2m} \pm \sqrt{\frac{c^2}{4m^2} - \frac{k}{m}}
\end{equation}

Define the natural frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ and damping ratio $\zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}$. Then:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{1,2} = -\zeta \omega_0 \pm i \omega_0\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}
\end{equation}

(assuming $\zeta < 1$ for the underdamped case; see below for other cases).

\subsection{Underdamped ($0 < \zeta < 1$)}

The discriminant is negative, so eigenvalues are complex: $\lambda = -\zeta \omega_0 \pm i \omega_d$ where $\omega_d = \omega_0 \sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$ is the damped frequency.

The mass oscillates while decaying. The solution is:
\begin{equation}
  q(t) = e^{-\zeta \omega_0 t} \left( q_0 \cos(\omega_d t) + \frac{\dot{q}_0 + \zeta \omega_0 q_0}{\omega_d} \sin(\omega_d t) \right)
\end{equation}

The trajectory in phase space is a \textbf{stable spiral}\index{Stable Spiral}, spiraling into the origin.

\subsection{Critically Damped ($\zeta = 1$)}

The eigenvalues coincide: $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = -\omega_0$. The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is not diagonalizable; it has a Jordan block.

The solution is:
\begin{equation}
  q(t) = (q_0 + (\dot{q}_0 + \omega_0 q_0)t) e^{-\omega_0 t}
\end{equation}

This is the fastest non-oscillatory return to equilibrium. The trajectory does \emph{not} spiral; it approaches the origin monotonically.

\subsection{Overdamped ($\zeta > 1$)}

The eigenvalues are real and distinct:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_1 = -\zeta \omega_0 + \omega_0\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}, \quad \lambda_2 = -\zeta \omega_0 - \omega_0\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}
\end{equation}

Both are negative (since $\zeta > 0$). The solution is:
\begin{equation}
  q(t) = C_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} + C_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}
\end{equation}

The slower eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ dominates for large times. The trajectory is a \textbf{stable node}\index{Stable Node}—no oscillation, just exponential decay. The system returns to rest more slowly than the critically damped case.

\subsection{Controllability and Observability}

The controllability matrix is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{C}_{\text{rank}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1/m \\ 1/m & -c/m^2 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Its determinant is $\det(\mathbf{C}_{\text{rank}}) = 0 - \frac{1}{m^2} = -\frac{1}{m^2} \neq 0$. Thus $\mathbf{C}_{\text{rank}}$ has full rank and the system is \textbf{controllable}. We can steer position and velocity to any desired values using the input force $u$.

If we measure position only, $\mathbf{C} = [1, 0]$, the observability matrix is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

which has rank 2. The system is \textbf{observable}. We can infer both position and velocity from position measurements alone (by differentiating or using a Kalman filter).

\section{Transfer Function to State Space and Back}
\label{sec:transfer_function_conversion}

\index{Transfer Function}

The transfer function $G(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)}$ relates input and output in the Laplace domain. For a linear system with zero initial conditions, the transfer function is:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:transfer_function_def}
  G(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}
\end{equation}

This is derived from the state-space equations by taking Laplace transforms:
\begin{align}
  s\mathbf{X}(s) &= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}(s) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}(s) \\
  \mathbf{Y}(s) &= \mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}(s) + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}(s)
\end{align}

Solve for $\mathbf{X}(s)$:
\begin{equation}
  (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}(s) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{X}(s) = (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}(s)
\end{equation}

Substitute into the output equation:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{Y}(s) = \left[\mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}\right]\mathbf{U}(s) = G(s)\mathbf{U}(s)
\end{equation}

**Conversion from transfer function to state space** is not unique—there are many valid state representations. The most common choice is the controllable canonical form (the companion matrix form from Section~\ref{sec:ode_conversion}).

\section{Nonlinear Systems and Phase Portraits}
\label{sec:nonlinear_phase}

While we have focused on linear systems, real physical systems are often nonlinear. For a nonlinear system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = f(\mathbf{x})$, the phase portrait can be qualitatively understood by:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Finding equilibrium points by solving $f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$
  \item Linearizing around each equilibrium: $\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta x}} \approx \mathbf{J}_f(\mathbf{x}^*) \boldsymbol{\delta x}$
  \item Classifying the equilibrium by its eigenvalues (Theorem~\ref{thm:stability})
  \item Plotting the vector field $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = f(\mathbf{x})$ to visualize global behavior
\end{enumerate}

The linearization is valid in a neighborhood of the equilibrium. For large perturbations, we must rely on numerical simulation or other techniques (Lyapunov theory, bifurcation analysis).

\section{Python Worked Example: State Space Simulation and Phase Portrait}
\label{sec:python_example}

Consider the underdamped mass-spring-damper system: $m=1$, $c=0.5$, $k=2$, with initial conditions $q_0 = 1$, $\dot{q}_0 = 0$.

\begin{verbatim}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import odeint

# Parameters
m, c, k = 1.0, 0.5, 2.0
A = np.array([[0, 1], [-k/m, -c/m]])
B = np.array([[0], [1/m]])

# System dynamics
def dynamics(x, t, u=0):
    return A @ x + B.flatten() * u

# Initial condition
x0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
t = np.linspace(0, 10, 1000)

# Simulate
x = odeint(dynamics, x0, t)

# Phase portrait
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5))

# Time evolution
ax1.plot(t, x[:, 0], label='Position q(t)')
ax1.plot(t, x[:, 1], label='Velocity dq/dt(t)')
ax1.set_xlabel('Time (s)')
ax1.set_ylabel('State')
ax1.legend()
ax1.grid()
ax1.set_title('Time Evolution')

# Phase portrait
ax2.plot(x[:, 0], x[:, 1], 'b-', linewidth=2)
ax2.plot(x[0, 0], x[0, 1], 'go', markersize=10, label='Initial')
ax2.plot(x[-1, 0], x[-1, 1], 'ro', markersize=10, label='Final')
ax2.set_xlabel('Position q')
ax2.set_ylabel('Velocity dq/dt')
ax2.grid()
ax2.legend()
ax2.set_title('Phase Portrait (Stable Spiral)')

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

# Eigenvalues
eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eig(A)
print(f"Eigenvalues: {eigenvalues}")
print(f"Damping ratio zeta = {c / (2*np.sqrt(k*m)):.3f}")
print(f"Natural freq omega_0 = {np.sqrt(k/m):.3f} rad/s")
\end{verbatim}

This code simulates the system and plots both the time evolution and the phase portrait. For the underdamped case ($\zeta = 0.177$), you should see a spiral converging to the origin with oscillation.

\section{Equilibrium Analysis Summary}
\label{sec:equilibrium_summary}

For any equilibrium point $\mathbf{x}^*$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Compute the Jacobian: $\mathbf{A} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\big|_{\mathbf{x}^*}$
  \item Find eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$
  \item Classify the equilibrium according to Theorem~\ref{thm:stability}
  \item If all eigenvalues have negative real part: \textbf{asymptotically stable}
  \item If any eigenvalue has positive real part: \textbf{unstable}
  \item If the largest real part is zero (center or boundary case): \textbf{marginally stable}
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary}

\begin{principle}{Key Takeaways: State Space Fundamentals}{summary_principle}
\textbf{1. State Vector:} The state $\mathbf{x}$ is the minimum information needed to predict the system's future. For an $n$-th order ODE, the state is $n$-dimensional.

\textbf{2. Kalman's Revolution (1960):} State-space representations replace transfer functions, enabling control of MIMO systems, design of optimal controllers, and systematic analysis of internal system structure.

\textbf{3. Standard Forms:} Any system can be written as $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}, t)$ (nonlinear) or $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$ (linear). The $\mathbf{A}$ matrix encodes natural stability; the $\mathbf{B}$ matrix encodes control authority.

\textbf{4. ODE Reduction:} Any $n$-th order ODE reduces to $n$ first-order state equations via companion form.

\textbf{5. Equilibrium and Linearization:} Equilibria are found by solving $f(\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{u}^*) = \mathbf{0}$. Local behavior is determined by the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_f = \partial f / \partial \mathbf{x}$.

\textbf{6. Stability from Eigenvalues:} Eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$ determine phase-portrait structure (node, spiral, saddle, center). Negative real parts mean stability.

\textbf{7. Matrix Exponential:} The solution $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0 + \int_0^t e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)\,\dd\tau$ decomposes into free response and forced response.

\textbf{8. Controllability (Kalman Rank Test):} The system is controllable iff $\text{rank}([\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}, \ldots, \mathbf{A}^{n-1}\mathbf{B}]) = n$. If not controllable, some state variables cannot be influenced by inputs.

\textbf{9. Observability (Dual of Controllability):} The system is observable iff $\text{rank}([\mathbf{C}^T, \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{C}^T, \ldots, (\mathbf{A}^T)^{n-1}\mathbf{C}^T]^T) = n$. If not observable, some state variables cannot be inferred from measurements.

\textbf{10. Duality:} A system is controllable iff its dual (with $\mathbf{A}^T$, $\mathbf{C}^T$, $\mathbf{B}^T$) is observable. This duality underpins Kalman filter design.
\end{principle}
