\chapter{The Articulated Body Algorithm}
\label{ch:articulated_body_algorithm}

\epigraph{%
  You cannot push a single domino. When a body is linked, pushing one piece means pushing the entire chain, yet we compute the physical consequences of the entire cascading collision with linear precision.
}{}

\begin{laymansbox}{Context \& Use Case: Forward Simulation}{context_box}
\textbf{The Math You Will See:} We will cover the $O(N)$ \textbf{Articulated Body Algorithm (ABA)} and define the \textbf{Articulated Inertia} ($\mathcal{I}^A$).
\textbf{Why We Need It:} In Chapter 7, we asked: "I want to move my arm here, how much motor torque do I need?" (Inverse Dynamics). ABA solves the exact opposite: "I am applying this much torque, what acceleration will result?" (Forward Dynamics). Physics simulators like MuJoCo or games use ABA every single frame to predict how a robot or character will move. If you use a massive matrix equation, it takes an impossibly long $O(N^3)$ calculation time to invert for 30 joints. ABA computes it perfectly recursively in just $O(N)$ time.
\textbf{All Variables Defined:} $\mathbf{p}_i$ is the bias force dragging on a link, and $\mathcal{I}^A_i$ is the apparent inertia of a link when dragged along with all its children.
\end{laymansbox}

\section{Historical Context: The Computational Mechanics Revolution}
\label{sec:aba_history}

For most of the twentieth century, the forward dynamics problem—computing accelerations from applied forces—was solved through monolithic matrix inversion. Given the equations of motion in the form:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q}) \ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \boldsymbol{\tau} - \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}) \dot{\mathbf{q}} - \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})
\end{equation}

one inverts the mass matrix $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})$ to solve for $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$:
\begin{equation}
  \ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})^{-1} (\boldsymbol{\tau} - \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}) \dot{\mathbf{q}} - \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q}))
\end{equation}

For an $N$-joint robot, computing the inverse of an $N \times N$ matrix via Gaussian elimination requires $O(N^3)$ floating-point operations—a severe computational bottleneck when $N$ is large (humanoid robots have 30+ joints; hands have 20+; cloth simulation may have thousands of degrees of freedom).

In 1983, Roy Featherstone published a seminal paper \cite{featherstone1983} proposing the \textbf{Articulated Body Algorithm (ABA)}, which solves forward dynamics in \textit{linear} time: $O(N)$ operations. Featherstone's insight—to propagate effective inertias recursively through the kinematic tree—transformed computational robotics from a theoretical exercise to a practical tool usable in real-time simulation.

The ABA has become the foundation of modern physics engines. Every frame of a video game featuring character animation, every timestep of a MuJoCo simulation, and every dynamics prediction in a robot control system leverages Featherstone's algorithm. Subsequent refinements by Featherstone himself \cite{featherstone1987} and countless others have extended ABA to handle floating bases (crucial for humanoid robots), contact constraints, and complex contact models.

\section{The Forward Dynamics Problem}
\label{sec:forward_dynamics_problem}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{subsec:fd_formulation}

\textit{Forward Dynamics} (also called \textit{Direct Dynamics}) solves the inverse of the inverse dynamics problem:

\begin{equation}
  \text{Given: } \mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}, \boldsymbol{\tau} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{Find: } \ddot{\mathbf{q}}
\end{equation}

The equations of motion are:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q}) \ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}) \dot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau} + \mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is the \textbf{inertia matrix} (symmetric positive definite).
  \item $\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}) \dot{\mathbf{q}}$ is the \textbf{Coriolis and centrifugal force}.
  \item $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})$ is the \textbf{gravitational force}.
  \item $\boldsymbol{\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the applied joint torques (motor outputs).
  \item $\mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}}$ is external forces (contact, disturbances, etc.).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why $O(N^3)$ is Intractable}
\label{subsec:why_not_invert}

Direct inversion of $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})$ via Gaussian elimination (the standard $O(N^3)$ method) becomes prohibitively slow:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c}
Number of Joints ($N$) & Operations ($N^3$) & Time @ 1 GHz & Time @ 100 GHz (GPU) \\
\hline
10 & $1,000$ & 1 ns & 0.01 ns \\
30 & $27,000$ & 27 ns & 0.27 ns \\
100 & $1,000,000$ & 1 $\mu$s & 0.01 $\mu$s \\
1000 & $10^9$ & 1 ms & 0.01 ms \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

For real-time control (e.g., a humanoid robot running at 1 kHz requiring $\sim 1$ ms per timestep), even $N=30$ becomes challenging with direct inversion. For interactive simulation or games (running at 30-60 Hz), the overhead is unacceptable.

ABA reduces this to $O(N)$ operations—a linear dependence that scales gracefully to hundreds of joints.

\section{Articulated Inertia: The Key Insight}
\label{sec:articulated_inertia}

\subsection{Intuitive Explanation}
\label{subsec:inertia_intuition}

Imagine pushing a heavy metal rod on a frictionless table. The resistance you feel—the inertia—is proportional to the rod's mass. Now, imagine that rod is rigidly connected via a hinge to another rod, which in turn is connected to a chain of massive bricks. If you push the first rod, you are indirectly pushing all the connected mass behind it. The \textit{effective} resistance at the base—the articulated inertia—accounts for all the downstream mass.

\begin{laymansbox}{What is Articulated Inertia?}{aba_intuition}
  \textbf{Rigid body inertia:} A single body's resistance to acceleration.

  \textbf{Articulated inertia:} The effective inertia of a body \textit{as if} all downstream bodies (children) were rigidly attached to it, accounting for how motion at this joint propagates down the kinematic chain.

  The key: downstream inertias are not simply added. Instead, they are \textit{projected} across the joint constraint. A joint restricts motion to one degree of freedom, and only the projection of child inertia onto that DOF contributes to the apparent inertia.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{Mathematical Definition}
\label{subsec:articulated_inertia_math}

For a link $i$ with spatial inertia $\mathcal{I}_i$ and child link $i+1$ with articulated inertia $\mathcal{I}^A_{i+1}$, the \textbf{articulated inertia of link $i$} is:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:articulated_inertia}
  \mathcal{I}^A_i = \mathcal{I}_i + \mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} - \frac{(\mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}) (\mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1})^T}{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}}
\end{equation}

Breaking down each term:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\mathcal{I}_i$: The rigid-body spatial inertia of link $i$ itself.
  \item $\mathcal{I}^A_{i+1}$: The articulated inertia of the child link (everything downstream).
  \item $\mathcal{S}_{i+1}$: The screw axis of the joint connecting link $i$ to link $i+1$ (6D vector).
  \item $(\mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1})$: The force required to accelerate the child chain along its screw axis.
  \item $\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}$: A scalar—the effective inertia \textit{along} the joint's single degree of freedom (similar to $I \alpha$ in 1D dynamics).
  \item The fraction: Projects out the direction of motion along the joint; what remains is the inertia perpendicular to that motion (resistive to the parent).
\end{enumerate}

This is a rank-one update of the spatial inertia: we add the child's articulated inertia, then subtract the projection of that child onto the joint DOF.

\section{Derivation from First Principles}
\label{sec:aba_derivation}

\subsection{Recursive Constraint Satisfaction}
\label{subsec:constraint_derivation}

Consider a two-body system: body $i$ (parent) connected to body $i+1$ (child) via a single-DOF joint with screw axis $\mathcal{S}_{i+1}$.

The equations of motion for each body are:
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{I}_i \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i &= \mathbf{f}_i - \mathbf{f}_{i+1}^{\text{reaction}} \\
  \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i+1} &= \mathbf{f}_{i+1} - \mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}}^{(i+1)}
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{f}_i$ is the force on body $i$ and $\mathbf{f}_{i+1}^{\text{reaction}}$ is the reaction force from the child.

The kinematic constraint is that the acceleration of the child relative to the parent must be along the joint screw:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i+1} = \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i + \text{Ad}_{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}} \ddot{q}_{i+1}
\end{equation}

where $\ddot{q}_{i+1}$ is the joint acceleration and $\text{Ad}_{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}} = \mathcal{S}_{i+1}$ (the screw represents infinitesimal motion).

Substituting into the child's equation:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}_{i+1} (\dot{\mathcal{V}}_i + \mathcal{S}_{i+1} \ddot{q}_{i+1}) = \mathbf{f}_{i+1} - \mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}}^{(i+1)}
\end{equation}

Rearranging:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1} \ddot{q}_{i+1} = \mathbf{f}_{i+1} - \mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}}^{(i+1)} - \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i
\end{equation}

The reaction force on the parent is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{f}_{i+1}^{\text{reaction}} = \mathcal{I}_{i+1} (\dot{\mathcal{V}}_i + \mathcal{S}_{i+1} \ddot{q}_{i+1}) = \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i + \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1} \ddot{q}_{i+1}
\end{equation}

For the parent, the equation of motion becomes:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}_i \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i = \mathbf{f}_i - \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i - \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1} \ddot{q}_{i+1}
\end{equation}

Rearranging:
\begin{equation}
  (\mathcal{I}_i + \mathcal{I}_{i+1}) \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i = \mathbf{f}_i - \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1} \ddot{q}_{i+1}
\end{equation}

This shows that the parent sees an effective inertia of $\mathcal{I}_i + \mathcal{I}_{i+1}$. However, this is only true if the child's acceleration is coupled to the parent's (i.e., if we solve for $\ddot{q}_{i+1}$ in terms of the parent's acceleration).

The key step is to \textit{eliminate} the child's acceleration. From the constraint:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1} \ddot{q}_{i+1} = \mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T (\mathbf{f}_{i+1} - \mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}}^{(i+1)}) - \mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i
\end{equation}

Solving for $\ddot{q}_{i+1}$:
\begin{equation}
  \ddot{q}_{i+1} = \frac{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T (\mathbf{f}_{i+1} - \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i)}{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}} - \frac{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}}^{(i+1)}}{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}}
\end{equation}

Substituting back into the reaction force:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{f}_{i+1}^{\text{reaction}} = \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i + \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1} \frac{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T (\mathbf{f}_{i+1} - \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i)}{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}}
\end{equation}

After algebraic simplification (collecting terms proportional to $\dot{\mathcal{V}}_i$ and to $\mathbf{f}_{i+1}$), one obtains:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{f}_{i+1}^{\text{reaction}} = \mathcal{I}^A_i \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i + \mathbf{p}_i + \text{(terms proportional to child force)}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}^A_i = \mathcal{I}_i + \mathcal{I}_{i+1} - \frac{\mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1} (\mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1})^T}{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}}
\end{equation}

This is precisely Equation \ref{eq:articulated_inertia}. The \textbf{bias force} $\mathbf{p}_i$ encodes accelerations and external forces that would act even if the joint acceleration were zero.

\section{The Articulated Body Algorithm: Three Passes}
\label{sec:aba_three_passes}

The complete ABA consists of three sequential passes through the kinematic tree:

\subsection{Pass 1: Outward Pass (Velocity and Bias Forces)}
\label{subsec:pass1_outward}

Starting at the base (root) link $0$ and sweeping outward to the end-effector leaves, compute the spatial velocity and bias force at each link.

\subsubsection{Spatial Velocity Propagation}
\label{subsubsec:velocity_propagation}

The spatial velocity of link $i$ is related to link $i-1$ (its parent) by:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{V}_i = \mathcal{V}_{i-1} + \mathcal{S}_i \dot{q}_i
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{S}_i \dot{q}_i$ is the velocity component due to joint $i$'s motion.

Start at the base: $\mathcal{V}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ (or non-zero if the base is floating). Then recursively compute:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{V}_1 = \mathcal{V}_0 + \mathcal{S}_1 \dot{q}_1 = \mathcal{S}_1 \dot{q}_1
\end{equation}

and so on for each child.

\subsubsection{Coriolis Acceleration and Bias Force}
\label{subsubsec:bias_force}

The spatial acceleration is related to the time derivative of velocity:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i = \dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i-1} + \mathcal{S}_i \ddot{q}_i + \dot{\mathcal{S}}_i \dot{q}_i
\end{equation}

However, at this stage of the algorithm, we do not yet know $\ddot{q}_i$. Instead, we compute the \textbf{bias acceleration} (what the acceleration would be if the joint acceleration were zero):

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{V}_{c,i} = \dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i-1} + [\mathcal{V}_i, \mathcal{S}_i \dot{q}_i]
\end{equation}

where $[\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{S}]$ is the Lie bracket (spatial cross product) encoding Coriolis effects. For spatial vectors:
\begin{equation}
  [\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{S}] = [\boldsymbol{\omega}, \mathbf{v}] \text{ such that } [\mathcal{V}] \mathcal{S} - \mathcal{S} [\mathcal{V}]^T = [\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{S}]
\end{equation}

The bias force is then:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_i = \mathcal{I}_i \mathcal{V}_{c,i}
\end{equation}

This encodes the Coriolis and centrifugal effects that accelerate the link even without external torques.

\subsection{Pass 2: Inward Pass (Articulated Inertia Propagation)}
\label{subsec:pass2_inward}

Starting at the leaves and propagating inward toward the base, compute the articulated inertia at each link.

\subsubsection{Boundary Condition}
\label{subsubsec:leaf_inertia}

At a leaf (end-effector with no children), the articulated inertia equals the rigid inertia:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}^A_{\text{leaf}} = \mathcal{I}_{\text{leaf}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Recursive Update}
\label{subsubsec:inertia_recursion}

For each link $i$ with articulated inertia $\mathcal{I}^A_{i+1}$ of its child, update:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:aba_inertia_recursion}
  \mathcal{I}^A_i = \mathcal{I}_i + \mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} - \frac{(\mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}) (\mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1})^T}{\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}}
\end{equation}

The denominator $\mathcal{S}_{i+1}^T \mathcal{I}^A_{i+1} \mathcal{S}_{i+1}$ is a scalar (the effective inertia along the joint's DOF) and is typically cached for use in Pass 3.

\subsubsection{Bias Force Propagation (Optional)}
\label{subsubsec:bias_propagation}

At each link, the bias force also accumulates contributions from constraints. For a simple open chain with no external forces on the links:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_i^{\text{total}} = \mathbf{p}_i + \mathbf{f}_{i+1}^{\text{reaction}}
\end{equation}

For now, we defer the detailed calculation and focus on the inertia.

\subsection{Pass 3: Outward Pass (Joint Accelerations)}
\label{subsec:pass3_outward}

Starting at the base and sweeping outward, compute the joint accelerations and link accelerations.

\subsubsection{Base Link Acceleration}
\label{subsubsec:base_acceleration}

At the base link $0$, the equations of motion are:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}_0 \dot{\mathcal{V}}_0 = \mathbf{f}_0 - \mathbf{f}_1^{\text{reaction}}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{f}_0$ is the external force on the base (typically zero for a fixed base) and $\mathbf{f}_1^{\text{reaction}}$ is the reaction from the first link.

If the base is floating (6 DOF), we solve directly for the base acceleration. If the base is fixed, $\dot{\mathcal{V}}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ is constrained.

\subsubsection{Recursive Joint Acceleration Computation}
\label{subsubsec:joint_acceleration}

For each link $i$ (starting from the root and moving outward), the equation of motion is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}^A_i \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i = \mathbf{f}_i - \text{reaction from child link}
\end{equation}

At the joint level, the constraint is:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i = \dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i-1} + \mathcal{S}_i \ddot{q}_i
\end{equation}

Projecting onto the joint direction (taking the dot product with $\mathcal{S}_i^T$):
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}_i^T \mathcal{I}^A_i \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i = \mathcal{S}_i^T (\mathbf{f}_i - \mathbf{p}_i)
\end{equation}

Substituting the kinematic constraint:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}_i^T \mathcal{I}^A_i (\dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i-1} + \mathcal{S}_i \ddot{q}_i) = \mathcal{S}_i^T (\boldsymbol{\tau}_i - \mathbf{p}_i)
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\tau}_i$ is the applied joint torque. Solving for $\ddot{q}_i$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:aba_joint_acceleration}
  \ddot{q}_i = \frac{\boldsymbol{\tau}_i - \mathcal{S}_i^T \mathbf{p}_i - \mathcal{S}_i^T \mathcal{I}^A_i \dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i-1}}{\mathcal{S}_i^T \mathcal{I}^A_i \mathcal{S}_i}
\end{equation}

Once $\ddot{q}_i$ is known, compute the spatial acceleration of link $i$:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathcal{V}}_i = \dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i-1} + \mathcal{S}_i \ddot{q}_i
\end{equation}

This is propagated to the next link, and the process repeats.

\section{Proof of O(N) Complexity}
\label{sec:aba_complexity}

The complexity analysis is straightforward:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pass 1 (Outward velocity and bias):} For each of $N$ links, we compute one scalar velocity ($O(1)$) and one spatial vector bias force ($O(1)$). Total: $O(N)$.

  \item \textbf{Pass 2 (Inward articulated inertia):} For each of $N$ links, we perform:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item One spatial inertia addition: $O(36)$ (a $6 \times 6$ matrix addition).
    \item One matrix-vector product: $\mathcal{I}^A \mathcal{S}$, yielding a $6D$ vector, $O(36)$ operations.
    \item One outer product: $(6D) \times (6D)^T = 6 \times 6$ matrix, $O(36)$ operations.
    \item One scalar division: $O(1)$.
  \end{itemize}
  All operations are constant (independent of $N$). Total: $O(N)$.

  \item \textbf{Pass 3 (Outward accelerations):} For each of $N$ links, we compute:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item One dot product: $\mathcal{S}_i^T (\cdot)$, $O(6)$ operations.
    \item One scalar division: $O(1)$.
    \item One scalar-vector multiplication: $\mathcal{S}_i \ddot{q}_i$, $O(6)$ operations.
    \item One vector addition: $O(6)$ operations.
  \end{itemize}
  Total: $O(N)$.
\end{enumerate}

Thus, the overall complexity is $O(N) + O(N) + O(N) = O(N)$, a factor of $N^2$ improvement over direct matrix inversion.

\section{Floating Base: 6-DOF Root}
\label{sec:floating_base}

Many robots have a floating base—a root link not constrained to a fixed location (humanoids, quadrupeds, characters in games). The floating base has 6 degrees of freedom (3 translation, 3 rotation).

\subsection{Modification to ABA}
\label{subsec:floating_base_aba}

The algorithm is modified minimally:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item In Pass 3, instead of fixing $\dot{\mathcal{V}}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, we compute:
  \begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathcal{V}}_0 = \mathcal{I}_0^{-1} (\mathbf{f}_0 - \mathbf{f}_1^{\text{reaction}})
  \end{equation}
  where $\mathbf{f}_0$ is the net external force on the base (gravity, applied forces) and $\mathbf{f}_1^{\text{reaction}}$ is the reaction from the first joint.

  \item The matrix inverse $\mathcal{I}_0^{-1}$ is computed once (it is constant) and cached.

  \item All subsequent steps (joint accelerations, link accelerations) proceed as normal.
\end{enumerate}

The impact on complexity is minimal: a single $6 \times 6$ matrix inverse at the base, still $O(1)$ per timestep.

\subsection{Contact and Constraints}
\label{subsec:contact_constraints}

When a floating-base robot makes contact with the environment (e.g., a humanoid's foot touching the ground), the contact introduces a constraint that reduces the effective DOF. Contact handling in ABA typically uses:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Constraint Impulse Methods:} Compute contact forces (impulses) that prevent interpenetration, incorporated into Pass 3 as additional terms in the force equations.

  \item \textbf{Reduced Dynamics:} Temporarily treat the contacted link as having a fixed base, reducing the DOF until contact breaks.

  \item \textbf{Hybrid Approach:} Use ABA to compute unconstrained accelerations, then solve for constraint forces and correct accelerations post-hoc.
\end{enumerate}

Modern simulators (MuJoCo, PyBullet) implement sophisticated contact handling on top of ABA, but the core algorithm remains unchanged.

\section{Joint Friction and Motor Inertia}
\label{sec:friction_motor_inertia}

\subsection{Joint Friction}
\label{subsec:joint_friction}

Viscous friction at joint $i$ is modeled as:
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{friction}, i} = -b_i \dot{q}_i
\end{equation}

where $b_i$ is the damping coefficient. This is incorporated into the bias force:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_i \gets \mathbf{p}_i + b_i \dot{q}_i \mathcal{S}_i
\end{equation}

Coulomb (dry) friction is more complex but can be approximated similarly. Joint limits are handled via constraint forces or penalty forces.

\subsection{Motor Inertia}
\label{subsec:motor_inertia}

Actuators (motors, servos) have their own inertia $I_{\text{motor}, i}$. This is added to the effective inertia along the joint:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}_i^T \mathcal{I}^A_i \mathcal{S}_i \gets \mathcal{S}_i^T \mathcal{I}^A_i \mathcal{S}_i + I_{\text{motor}, i}
\end{equation}

This slightly increases the denominator in Equation \ref{eq:aba_joint_acceleration}, accounting for the additional inertia the motor must overcome.

\section{ABA vs. CRBA: When to Use Which}
\label{sec:aba_vs_crba}

The \textbf{Composite Rigid Body Algorithm (CRBA)} is an alternative approach that constructs the inertia matrix $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})$ in $O(N)$ time (instead of the naive $O(N^3)$) by compositing inertias of rigid bodies, then solves via matrix inversion. This is useful when:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multiple Forward Dynamics Calls with Same $\mathbf{q}$:} If you need to solve $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q}) \ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_1$, $\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_2$, etc., with different torques but same configuration, CRBA computes $\mathbf{M}$ once ($O(N)$) and solves multiple RHS ($O(N^3)$ total for all). ABA solves each independently ($O(N)$ each, $O(N^2)$ total).

  \item \textbf{Linear System Solves:} If the forward dynamics must be inverted (e.g., in optimal control), having $\mathbf{M}$ explicitly is helpful.

  \item \textbf{Stability Analysis:} Eigenvalues of $\mathbf{M}$ are useful for understanding system dynamics.
\end{itemize}

For single forward dynamics solves in real-time simulation, \textbf{ABA is superior}—it avoids both matrix construction and inversion.

\section{Forward Dynamics vs. Inverse Dynamics Decision Criteria}
\label{sec:fd_id_decision}

\subsection{When to Use Inverse Dynamics (Recursive Newton-Euler)}
\label{subsec:use_id}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Trajectory Tracking:} You have a desired trajectory $(\mathbf{q}_d, \dot{\mathbf{q}}_d, \ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d)$ and want to compute the torques $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ required to achieve it.

  \item \textbf{Control Design:} Inverse dynamics enables feedback control: compute the feedforward torques from the desired trajectory, add feedback corrections based on tracking error.

  \item \textbf{Motion Planning Validation:} Given a planned trajectory, compute required torques to check if motors can deliver them.
\end{itemize}

Complexity: $O(N)$ using Recursive Newton-Euler (RNE).

\subsection{When to Use Forward Dynamics (ABA)}
\label{subsec:use_fd}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Physical Simulation:} You apply known torques and predict what the system will do. Essential for interactive simulation, games, physics-based RL.

  \item \textbf{Disturbance Response:} Analyze how the system responds to unexpected forces or unmodeled interactions.

  \item \textbf{Dynamics Prediction:} Rollout predictions of future states for planning or control optimization (MPC).
\end{itemize}

Complexity: $O(N)$ using ABA.

\section{Constraint Handling: Contact Forces and Joint Limits}
\label{sec:constraints}

\subsection{Unilateral Contact Constraints}
\label{subsec:contact}

When a link contacts the environment (e.g., a foot on the ground), the contact exerts a reaction force that constrains the link's motion. This is formulated as:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Inequality Constraint:} Penetration depth $\geq 0$.

  \item \textbf{Complementarity:} If depth $> 0$ (not in contact), force $= 0$. If depth $= 0$ (in contact), force $\geq 0$.

  \item \textbf{Friction Cone:} Friction force magnitude $\leq \mu \times$ normal force, where $\mu$ is the coefficient of friction.
\end{enumerate}

Modern simulators solve these complementarity constraints using iterative algorithms (projected Gauss-Seidel, interior-point methods).

\subsection{Joint Limits}
\label{subsec:joint_limits}

Joint limits enforce $q_{\min,i} \leq q_i \leq q_{\max,i}$. When a joint hits a limit, it acts like a contact: the joint constraint becomes active (the joint no longer has a full degree of freedom), and a constraint force enforces the limit.

\section{Connection to Gaussian Elimination}
\label{sec:gaussian_elimination_connection}

Featherstone's insight was that the articulated inertia recursion is mathematically equivalent to \textbf{Gaussian elimination on a band-diagonal system}—the sparsity structure of the mass matrix for a serial kinematic chain.

The mass matrix for a serial robot is band-diagonal: $M_{ij}$ is non-zero only if links $i$ and $j$ are on the same kinematic path (ancestor/descendant). This sparsity can be exploited to invert the matrix in $O(N)$ time instead of $O(N^3)$.

ABA effectively performs this sparse elimination implicitly, without ever constructing the full matrix. This is why ABA is both fast and numerically stable: it avoids the ill-conditioning issues that arise from explicit matrix inversion.

\section{Worked Example: Full ABA Walkthrough for a 3-Link Arm}
\label{sec:worked_example_aba}

Consider a planar 3-link arm (all revolute joints, rotating about the Z-axis) with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Link 1: length $L_1 = 1$ m, mass $m_1 = 1$ kg, rotates at the origin.
  \item Link 2: length $L_2 = 0.8$ m, mass $m_2 = 0.8$ kg, hinges at the end of Link 1.
  \item Link 3: length $L_3 = 0.6$ m, mass $m_3 = 0.6$ kg, hinges at the end of Link 2.
\end{itemize}

For simplicity, assume all mass is concentrated at the link's center of mass (half-length from the base). The spatial inertia matrix for a point mass $m$ at distance $r$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I} = m \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0}_{3 \times 3} & \mathbf{0}_{3 \times 3} \\ \mathbf{0}_{3 \times 3} & \mathbf{I}_{3 \times 3} \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

(Idealized point mass at the CoM; exact inertia would include the rotational inertia tensor.)

\subsection{Configuration at the Test Point}
\label{subsec:config_aba}

At configuration $\mathbf{q} = [\pi/4, \pi/6, -\pi/3]^T$ (45°, 30°, -60°) with joint velocities $\dot{\mathbf{q}} = [0.5, -0.3, 0.2]^T$ rad/s and applied torques $\boldsymbol{\tau} = [10, 8, 5]^T$ N$\cdot$m.

\subsection{Pass 1: Velocity and Bias}
\label{subsec:pass1_example}

The screw axes (all Z-axis rotations) are:
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{S}_1 &= [0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0]^T \\
  \mathcal{S}_2 &= [0, 0, 1, 0, 0, L_1]^T = [0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1]^T \\
  \mathcal{S}_3 &= [0, 0, 1, 0, 0, L_1 + L_2]^T = [0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1.8]^T
\end{align}

Spatial velocities:
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{V}_1 &= \mathcal{S}_1 \dot{q}_1 = [0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0]^T \times 0.5 = [0, 0, 0.5, 0, 0, 0]^T \\
  \mathcal{V}_2 &= \mathcal{V}_1 + \mathcal{S}_2 \dot{q}_2 = [0, 0, 0.5, 0, 0, 0]^T + [0, 0, -0.3, 0, 0, 1]^T = [0, 0, 0.2, 0, 0, -0.3]^T \\
  \mathcal{V}_3 &= \mathcal{V}_2 + \mathcal{S}_3 \dot{q}_3 = [0, 0, 0.2, 0, 0, -0.3]^T + [0, 0, 0.2, 0, 0, 1.8]^T = [0, 0, 0.4, 0, 0, 1.5]^T
\end{align}

(Note: For planar motion, we extract the Z-component of angular velocity and the X, Y components of linear velocity; a full 6D treatment would track all components.)

Bias forces (assuming no external forces and using the Coriolis bracket):
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_i = \mathcal{I}_i (\dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i-1} + [\mathcal{V}_i, \mathcal{S}_i \dot{q}_i])
\end{equation}

For this simplified example, we set $\mathbf{p}_i = m_i \mathcal{V}_{c,i}$ where $\mathcal{V}_{c,i}$ is the Coriolis acceleration.

\subsection{Pass 2: Articulated Inertia}
\label{subsec:pass2_example}

Starting from the leaf (Link 3) and working backward:

Link 3 (leaf):
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}^A_3 = \mathcal{I}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ \vdots & & \vdots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & m_3 \\ 0 & \cdots & m_3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

(Idealized representation; full $6 \times 6$ matrix in spatial form.)

For Link 2, we apply Equation \ref{eq:aba_inertia_recursion}:

First compute:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}^A_3 \mathcal{S}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ m_3 \times 1.8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1.08 m_3 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

The scalar $\mathcal{S}_3^T \mathcal{I}^A_3 \mathcal{S}_3 = 1.8 \times m_3 \times 1.8 = 3.24 \times 0.6 = 1.944$ (kg$\cdot$m$^2$).

The rank-one correction:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \mathcal{I}_2 = \frac{\mathcal{I}^A_3 \mathcal{S}_3 (\mathcal{I}^A_3 \mathcal{S}_3)^T}{\mathcal{S}_3^T \mathcal{I}^A_3 \mathcal{S}_3}
\end{equation}

Thus:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}^A_2 = \mathcal{I}_2 + \mathcal{I}^A_3 - \Delta \mathcal{I}_2
\end{equation}

Similarly for Link 1, applying the recursion with $\mathcal{I}^A_2$ as the child inertia. The result is a series of $6 \times 6$ matrices representing the effective inertia felt at each joint.

\subsection{Pass 3: Joint Accelerations}
\label{subsec:pass3_example}

For Joint 1, using Equation \ref{eq:aba_joint_acceleration}:
\begin{equation}
  \ddot{q}_1 = \frac{\tau_1 - \mathcal{S}_1^T \mathbf{p}_1 - \mathcal{S}_1^T \mathcal{I}^A_1 \times 0}{\mathcal{S}_1^T \mathcal{I}^A_1 \mathcal{S}_1} = \frac{10 - \mathcal{S}_1^T \mathbf{p}_1}{\mathcal{S}_1^T \mathcal{I}^A_1 \mathcal{S}_1}
\end{equation}

(The base acceleration $\dot{\mathcal{V}}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ for a fixed base.)

Assuming $\mathcal{S}_1^T \mathbf{p}_1 \approx 2$ N$\cdot$m (from Coriolis effects) and $\mathcal{S}_1^T \mathcal{I}^A_1 \mathcal{S}_1 \approx 3$ kg$\cdot$m$^2$ (effective inertia):
\begin{equation}
  \ddot{q}_1 \approx \frac{10 - 2}{3} = \frac{8}{3} \approx 2.67 \text{ rad/s}^2
\end{equation}

Once $\ddot{q}_1$ is known:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathcal{V}}_1 = \dot{\mathcal{V}}_0 + \mathcal{S}_1 \ddot{q}_1 = \mathcal{S}_1 \times 2.67
\end{equation}

For Joint 2:
\begin{equation}
  \ddot{q}_2 = \frac{\tau_2 - \mathcal{S}_2^T \mathbf{p}_2 - \mathcal{S}_2^T \mathcal{I}^A_2 \dot{\mathcal{V}}_1}{\mathcal{S}_2^T \mathcal{I}^A_2 \mathcal{S}_2}
\end{equation}

And similarly for Joint 3. The numerical values depend on the exact inertia matrices, but the pattern is clear: each joint's acceleration is solved sequentially using previously computed values.

\section{Python Worked Example}
\label{sec:python_aba_example}

Here is a Python implementation of the Articulated Body Algorithm:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Articulated Body Algorithm Implementation]
import numpy as np

class ArticulatedBodyRobot:
    """Implements the Articulated Body Algorithm for forward dynamics"""

    def __init__(self, masses, lengths, damping=None):
        """
        Initialize robot with link parameters.
        masses: list of link masses
        lengths: list of link lengths
        damping: joint damping coefficients (optional)
        """
        self.n_links = len(masses)
        self.masses = np.array(masses)
        self.lengths = np.array(lengths)
        self.damping = damping if damping is not None else np.zeros(self.n_links)

        # Spatial inertia for each link (point mass at center)
        self.I_rigid = []
        for i, m in enumerate(masses):
            I = np.zeros((6, 6))
            I[3:6, 3:6] = m * np.eye(3)  # Linear momentum: m * I
            # Rotational inertia (simplified: point mass)
            I[0:3, 0:3] = 1e-6 * np.eye(3)  # Small rotational inertia
            self.I_rigid.append(I)

    def get_screw_axes(self, q):
        """Compute screw axes for the current configuration"""
        S = []
        cum_length = 0
        for i in range(self.n_links):
            # Z-axis rotation at current cumulative position
            screw = np.array([0, 0, 1, 0, 0, cum_length])
            S.append(screw)
            cum_length += self.lengths[i]
        return S

    def pass1_velocities_and_biases(self, q, dq):
        """Pass 1: Outward pass computing velocities and bias forces"""
        V = [np.zeros(6)]  # Base velocity (fixed base)
        S = self.get_screw_axes(q)

        for i in range(self.n_links):
            V_child = V[-1] + S[i] * dq[i]
            V.append(V_child)

        # Compute bias (Coriolis) forces (simplified)
        p = [np.zeros(6)]  # Base bias
        for i in range(self.n_links):
            # Coriolis acceleration (simplified for planar case)
            coriolis = self.masses[i] * np.linalg.norm(V[i+1][:3]) * 0.1
            p_i = np.zeros(6)
            p_i[3:6] = coriolis  # Add to force component
            p.append(p_i)

        return V, p, S

    def pass2_articulated_inertias(self, S):
        """Pass 2: Inward pass computing articulated inertias"""
        I_A = [self.I_rigid[-1].copy()]  # Start with leaf inertia

        for i in range(self.n_links - 2, -1, -1):
            # Articulated inertia recursion
            I_child = I_A[0]
            S_i = S[i+1]

            # Compute components
            IS = I_child @ S_i
            denom = S_i @ I_child @ S_i

            if abs(denom) > 1e-10:
                # Rank-one update
                I_A_i = self.I_rigid[i] + I_child - np.outer(IS, IS) / denom
            else:
                I_A_i = self.I_rigid[i] + I_child

            I_A.insert(0, I_A_i)

        return I_A

    def pass3_accelerations(self, q, dq, tau, I_A, p, S):
        """Pass 3: Outward pass computing joint accelerations"""
        ddq = np.zeros(self.n_links)
        V_dot = np.zeros((self.n_links + 1, 6))  # Link accelerations

        for i in range(self.n_links):
            S_i = S[i]

            # Denominator: effective inertia along joint
            denom = S_i @ I_A[i] @ S_i

            # Numerator: torque minus biases minus parent acceleration effects
            numer = tau[i] - S_i @ p[i+1] - S_i @ I_A[i] @ V_dot[i]

            if abs(denom) > 1e-10:
                ddq[i] = numer / denom
            else:
                ddq[i] = 0

            # Compute link acceleration
            V_dot[i+1] = V_dot[i] + S_i * ddq[i]

        return ddq

    def forward_dynamics(self, q, dq, tau):
        """
        Solve forward dynamics: ddq = ForwardDynamics(q, dq, tau)

        Returns joint accelerations ddq
        """
        # Pass 1: Velocities and bias forces
        V, p, S = self.pass1_velocities_and_biases(q, dq)

        # Pass 2: Articulated inertias
        I_A = self.pass2_articulated_inertias(S)

        # Pass 3: Joint accelerations
        ddq = self.pass3_accelerations(q, dq, tau, I_A, p, S)

        # Add damping
        ddq -= self.damping * dq

        return ddq

# Example: 3-link planar arm
masses = [1.0, 0.8, 0.6]  # kg
lengths = [1.0, 0.8, 0.6]  # m
robot = ArticulatedBodyRobot(masses, lengths, damping=np.array([0.1, 0.1, 0.1]))

# Test configuration
q = np.array([np.pi/4, np.pi/6, -np.pi/3])
dq = np.array([0.5, -0.3, 0.2])
tau = np.array([10, 8, 5])  # Applied torques

ddq = robot.forward_dynamics(q, dq, tau)
print("Joint accelerations (rad/s^2):", np.round(ddq, 4))

# Integration step (simple Euler)
dt = 0.01  # 10 ms timestep
dq_new = dq + ddq * dt
q_new = q + dq * dt

print("Updated joint velocities:", np.round(dq_new, 4))
print("Updated joint positions:", np.round(q_new, 4))
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Contact Forces and Impact Dynamics}
\label{sec:contact_impact}

When a link collides with an obstacle (e.g., a foot striking the ground), an impulsive contact force acts. ABA can be extended to handle contact by:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Detection:} Identify which link has penetrated the environment.

  \item \textbf{Impulse Computation:} Solve for the impulse (integrated force) required to prevent further penetration.

  \item \textbf{Velocity Update:} Update the link's velocity according to the impulse.

  \item \textbf{Continuation:} Run ABA with the updated velocities for the next timestep.
\end{enumerate}

The mathematical framework involves solving a complementarity problem (contact force must be non-negative, and the contact constraint—zero relative acceleration—must hold). Modern solvers use interior-point methods or the Lemke algorithm to solve these efficiently.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:ch10_summary}

The Articulated Body Algorithm is a revolutionary approach to forward dynamics:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{$O(N)$ Complexity:} ABA solves forward dynamics in linear time, versus $O(N^3)$ for matrix inversion—a dramatic speedup for large robots.

  \item \textbf{Three-Pass Structure:}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Outward pass:} Propagate velocities and Coriolis effects.
    \item \textit{Inward pass:} Accumulate articulated inertias from leaves to root.
    \item \textit{Outward pass:} Solve joint accelerations from root to leaves.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Articulated Inertia:} The effective inertia of a body accounting for all downstream bodies, computed via a rank-one update that projects child inertia across the joint constraint.

  \item \textbf{Floating Base:} Extends naturally to robots with 6-DOF roots (humanoids, quadrupeds).

  \item \textbf{Practical Implementation:} All operations are local matrix-vector products and scalar divisions; the algorithm is numerically stable and easy to implement.

  \item \textbf{Real-Time Capability:} ABA powers all modern physics engines, game engines, and robot simulators.
\end{enumerate}
