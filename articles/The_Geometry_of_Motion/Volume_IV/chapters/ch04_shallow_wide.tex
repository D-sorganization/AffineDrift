\section{A Conditional Argument About Speed}

A golf swing joins neural computation to a moving mechanical system. The controller
must act on incomplete, delayed information while muscles, tendons, contacts and
the club continue to evolve. Parallel processing can shorten some computational
paths, and a learned policy can turn an existing state estimate into a command
without solving an optimization problem from scratch. Neither observation proves
that the brain has a universally shallow architecture. The useful question is:
\emph{which new information must influence which output before a specified deadline?}

\subsection{Define the Clock Before Counting Stages}

A response measured at EMG onset, a change in muscle force, and successful recovery
of the club trajectory have different endpoints. A budget must state the stimulus
or perturbation onset, sensory modality, preparation and output landmark. Include
sensory transduction, propagation, processing and the relevant output delays only
once. Electromechanical delay belongs in a force-response budget; it does not
belong after an endpoint defined as EMG onset.

There is no universal 1~ms lower bound for chemical synaptic delay. Katz and
Miledi reported minimum delays of 0.4--0.5~ms at 20$^\circ$C, measured from the
peak presynaptic inward current to the onset of the postsynaptic current at a
neuromuscular junction~\cite{katzMiledi1965review}. Those landmarks and temperature
matter: this is neither a whole sensorimotor-loop latency nor a universal cortical
processing time. Action-potential width, receptor rise time, refractory period,
axonal travel time and input-to-output latency are different measurements.

\begin{definition}{A Stipulated Serial Depth Budget}{depth-budget}
For a serial model with $L$ new processing stages, define
\begin{equation}
 T_{\mathrm{required}}=T_{\mathrm{fixed}}+\sum_{j=1}^{L}\tau_j.
 \label{eq:ch4:depth-budget}
\end{equation}
$T_{\mathrm{fixed}}$ contains endpoint-specific delays excluded from the stage
costs $\tau_j$. If every stage is assigned the same positive cost $\tau$ and
$T_{\mathrm{available}}\geq T_{\mathrm{fixed}}$, then
\[
 L_{\max}=\left\lfloor
 \frac{T_{\mathrm{available}}-T_{\mathrm{fixed}}}{\tau}
 \right\rfloor.
\]
If the available time is smaller than the fixed delay, even zero additional
stages cannot meet this endpoint deadline. An illustrative budget of 50~ms,
20~ms fixed delay and 5~ms per stage permits six stages. These numbers define
a toy calculation; they are not measured golf-response times.
\end{definition}

For a computation with parallel branches, use the longest required dependency
path, including communication and synchronization costs, rather than the sum
of all operations. For recurrence, unroll a \emph{finite correction interval}
with a declared initial state. A feedback loop cannot be assigned a finite
critical path by treating its cyclic graph as an acyclic feedforward network.

\textbf{Throughput is not latency.} A pipeline may produce a command every
millisecond while each command still depends on sensory information acquired
much earlier. Conversely, an existing recurrent state can support a rapid
readout without replaying its entire history. Prediction can exploit predictable
motion, but it cannot incorporate an unpredictable disturbance before information
about that disturbance is available. Thus a fresh correction's latency and the
time used to establish the current policy or state estimate must be distinguished.

\section{Anatomical Layers and Computational Paths}

Cortical laminae describe anatomy; they are not a count of sequential artificial
network transformations. Parallel projections, recurrent connections and multiple
input and output populations prevent a six-lamina description from specifying
six serial computations. Nor does a short response identify every circuit
participating in preparation, ongoing estimation or later correction.

Even the anatomical description needs regional and species qualifications.
Yamawaki and colleagues identified a thin population at the layer~3/5A border in
mouse forelimb M1 with layer-4-like connectivity: thalamic inputs, ascending
local outputs to layers~2/3 and relatively weak long-range outputs
\cite{yamawaki2014review}. This challenges a categorical absence of M1 layer~4;
it does not establish uniform granular anatomy in all motor cortex or a fixed
number of synapses in a human golf-control loop.

\begin{insight}{Architecture as a Testable Model}{arch-is-algo}
Parallel pathways, recurrent state, prediction and hierarchical organization
are candidate ways to coordinate a delayed system. Their benefit depends on
available information, connectivity, learning, energy, communication and the
mechanical task. Neither total neuron count nor histological layer count
specifies the latency or accuracy of a controller. Width also has costs;
its computation and communication cannot be assumed free.
\end{insight}

\section{What a Network Comparison Can Establish}

A static artificial neuron applies an activation function to a weighted sum.
This is a useful mathematical model, not a complete description of a biological
neuron's dendrites, spikes or temporal state. The following example compares
\emph{untrained feedforward mappings}, not successful motor controllers. Both
have 20 inputs, ten signed abstract outputs, ReLU hidden activations and a
linear output. Signed outputs are not bounded muscle activations.

The deep model has nine hidden layers of width~50 followed by ten outputs:
ten affine maps and 21,960 weights and biases. A single hidden layer of width
$h$ followed by ten outputs has $31h+10$ parameters. Choosing $h=708$ gives
21,958, two fewer than the deep model. This is nearly equal parameter count,
not equal expressiveness, training difficulty or task performance. Choosing
width~2000 instead would give 62,010 parameters and would not match that budget.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Parameter Counts and an Assumed Stage Delay}]
import numpy as np

Network = list[tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]]


def make_network(widths: list[int], rng: np.random.Generator) -> Network:
    """Create an untrained toy mapping with zero biases."""
    return [(rng.normal(0, 0.1, (right, left)), np.zeros(right))
            for left, right in zip(widths[:-1], widths[1:], strict=True)]


def parameter_count(model: Network) -> int:
    """Count every weight and bias, including the output map."""
    return sum(weights.size + bias.size for weights, bias in model)


def forward(model: Network, state: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Use ReLU hidden maps and a common linear signed output."""
    hidden = state
    for weights, bias in model[:-1]:
        hidden = np.maximum(0, weights @ hidden + bias)
    weights, bias = model[-1]
    return weights @ hidden + bias


rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
deep_model = make_network([20] + [50] * 9 + [10], rng)
wide_model = make_network([20, 708, 10], rng)
toy_state = rng.normal(size=20)
deep_output = forward(deep_model, toy_state)
wide_output = forward(wide_model, toy_state)
assumed_stage_ms = 5.0  # A declared model cost, not a timing measurement.
assumed_delays_ms = tuple(
    len(model) * assumed_stage_ms for model in (deep_model, wide_model)
)
counts = (parameter_count(deep_model), parameter_count(wide_model))
assert counts == (21960, 21958)
assert assumed_delays_ms == (50.0, 10.0)
\end{lstlisting}

The fivefold delay ratio follows directly from assigning each affine map the
same 5~ms cost, independent of width. It is not a measured software speedup or
a biological result. Actual delays may change with width, hardware, memory
movement and parallel resources. Random outputs establish no tracking accuracy;
a meaningful comparison trains both controllers on a common task and tests
held-out performance under measured input-to-output delays and actuator limits.

\subsection{Approximation Is Not a Control Guarantee}

Cybenko's theorem establishes uniform approximation of continuous functions on
a compact unit cube by sufficiently many units with a continuous sigmoidal
activation~\cite{cybenko1989review}. It does not supply a small required width,
a successful training algorithm or closed-loop stability. That specific theorem
also does not analyze the ReLU listing above. Approximating a state-to-command
map on a bounded domain is different from reconstructing unavailable state or
controlling trajectories that leave that domain. An approximate policy can
perform poorly near constraints even when a global average error is small.

\subsection{Hierarchy and Information Loss}

Suppose a model deliberately assigns dimensions $n_l=n_0/r^l$, $r>1$, to
levels $l=0,\ldots,L$, and each level uses a dense square map with cost
proportional to $n_l^2$. Treat these dimensions as idealized; an implementation
must choose integers. The algebraic cost is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{l=0}^{L}n_l^2
 =n_0^2\frac{1-r^{-2(L+1)}}{1-r^{-2}}.
 \label{eq:ch4:hierarchical-dim}
\end{equation}
For fixed $r>1$ this is bounded as $L$ increases, but still scales as
$\mathcal{O}(n_0^2)$. The calculation assumes the representation and the square
maps; it does not include learning or communication costs, nor eliminate
exponential state-space sampling or approximation complexity in general.

Compression must preserve information needed for the task. Two states with the
same position $q$ and opposite velocities $v$ need opposite damping commands
$u=-kv$, $k>0$. A position-only representation maps them to the same value and
cannot produce both correct commands. A history-dependent estimator might
recover velocity, but then the retained history or estimator state is part of
the controller. Hierarchy helps only when its chosen representations and
interfaces preserve the distinctions needed downstream.

\section{Synergies: Reconstruction and Interpretation}

For a nonnegative processed EMG vector $\bm e(t)$, a synchronous spatial model is
\begin{equation}
 \bm e(t)\approx W\bm c(t),\qquad
 W\in\Reals_{\geq0}^{p\times k},\quad
 \bm c(t)\in\Reals_{\geq0}^{k}.
 \label{eq:ch4:synergy-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The residual matters. EMG is a measurement of electrical activity, not a direct
identity with a neural control input, activation state, muscle force or joint
torque. Time-varying synergy templates are a different model: temporal structure
belongs to the templates as well as their recruitment and timing.

d'Avella and Bizzi recorded 13 hindlimb muscles in three frogs during jumping,
swimming and walking. Five components gave average reconstruction $R^2$ values
of 0.89 for synchronous synergies and 0.80 for time-varying synergies
\cite{dAvellaBizzi2005}. Those results concern particular data, preprocessing
and model definitions; they do not establish a universal four-to-six-synergy,
over-90\% rule for human golf. The two representations also differ in
parameterization, so component count alone is not a capacity comparison.

Tresch and colleagues compared factorization methods with simulated data and
withdrawal EMG from frogs after spinalization. Recovery depended on assumptions about noise
and the factors; different criteria could suggest different model orders
\cite{Tresch2006}. Agreement across methods can strengthen a description of
data structure, but cannot identify its unique neural generator. For example,
$W D$ and $D^{-1}\bm c$ give the same reconstruction for any positive diagonal
$D$, and component permutations also leave the product unchanged. Further
ambiguities can remain. Task restrictions, mechanics and processing choices
must be considered alongside a neural-module interpretation.

\subsection{A Reproducible Factorization Example}

Let rows of $X$ index time and columns index channels, so $X\approx CW^T$.
The following nonnegative least-squares example uses multiplicative updates
\cite{leeSeung2000review}. The joint optimization is not convex. The ideal
updates' nonincreasing objective does not guarantee a global optimum, unique
factors or exact floating-point monotonicity in this implementation.

Define the \emph{uncentered reconstruction score}
\[
 S=1-\frac{\lVert X-CW^T\rVert_F^2}{\lVert X\rVert_F^2}.
\]
This convention is sometimes called uncentered variance accounted for. It is
not centered statistical $R^2$; it is at most one, may be negative, and is
undefined for an all-zero dataset. A high training score alone neither selects
a unique rank nor establishes out-of-sample performance.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Validated Nonnegative Factorization of Synthetic Channels}]
import numpy as np


def positive_integer(value: int, name: str) -> None:
    """Reject zero, fractional values and booleans for count options."""
    if (isinstance(value, (bool, np.bool_))
            or not isinstance(value, (int, np.integer)) or value <= 0):
        raise ValueError(f"{name} must be a positive integer")


def extract_synergies(
    emg_data: np.ndarray, n_synergies: int,
    max_iter: int = 500, tol: float = 1e-6
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, float]:
    """Return W, C and uncentered score for finite nonnegative data.

    W: channels by rank; C: samples by rank. Reconstruct with C @ W.T.
    Rows are samples; columns are channels. Zero data are rejected.
    This teaching example uses one reproducible local random start.
    """
    data = np.asarray(emg_data, dtype=float)
    if (data.ndim != 2 or data.size == 0
            or not np.all(np.isfinite(data)) or np.any(data < 0)):
        raise ValueError("data must be finite, nonnegative and nonempty 2D")
    positive_integer(n_synergies, "n_synergies")
    positive_integer(max_iter, "max_iter")
    if not np.isfinite(tol) or tol < 0:
        raise ValueError("tol must be finite and nonnegative")
    scale = np.max(data)
    if scale == 0:
        raise ValueError("relative reconstruction score needs nonzero data")
    normalized = data / scale
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    weights = rng.uniform(0.1, 1.0, (data.shape[1], n_synergies))
    coefficients = rng.uniform(0.1, 1.0, (data.shape[0], n_synergies))
    denominator_floor = np.finfo(float).tiny
    total = np.sum(normalized**2)
    previous_score = -np.inf
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        coefficients *= (normalized @ weights) / np.maximum(
            coefficients @ (weights.T @ weights), denominator_floor)
        weights *= (normalized.T @ coefficients) / np.maximum(
            weights @ (coefficients.T @ coefficients), denominator_floor)
        residual = normalized - coefficients @ weights.T
        score = float(1 - np.sum(residual**2) / total)
        if abs(score - previous_score) < tol:
            break
        previous_score = score
    # Fit normalized data; split its original scale across factors.
    return weights * np.sqrt(scale), coefficients * np.sqrt(scale), score


rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
true_weights = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, (8, 3))
time = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 200)
true_coefficients = np.maximum(
    0, np.sin(time[:, None] + np.arange(3) * 2 * np.pi / 3)
)
# Arbitrary channels and positive-biased synthetic noise, not walking data.
synthetic = (true_coefficients @ true_weights.T
             + 0.05 * np.abs(rng.normal(size=(200, 8))))
reconstruction_scores = [extract_synergies(synthetic, rank)[2]
                         for rank in range(1, 7)]
\end{lstlisting}

The three generating factors are a construction, not an experimental discovery.
Channel numbers have no assigned muscle anatomy. Additional fitted components
can fit noise; rank inferred from a training-score threshold need not equal the
generating count. The Gram-matrix parentheses avoid constructing a channel-by-sample
matrix in the weight update; the benefit depends on sample count and rank.

For an empirical study, compare explicitly recorded random starts, preprocessing
and model orders. Fit normalization and the basis on training data only; for
held-out reconstruction, keep the basis fixed and estimate only permitted
coefficients. Distinguish that reconstruction from forecasting future EMG.
Check robustness across trials, tasks and subjects. The listing's one fixed seed
is reproducibility for a demonstration, not such a model-selection study.

\section{From This Hypothesis to a Golf Experiment}

A useful swing model connects mechanical state, actuator state and controller
state. Joint and club positions alone generally omit velocity, muscle activation,
tendon deformation and relevant history. Internal models (Chapter~\ref{ch:internal-models})
can estimate missing state and predict its evolution. Passive mechanics
(Chapter~\ref{ch:passive-control}) begin responding before a new neural command
arrives, while activation and mechanical history change the available force.
The coupled-state treatment in Chapter~\ref{ch:biology-nonlinear} explains why
local mechanical response, whole-body stability and task accuracy are separate
questions.

Ground-force, hand-motion and club-speed peaks are outputs of that coupled
system. Their ordering does not count neural layers, and a compact description
of their coordination does not uniquely identify the controller. A controller
that uses fewer freshly evaluated stages could reduce delay under a particular
implementation; a deeper or recurrent controller might compensate with better
estimation or prediction. These alternatives must be tested at the same task,
information access and physical constraints.

\begin{insight}{The Shallow-Wide Hypothesis}{shallow-wide-hypothesis}
When a correction requires new information under a tight deadline, reducing the
critical path can help \emph{if} the resulting representation preserves the
information and policy accuracy needed for the task. Test this conditional
claim with measured latency, trained controllers, held-out perturbations and
matched actuator limits. It is an engineering hypothesis, not a universal
layer-count law or proof of a human neural mechanism.
\end{insight}

A discriminating experiment varies perturbation predictability, sensing delay,
available state variables and controller architecture separately. Report
input-to-command and input-to-force latencies, tracking error, delivered club
state, contact feasibility and robustness. Compare parameter count alongside
actual compute and communication costs. A benefit under those conditions can
support a model choice; translating it to human control requires corresponding
physiological measurements and causal tests.

\section*{Exercises}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{Endpoint and Budget.} For the stipulated 50/20/5~ms example,
 compute the allowable stage count. Repeat with 15~ms available. Explain
 why neither answer estimates the number of synapses in a golfer.
 \item \textbf{Count and Measure.} Verify both parameter counts, then measure
 runtime on a declared machine. Explain why the measured ratio need not be
 five and why untrained outputs cannot rank control performance.
 \item \textbf{Lost State.} Construct two equal-position states requiring
 opposite damping commands. Identify the additional information a compressed
 controller or its estimator must retain.
 \item \textbf{Factorization.} Reconstruct the synthetic channels at ranks one
 through six. Rescale or permute fitted factors without changing their product.
 Explain what training score, held-out reconstruction and neural identification
 each establish.
 \item \textbf{Controlled Comparison.} Design a reaching or golf simulation
 with identical sensing and actuator constraints. Separate a predictable
 disturbance from an unannounced one; compare trained policies with measured
 delays and state explicitly what would remain untested in humans.
\end{enumerate}
