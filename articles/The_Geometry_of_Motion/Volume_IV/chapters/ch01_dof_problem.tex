\chapter[The Degrees-of-Freedom Problem]{The Degrees-of-Freedom\\Problem}
\label{ch:dof-problem}

A successful swing is an outcome of coordinated motion, forces and contact.
It does not specify a unique joint trajectory or muscle history.
The question associated with Bernstein's account of coordination
\cite{Bernstein1967} is how a body with many available motions produces a
repeatable task outcome while remaining adaptable.
Answering it requires distinguishing three things: the geometry of equivalent
outcomes, the dynamics that make a movement feasible, and the evidence from
which a controller might be inferred.

\section{Bernstein's Problem as a Modeling Question}

\begin{definition}{The Degrees-of-Freedom Problem}{dof-problem}
Let $\mathcal Q$ be an $n$-dimensional configuration manifold and let a local
chart be denoted by $\bm q$.
Choose a task map $h:\mathcal Q\to\Reals^k$ and a desired value $\bm y_0$.
The configurations satisfying that task form the level set
$\mathcal M_{\bm y_0}=\{\bm q:h(\bm q)=\bm y_0\}$.
Where the task Jacobian has constant rank $r$ in a neighborhood, the local level-set dimension
is $n-r$; it is $n-k$ only for $k$ independent task constraints.
This counts local configuration freedom, not feasible control histories.
\end{definition}

A seven-coordinate arm with a regular three-dimensional endpoint-position
map has four local task-null directions.
If a locally independent six-dimensional pose is specified instead, it has
one. These are illustrative model counts, not anatomical measurements.
Dependent constraints must not be counted twice, and joint limits can remove
available motion even when a Jacobian has a nontrivial kernel.
At a singular configuration, rank can change and a tangent-kernel count alone
need not describe the dimension of an exact solution set.

\subsection{Configuration Freedom and Muscle Allocation}

At a fixed posture, a simplified muscle-force allocation model writes
\begin{equation}
 R(\bm q)\bm F=\bm\tau_{\mathrm{req}},\qquad
 0\leq F_i\leq F_{i,\max},
 \label{eq:ch1:force-allocation}
\end{equation}
where $R\in\Reals^{n\times p}$ is a signed moment-arm matrix, $\bm F$ contains
$p$ tensile forces, and $\bm\tau_{\mathrm{req}}$ is the required generalized
load after other modeled contributions have been separated.
If the equality is feasible, its unconstrained affine solution space has
dimension $p-\operatorname{rank}R$, not automatically $p-n$.
Force limits restrict it to a feasible set that may contain a continuum, one
point, or no solution. Activations and excitations are distinct variables:
force also depends on muscle length, velocity, tendon state and history.

For example, use a dimensionless toy map
\[
 R=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
 \bm\tau_{\mathrm{req}}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\3\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The equality gives $\bm F=(3-t,3-t,t)^T$.
Bounds $0\leq F_i\leq3$ leave $0\leq t\leq3$; imposing $F_{3,\max}=0$
leaves only $(3,3,0)^T$.
A nullity of one therefore does not guarantee compensatory capacity.
Configuration and force nullspaces act on different physical variables;
adding their dimensions does not generally yield a trajectory redundancy.
The familiar arithmetic $p-k$ can describe a particular regular algebraic
model with $n+p$ variables and $n+k$ independent equalities, but those
assumptions must be established rather than inferred from anatomical counts.

A movement must additionally satisfy equations of motion, contact constraints,
actuator dynamics and boundary conditions.
A kinematically equivalent posture can change inertial coupling, moment arms,
force capacity, energy use and the response to the next perturbation.
This connects redundancy to the muscle-state and load-accounting treatment
in Volume III rather than providing an alternative to it.

\subsection{What Is the Task in a Golf Swing?}

Six pose coordinates describe a local rigid-club position and orientation.
They do not specify clubhead velocity, the face contact point, collision
conditions or the resulting ball flight.
For a swing outcome, a more useful terminal map can depend on configuration,
velocity and implement state: $\bm y=g(\bm x(T))$.
The state $\bm x$ may include shaft deformation and internal actuator states;
$T$ and the definition of impact must also be specified.
A Jacobian of this state map has a different domain from $J_h(\bm q)$.

Consequently, there is no universal ``200 coordinates minus six constraints''
calculation for golf, nor a single neural delay that determines the available
controller dimension. Model resolution, task tolerance, contact mode and
measurement bandwidth determine what a chosen count means.
A small variation in club position can coexist with a consequential change
in delivery velocity or face orientation.
Coordination should be evaluated against the declared outcome and its
uncertainty, not solely against visual similarity of joint motion.

\section{From Redundancy to Motor Abundance}

\begin{insight}{Redundancy as Motor Abundance}{abundance}
Extra feasible solutions can provide alternatives when conditions change.
They may allow a golfer to preserve an outcome while changing joint loading,
or a humanoid to avoid an obstacle while maintaining a grasp.
This is an opportunity conditional on available force, motion and information;
a high-dimensional model alone guarantees neither robustness nor learning.
\end{insight}

Latash's abundance perspective emphasizes families of useful solutions
rather than the elimination of every additional degree of freedom
\cite{Latash2012}.
That interpretation motivates examining task-preserving covariation.
It is not a proof of one neural implementation or a rejection of every
optimization model: a feedback policy can also permit many realizations.
Variability that is irrelevant to one output may matter to fatigue,
contact stability, another objective, or a later time.

\section{The Uncontrolled Manifold Hypothesis}

The UCM approach compares observed variability with directions that locally
preserve a selected task variable \cite{ScholzSchoner1999}.
Its original sit-to-stand study tested alternative task hypotheses in nine
participants; that evidence does not establish a universal golf controller.
Here ``uncontrolled'' denotes a proposed relative pattern of stabilization,
not an uncontrollable subspace of a dynamical system.
Reachability concerns which states inputs can attain; a task kernel concerns
which instantaneous variations an output fails to distinguish.

\begin{definition}{The Uncontrolled Manifold}{ucm}
Distinguish the exact level set $\mathcal M_{\bm y_0}$ from its local linear
approximation. At a regular point $\bm q_0$ with $h(\bm q_0)=\bm y_0$,
\begin{equation}
 T_{\bm q_0}\mathcal M_{\bm y_0}
 =\ker J_h(\bm q_0)
 =\{\delta\bm q:J_h(\bm q_0)\delta\bm q=\bm0\}.
 \label{eq:ch1:ucm-def}
\end{equation}
The kernel is commonly called the linearized UCM.
It preserves the task to first order; it is not generally the exact curved
set of finite task-preserving displacements.
\end{definition}

Taylor expansion makes the limitation explicit:
\begin{equation}
 h(\bm q_0+\delta\bm q)-h(\bm q_0)
 =J_h\delta\bm q+
 \tfrac12 D^2h[\delta\bm q,\delta\bm q]
 +\mathcal O(\|\delta\bm q\|^3).
 \label{eq:ch1:curvature}
\end{equation}
This expression assumes sufficient local smoothness and a common coordinate
chart. A null first-order term leaves the curvature term.
For $h(q_1,q_2)=q_1^2+q_2^2$ at $(1,0)$, $(0,\epsilon)$ is tangent but gives
$h(1,\epsilon)=1+\epsilon^2$.
At $\epsilon=0.1$ the task changes by $0.01$; the exact nearby solution is
$(\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2},\epsilon)$.
At the singular point $(0,0)$, by contrast, $J_h=0$ while the zero level set
contains only that point. A two-dimensional kernel is not a two-dimensional
family of exact zero-task solutions there.

\begin{laymansbox}{Mathematical Reminder: The Geometry of the UCM}{ucm_geometry_reminder}
A tangent gives the direction of a road at one point, not the road's whole
path. Following the tangent in a straight line eventually leaves a curved
road. Similarly, tangent-aligned variations can still change a nonlinear task.
Maintaining the exact task requires following the level set or correcting
its finite error; a covariance ellipse alone does not provide that mechanism.
\end{laymansbox}

\subsection{Coordinates, Metrics and Numerical Rank}

Choose a chart and coordinate scales before saying that two directions are
orthogonal. With positive diagonal scales $D$, analyze
$\delta\bm z=D^{-1}\delta\bm q$ and $J_z=J_hD$.
Euclidean orthogonality in $\bm z$ corresponds to the metric
$D^{-T}D^{-1}$ in $\bm q$.
Mixing metres and radians without a declared scale makes a variance ratio
ambiguous; even changing one coordinate's scale changes that metric.
Joint angles crossing a branch cut must be unwrapped or treated in an
appropriate local chart before taking an arithmetic mean.

For the chosen scaled Jacobian, an SVD supplies orthonormal bases $N$ for
$\ker J_z$ and $B$ for $\operatorname{range}(J_z^T)$.
The projectors are $P_N=NN^T$ and $P_B=BB^T$, with $P_N+P_B=I$.
Numerical rank depends on a stated singular-value tolerance.
The implementation below uses a relative tolerance, so multiplying the whole
Jacobian by a nonzero scalar does not change rank in ordinary finite precision.
For heterogeneous outputs, task scaling and near-singular directions still
require an explicit sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Variance Is a Statistic, Not a Mechanism}

Let $N_s\geq2$ repeated observations be aligned at a declared event or phase,
with scaled deviations $\bm d_i=\bm z_i-\bar{\bm z}$.
Evaluate the task Jacobian at the corresponding mean configuration.
Using sample covariance $C=(N_s-1)^{-1}\sum_i\bm d_i\bm d_i^T$, define
\begin{equation}
 V_N=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(P_NC)}{n-r},\qquad
 V_B=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(P_BC)}{r},\qquad
 \rho=\frac{V_N}{V_B}.
 \label{eq:ch1:variance}
\end{equation}
These expressions require $0<r<n$ and $V_B>0$ for a finite ratio.
A zero-dimensional group has no per-dimension variance; $0/0$ is not evidence
of perfect stabilization. The function returns \texttt{None} for these
undefined quantities.
The $N_s-1$ covariance convention is unbiased for independent, identically
distributed observations with finite covariance; it does not make correlated
samples, pooled participants or phase-misaligned swings independent trials.

A value $\rho>1$ describes greater variance per dimension along the chosen
linearized task-null directions. Isotropic covariance gives $\rho=1$ when
both groups exist. A sample ratio needs uncertainty analysis and appropriate
comparisons; it is not a stand-alone significance test.
Also, the complementary variance does not equal task-output variance:
to first order the latter is $J_zCJ_z^T$, weighted by task sensitivities.
Changing phase alignment, coordinate scales, measurement errors or the task
map can change the interpretation.

A manufactured counterexample demonstrates the inference limit.
For a stable linear stochastic model $d\bm z=A\bm z\,dt+L\,d\bm W$,
let $\bm W$ be standard Brownian motion and write $Q=LL^T$.
Stationary covariance satisfies $AC+CA^T+Q=0$.
Both
\[
 C=\operatorname{diag}(1,9),\quad
 (A_1,Q_1)=(-I,2C),\quad
 (A_2,Q_2)=(-\operatorname{diag}(9,1),18I)
\]
satisfy this equation.
For task Jacobian $(1,0)$, both give $\rho=9$.
The first has isotropic restoring dynamics and anisotropic noise; the second
has directional restoring dynamics and isotropic noise.
Thus identical covariance cannot identify which mechanism generated it.
Controlled perturbations, temporal information and independent noise models
can help discriminate hypotheses. This algebra is not a fitted neural model.

\section{A Reproducible Reaching Example}

Run this listing from the repository root with its pinned Python dependencies.
The tested implementation in \texttt{src/affine\_control/ucm\_analysis.py}
validates array shape, finite real values, rank tolerance and numerical range.
Its input is a fixed Jacobian, so the caller owns the task model and its
linearization point. The three-link arm below uses relative joint angles in
radians and link lengths in metres, with Euclidean joint-angle scaling.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Manufactured Reaching Variance and Finite Task Error}]
import numpy as np
from src.affine_control.ucm_analysis import ucm_variance


def planar_endpoint(angles, lengths):
    headings = np.cumsum(angles, axis=-1)
    x = np.sum(lengths * np.cos(headings), axis=-1)
    y = np.sum(lengths * np.sin(headings), axis=-1)
    return np.stack((x, y), axis=-1)


def planar_jacobian(angles, lengths):
    headings = np.cumsum(angles)
    terms = np.array([-lengths * np.sin(headings),
                     lengths * np.cos(headings)])
    return np.cumsum(terms[:, ::-1], axis=1)[:, ::-1]


nominal = np.array([0.5, 1.2, -0.3])
lengths = np.array([0.3, 0.25, 0.2])
jacobian = planar_jacobian(nominal, lengths)
_, _, vt = np.linalg.svd(jacobian, full_matrices=True)
basis = np.column_stack((vt[2:].T, vt[:2].T))
# Six symmetric observations: sample mean = nominal.
# N-1 = 5; paired axes have covariance diag(sigma**2).
sigma = np.array([0.03, 0.01, 0.01])
axes = np.sqrt(2.5) * np.diag(sigma)
deviations = np.vstack((axes, -axes)) @ basis.T
result = ucm_variance(nominal + deviations, jacobian)
iso_axes = np.sqrt(2.5) * 0.01 * np.eye(3)
iso_data = nominal + np.vstack((iso_axes, -iso_axes))
isotropic = ucm_variance(iso_data, jacobian)
step = 0.03 * vt[2]
null_step_linear_error = np.linalg.norm(jacobian @ step)
null_step_actual_error = np.linalg.norm(
    planar_endpoint(nominal + step, lengths)
    - planar_endpoint(nominal, lengths))
print(result)             # VN = 0.0009, VB = 0.0001, ratio = 9
print(isotropic.ratio)    # 1, apart from roundoff
print(null_step_actual_error)  # nonzero despite J @ step ~ 0
\end{lstlisting}

The larger null variance is deliberately constructed, not discovered in
experimental data. Six deterministic points demonstrate covariance accounting;
they do not estimate human variability or provide a statistical confidence
interval. The finite endpoint residual exposes the linearization limit.
The tests compare the printed Jacobian with Cartesian finite differences and
verify both the anisotropic example and its isotropic control.

\section{Learning, Constraints and Passive Dynamics}

Freezing, freeing and exploiting degrees of freedom are useful questions to
bring to descriptions of skill acquisition associated with Bernstein's
framework \cite{Bernstein1967}; they should not be imposed as a universal
three-stage law or a fixed sequence for every golfer.
A joint moving through a small range is not thereby absent from the model.
True mechanical locking, increased stiffness, correlated motion and a changed
control policy have different effects on the system's feasible states.
None implies that configuration dimension becomes exactly the task dimension.

Likewise, wrist release is a coupled dynamical event involving geometry,
applied loads and joint/implement mechanics; naming a centrifugal effect does
not establish zero muscle action or a unique expert mechanism.
A control-affine model $\dot{\bm x}=f(\bm x)+G(\bm x)\bm u$ can describe the
interaction of drift and inputs under stated assumptions.
Reachability depends on that complete system and its admissible controls
\cite{AgrachevSachkov2004}.
Removing a physical constraint can add feasible trajectories when the old
ones remain admissible in the enlarged model, but merely observing more joint
motion proves neither a larger reachable set nor better task performance.

For golf and humanoid movement, the useful chain is therefore explicit:
choose the outcome; identify its local geometry; impose the coupled physical
constraints; then test candidate explanations against repeated observations
and perturbations. This preserves the insight of motor abundance while
keeping kinematic equivalence, dynamic feasibility and neural inference distinct.
