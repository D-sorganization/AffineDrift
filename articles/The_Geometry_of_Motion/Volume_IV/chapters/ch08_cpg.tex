\chapter{Central Pattern Generators}
\label{ch:cpg}

A rhythmic command generator can simplify the description of repeated movement.
Its existence does not establish that a body driven by it walks stably, or that
a finite golf swing is generated by the same biological circuit. We distinguish
neural evidence, a rate-model oscillator, phase coordination and mechanical
execution before connecting them.

\section{What Biological Evidence Establishes}

Central pattern generators (CPGs) are neural circuits capable of generating
patterned rhythmic output without requiring a matching rhythmic input. Tonic
excitation or neuromodulation may still be necessary. Sensory feedback and
descending signals can initiate, modify or reset the pattern; ``central'' does
not mean isolated from the rest of the behaving system.

Cohen and Wall\'en recorded organized ventral-root bursting in isolated lamprey
spinal cord after adding agents such as D-glutamate or L-DOPA to the bathing
solution\cite{CohenWallen1980}. This preparation supports central generation of
a swimming-like neural pattern. It does not contain a freely swimming animal,
prove a particular two-unit model, or establish a human golf-swing CPG.
Inferring the source of a rhythm from a moving animal also requires separating
central generation from mechanically induced and sensory-driven oscillation.

\section{A Two-Unit Rate Model}

The Matsuoka family of models combines inhibition and adaptation
\cite{Matsuoka1985}. In the symmetric two-unit example below,
\begin{align}
 \tau_1\dot x_i&=-x_i-\beta v_i-w y_j+u,\quad j\ne i,\\
 \tau_2\dot v_i&=-v_i+y_i,\qquad y_i=\max(0,x_i).
 \label{eq:ch8:matsuoka}
\end{align}
$x_i,v_i,y_i,u$ are normalized rate-model quantities; $x_i$ is not a calibrated
membrane voltage. Time constants $\tau_1,\tau_2$ are positive and have time units;
$\beta,w,u\geq0$. The rectifier is Lipschitz continuous, though not differentiable
at zero. These equations define a well-posed ODE for any such fixed finite
parameters, but not every choice produces sustained oscillation. They omit
spiking, conductance dynamics and sensory feedback.

For $u>0$, a symmetric equilibrium always exists:
\begin{equation}
 x_1=x_2=v_1=v_2=x_*=\frac{u}{1+\beta+w}>0.
\end{equation}
In this positive region, sum and difference perturbations decouple. Their
linearized matrices are
\begin{equation}
 J_+=\begin{pmatrix}-(1+w)/\tau_1&-\beta/\tau_1\\1/\tau_2&-1/\tau_2\end{pmatrix},
 \quad
 J_-=\begin{pmatrix}(w-1)/\tau_1&-\beta/\tau_1\\1/\tau_2&-1/\tau_2\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The sum mode is locally stable. For the difference mode,
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{tr}J_-=\frac{w-1}{\tau_1}-\frac{1}{\tau_2},\qquad
 \det J_-=\frac{1+\beta-w}{\tau_1\tau_2}.
\end{equation}
Thus $1+\tau_1/\tau_2<w<1+\beta$ makes this equilibrium unstable to
antisymmetric perturbations. This local calculation alone is not a proof of a
unique attracting cycle in a piecewise-linear four-state system. An exactly
symmetric initial condition stays in the symmetric subspace and need not enter
an alternating rhythm. With $w=0$ the uncoupled positive equilibria are stable.
For $w>1+\beta$, a state with one active unit and the other suppressed is also
possible. Inhibition plus adaptation is therefore a mechanism to investigate,
not a universal guarantee of alternation.

\subsection{Amplitude and Time Scaling}

Because $\max(0,cx)=c\max(0,x)$ for $c>0$, simultaneously scaling the drive and
initial state by $c$ scales the entire state trajectory by $c$. Its time
dependence is unchanged. If that trajectory is periodic, its period is unchanged
too. This disproves a generic claim that increasing tonic input must increase
frequency in this model. Changing drive alone with a fixed initial condition
can change transients, and nonlinear saturation or feedback can break the scaling.

Scaling both time constants by $s>0$ instead produces
$z_s(t)=z(t/s)$ from the same initial state. A periodic trajectory's frequency
then scales by $1/s$. Changing only the ratio of time constants is a different
operation: it changes the vector field and can alter waveform or stability.

\subsection{A Checked Simulation}

The listing returns all four states at caller-supplied times, including the final
time. It checks parameter and input domains and reports integration failure.
An asymmetric initial state is deliberate. A simulation or a zero-crossing count
is not a proof of a limit cycle: inspect late-time waveform convergence and
perturbation recovery, refine tolerances and compare sections across cycles.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={A Normalized Two-Unit Rate Model}]
from dataclasses import dataclass

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class MatsuokaParameters:
    """Time constants in seconds; other parameters normalized."""

    tau: tuple[float, float] = (0.1, 0.3)
    adaptation: float = 2.5
    inhibition: float = 2.0
    drive: float = 1.0

    def __post_init__(self) -> None:
        times = np.asarray(self.tau, dtype=float)
        gains = np.asarray(
            [self.adaptation, self.inhibition, self.drive], dtype=float
        )
        if times.shape != (2,) or not np.isfinite(times).all():
            raise ValueError("tau must contain two finite times")
        if np.any(times <= 0) or not np.isfinite(gains).all():
            raise ValueError("Positive times and finite gains required")
        if np.any(gains < 0):
            raise ValueError("Gains and drive must be nonnegative")
        object.__setattr__(self, "tau", tuple(times))


def checked_state(state: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Return a finite four-state copy in x1, v1, x2, v2 order."""
    result = np.array(state, dtype=float, copy=True)
    if result.shape != (4,) or not np.isfinite(result).all():
        raise ValueError("state must contain four finite values")
    return result


class MatsuokaOscillator:
    """Integrate the declared rate model, without a plant or contacts."""

    def __init__(self, parameters: MatsuokaParameters = MatsuokaParameters()) -> None:
        self.parameters = parameters

    def dynamics(self, time: float, state: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        x1, v1, x2, v2 = checked_state(state)
        param = self.parameters
        y1, y2 = max(0.0, x1), max(0.0, x2)
        dx1 = (-x1 - param.adaptation*v1 - param.inhibition*y2 + param.drive)
        dx2 = (-x2 - param.adaptation*v2 - param.inhibition*y1 + param.drive)
        return np.array([dx1/param.tau[0], (-v1+y1)/param.tau[1],
                         dx2/param.tau[0], (-v2+y2)/param.tau[1]])

    def simulate(self, times: np.ndarray, initial: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """Return states at increasing finite times beginning at zero."""
        grid = np.asarray(times, dtype=float)
        start = checked_state(initial)
        if grid.ndim != 1 or grid.size < 2 or not np.isfinite(grid).all():
            raise ValueError("At least two finite times required")
        if grid[0] != 0 or np.any(np.diff(grid) <= 0):
            raise ValueError("Times must increase strictly from zero")
        solution = solve_ivp(self.dynamics, (0.0, grid[-1]), start,
                             t_eval=grid, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
        if not solution.success or not np.isfinite(solution.y).all():
            raise RuntimeError("Rate-model integration failed")
        return solution.y


times = np.linspace(0.0, 5.0, 1001)
states = MatsuokaOscillator().simulate(times, np.array([0.1, 0, -0.1, 0]))
output = np.maximum(states[0], 0) - np.maximum(states[2], 0)
\end{lstlisting}

For a sampled period estimate, use crossings in a single direction, interpolate
crossing times, and discard a declared transient interval. Require several
cycles and examine period and amplitude variation. A decaying oscillation can
cross zero repeatedly; a plateau at zero can create duplicate sign transitions.
Neither observation alone demonstrates a sustained rhythm.

\section{Phase Coordination and Its Limits}

A separate phase model is
\begin{equation}
 \dot\phi_i=\omega_i+\sum_j K_{ij}
 \sin(\phi_j-\phi_i-\psi_{ij}).
 \label{eq:ch8:phase-coupling}
\end{equation}
Angles are in radians, $\omega_i$ in radians per unit time and $K_{ij}$ in inverse
time (radians dimensionless). To prescribe relative phases $\alpha_i$, define
$\psi_{ij}=\alpha_j-\alpha_i$. For identical $\omega_i=\omega$, the trajectory
$\phi_i=\omega t+\alpha_i$ then has zero coupling error. With symmetric positive
weights on a connected graph, the linearized phase-error dynamics are
$\dot\eta=-L\eta$, where $L$ is its graph Laplacian. Relative phases decay
locally; the common phase remains neutral. This is a local coordination result,
not a global synchronization or mechanical stability theorem.

For two oscillators with zero bias and coupling $K>0$, define
$\delta=\phi_2-\phi_1$ and $\Delta\omega=\omega_2-\omega_1$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \dot\delta=\Delta\omega-2K\sin\delta.
\end{equation}
A locked phase exists only if $|\Delta\omega|\leq2K$; the branch with
$\cos\delta>0$ is locally attracting. Equality is nonhyperbolic and does not
give the same exponential stability conclusion. Phase difference is not a fixed
time delay when frequency changes.

For the explicit leg order (left fore, right fore, left hind, right hind),
$\alpha=(0,\pi,\pi,0)$ synchronizes diagonal pairs, a trot-like phase pattern;
$(0,\pi,0,\pi)$ gives a pace-like pattern; $(0,0,\pi,\pi)$ gives a bound-like
pattern. The last is not a generic gallop. These vectors specify timing only.
Foot trajectories, duty factors, contact forces, ground clearance, actuator
limits and perturbation recovery are still needed to establish an actual gait.

\section{From Rhythm to Golf Mechanics}

A finite swing can be represented using a gated oscillator or a phase-indexed
trajectory as an engineering choice. That does not identify a biological CPG.
Ordering peaks of pelvis, torso, arm and club velocities is also not sufficient
to identify coupled phase oscillators or prove energy transfer. The plant's
inertia, elastic storage, constraints and applied forces must satisfy their own
equations, as in Chapter~\ref{ch:passive-control}.

Compare rhythmic and finite-motion policies with the same mechanical model,
actuator budget and feedback information. Assess impact outcomes and
perturbation recovery. A plausible rhythm alone is insufficient.
