\chapter{Ideomotor Planning}
\label{ch:ideomotor}

An intended effect, a prediction of an effect and an observed effect play different
roles. A golfer may intend a particular launch direction, predict another from the
current club state, and observe a third after impact. Collapsing those quantities
into one ``prediction error'' hides the difference between selecting a goal,
estimating the state and learning from a miss. This chapter connects the ideas
while keeping their mathematical and evidential roles explicit.

\section{Action Effects and the Scope of Event Coding}

Ideomotor theories give anticipated consequences a role in selecting actions.
The Theory of Event Coding (TEC) proposes a shared representational format for
perceived events and planned actions: task-relevant features can be integrated
into event codes\cite{Hommel2001}. It concerns the cognitive relationship between
perception and action planning. It does not by itself specify muscle recruitment,
joint torques, a state estimator or a feedback law.

An action-effect association can be studied by changing which sensory effect
follows an action and testing how the learned effect influences later selection.
Support for such an association does not uniquely establish a predictive-coding
circuit, a Bayesian algorithm or a particular neural implementation. TEC's
``distal'' description is also not synonymous with a coaching instruction to
attend only outside the body: anticipated body-related effects can enter the
theory. Claims about the benefits of a particular attentional cue require their
own task-specific evidence.

For golf, a desired ball flight is an outcome specification. It constrains a
family of impact states through collision and flight mechanics; it does not
identify a unique backswing, torque history or muscle pattern. The inverse-model
discussion in Chapter~\ref{ch:internal-models} explains why selecting an effect
and computing a feasible command remain separate problems. An intention can
organize the task without replacing the mechanics that realize it.

\section{Prediction Error, Innovation and Task Error}

Consider the engineering model
\begin{align}
 x_{k+1}&=A x_k+B u_k+w_k, & y_k&=H x_k+v_k,\\
 \hat x^-_k&=A\hat x^+_{k-1}+B u_{k-1}, &
 \hat y^-_k&=H\hat x^-_k.
\end{align}
Here $x$ is a state, $u$ an applied input, $y$ an observation and the superscripts
$-$ and $+$ denote estimates before and after the current observation. With
zero-mean independent Gaussian process and measurement noises, known matrices
and a Gaussian initial state, the Kalman recursion gives the conditional mean
and covariance. Those are assumptions of this model, not established identities
between its variables and neural populations.

The innovation and its covariance are
\begin{equation}
 \nu_k=y_k-H\hat x^-_k,\qquad
 S_k=H P^-_k H^{\mathsf T}+R,
 \label{eq:ch5:prediction-error}
\end{equation}
where $R\succ0$ is measurement-noise covariance and $P^-_k\succeq0$ is the
prior state covariance, so $S_k$ is invertible. The update is
$\hat x^+_k=\hat x^-_k+K_k\nu_k$, with
$K_k=P^-_k H^{\mathsf T}S_k^{-1}$. A residual formed using $\hat x^+_k$ has
already used the measurement and is not this prior innovation. A nonlinear
residual $y-h(\hat x)$ resembles it algebraically, but generally requires
additional approximation or a different estimator.

\begin{example}{Precision Is Not a Gain}{precision-gain}
 For a scalar observation $y=x+v$, let $P^-=4$ and $R=1$ in squared observation
 units. The measurement precision is $R^{-1}=1$, while $K=4/5$ and the posterior
 variance is $4/5$. Keeping $R=1$ but using $P^-=1/4$ changes $K$ to $1/5$.
 Measurement precision alone therefore does not determine the correction.
 In several dimensions the gain also depends on observation geometry $H$.
\end{example}

Define a desired observation $r_k$. Task error $y_k-r_k$ and innovation
$y_k-\hat y^-_k$ can disagree: an exactly predicted miss has zero innovation
and nonzero task error. Conversely, an unexpectedly successful shot can have
zero task error and nonzero innovation. The first concerns performance relative
to a goal; the second concerns consistency with a prediction. Which error drives
which learning process is an experimental question, not a choice of notation.

An efference copy is an internal copy of a motor command. In a forward-model
interpretation it informs prediction; it is not itself a feedforward actuator
signal. The predictor must also represent the relevant state, dynamics and
observation process. Command does not become observation without the intervening
plant. Likewise, a forward model need not be a linear transition matrix.

If a sensor is delayed by $d$ samples, its observation refers to $x_{k-d}$.
Comparing that observation with a current-state prediction without aligning
timestamps introduces an artificial error. Prediction can propagate an estimate
forward, but uncertainty and model mismatch remain. The duration of a golf
movement does not establish a universal sensory delay, a universal bandwidth
limit or the complete absence of useful feedback. Those depend on the loop,
measurement and intervention being considered.

\section{Active Inference and Receding-Horizon Control}

Predictive processing is a family of hypotheses about inference and neural
computation. Active-inference proposals connect action to the observations
used for inference\cite{FristonAction2010Review}. For a fixed
observation $y$ and latent state $z$, variational free energy satisfies
\begin{equation}
 F[q;y]=\mathbb E_q[\log q(z)-\log p(y,z)]
       =D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p(z\mid y))-\log p(y).
\end{equation}
Minimizing over $q$ at fixed $y$ is an inference problem. An action law additionally
requires a generative process, a representation of preferences, an information
pattern and a rule linking action to future observations. A quadratic tracking
cost alone does not supply those choices. Chapter~\ref{ch:computational}
develops the distinction between this identity and control objectives.

An explicit engineering alternative is output-tracking MPC. At time $k$, optimize
a sequence $u_{0:N-1}$, using local horizon index $j$ and predicted states $x_j$,
subject to $x_0=\hat x_k$ and the prediction dynamics
$x_{j+1}=f(x_j,u_j)$:
\begin{equation}
 \min_{u_{0:N-1}}\ \Phi(x_N)+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
 \left[\|h(x_j)-r_{k+j}\|_W^2+\|u_j\|_U^2\right],
 \label{eq:ch5:tracking-mpc}
\end{equation}
with stated feasible-state/input sets and weights $W\succeq0$, $U\succ0$.
Apply the first command, obtain new information and solve again. Weights carry
the units needed for a consistent objective. An uncertain problem must also
specify the prediction distribution and how constraints handle uncertainty.
This is MPC because of its dynamics, horizon and repeated first-action policy,
not because its objective contains squared errors. Equivalence to an
active-inference construction must be demonstrated for matched models,
preferences, constraints and action updates; it is not a general identity.

\begin{example}{Perfect Prediction Can Still Miss the Goal}{predicted-miss}
 Let $x_1=x_0+u$, $x_0=0$, $y_1=x_1$ and desired $r=1$, all in declared
 normalized units. A perfect predictor gives $\hat y_1=u$ for every $u$.
 Minimizing $(y_1-\hat y_1)^2+\lambda u^2$, $\lambda>0$, selects $u=0$.
 Minimizing the tracking objective $(u-1)^2+\lambda u^2$ instead gives
 $u=1/(1+\lambda)$. Setting $\lambda=1$ yields $0$ versus $1/2$.
 The difference is the goal specification, despite identical prediction accuracy.
\end{example}

\section{How the Pieces Connect in a Swing}

Outcome preferences select what matters; mechanics map feasible commands to
impact states; an estimator combines previous state information with new
measurements; a controller acts on its information; learning changes a model,
policy or preference across experience. Passive dynamics and stored elastic
energy participate in the plant throughout this chain. They do not remove the
need to distinguish intention, prediction and observation.

A technically useful golf experiment should therefore state which outcome is
changed, which variables are measured, which command or feedback is manipulated,
and which competing models make different predictions. Similar-looking swings
or reduced error alone cannot identify the algorithm that generated them.

\begin{enumerate}
 \item Derive the two scalar Kalman gains above. Change observation units and
 transform $H$ and $R$ consistently; explain why a precision is not a gain.
 \item Solve the one-step example with $|u|\leq u_{\max}$. Determine when the

 \item Specify an action-effect association experiment and an alternative
