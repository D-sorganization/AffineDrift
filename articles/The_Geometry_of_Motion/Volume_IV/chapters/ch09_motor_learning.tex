\chapter{Motor Learning and Adaptation}
\label{ch:motor-learning}

Improved performance, changed prediction, a persistent aftereffect and faster
relearning are different observations. They can involve interacting processes
and do not map one-to-one onto three brain regions or three fixed time scales.
This chapter develops a bounded trial-by-trial model, then shows what it can
and cannot establish about learning a movement.

\section{Distinguishing Processes and Measurements}

Error-based adaptation changes subsequent behavior in response to experienced
errors. Task error measures success relative to a goal; sensory prediction error
measures discrepancy from an expected observation. Explicit aiming strategies,
reward-based action selection and use-dependent biases from repetition can also
affect the measured movement. These categories describe different questions,
not mutually exclusive anatomical compartments. A falling error curve alone
does not isolate any one of them.

Huang and colleagues distinguish internal-model adaptation from proposed
model-free contributions to repetition biases and savings
\cite{Huang2011}. Their account concerns particular adaptation paradigms, not a
universal assignment of every fast change to one neural structure. Similarly,
the fast and slow processes inferred by Smith and colleagues describe retention
and error sensitivity in a short-term reaching model; the paper explicitly
does not uniquely identify their neural realization\cite{SmithDualRate2006Review}.

Consolidation concerns stabilization or other time-dependent changes in memory
assessed with a specified retention/interference protocol. Use-dependent bias
means a repeated movement influences later movement selection. Structural
learning concerns regularities across tasks. These are not interchangeable
names for repetition, nor does an immediate aftereffect prove consolidation.
Seconds within a movement, trial number and elapsed time between practice
sessions must also be distinguished.

\section{A Single-Rate Model With an Exact Solution}

Let $p_k$ be an imposed angular perturbation, $x_k$ the learned compensation
\emph{before} trial $k$, and $e_k$ the error available to this idealized model:
\begin{equation}
 e_k=p_k-x_k,\qquad x_{k+1}=a x_k+b e_k=(a-b)x_k+b p_k.
 \label{eq:ch9:single-rate}
\end{equation}
All three angular quantities use the same units. Retention $a$ and sensitivity
$b$ are dimensionless per-trial parameters. This deterministic scalar recurrence
does not separate explicit from implicit learning, estimate a neural state or
represent within-swing feedback. Its error is a declared input to the model,
not automatically an experimentally isolated sensory prediction error.

For constant $p$, write $\lambda=a-b$. If $|\lambda|<1$,
\begin{equation}
 x_\infty=\frac{b p}{1-a+b},\quad
 e_\infty=\frac{(1-a)p}{1-a+b},\quad
 x_k=x_\infty+(x_0-x_\infty)\lambda^k.
\end{equation}
Convergence is monotone relative to the fixed point when $0\leq\lambda<1$;
negative $\lambda$ gives alternating deviations. Stable parameter choices need
not eliminate error. With $a=0.98$, $b=0.05$, $p=30^\circ$, the asymptotic
compensation is $150/7\approx21.43^\circ$ and error is
$60/7\approx8.57^\circ$. Those are manufactured model values, not a human
adaptation ceiling. The listing restricts $a,b$ to $[0,1]$ and $|a-b|<1$;
this is a declared example domain, not a universal biological parameter law.

\subsection{Washout Is Not a Zero-Error Clamp}

After $N$ adaptation trials the state is $x_N$, including the update after the
last observed error. Removing the perturbation gives first washout error
$-x_N$ and subsequent states $x_{N+j}=\lambda^j x_N$. For positive compensation,
the initial aftereffect has the opposite sign to the initial perturbation error.
By contrast, an ideal zero-error clamp supplies $e_k=0$ regardless of the state,
so $x_{N+j}=a^j x_N$. The different decay factors follow from different
feedback conditions. A force-channel experiment that constrains lateral error
is not the same as removing the force field, and neither is automatically
equivalent to every visual clamp or absence of feedback.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Trial States With Explicit Feedback Conditions}]
from dataclasses import dataclass

import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import lfilter


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class AdaptationModel:
    """Scalar trial model; all angular inputs and outputs are degrees."""

    retention: float = 0.98
    sensitivity: float = 0.05

    def __post_init__(self) -> None:
        rates = np.asarray([self.retention, self.sensitivity], dtype=float)
        if not np.isfinite(rates).all() or np.any((rates < 0) | (rates > 1)):
            raise ValueError("Rates must be finite and within [0, 1]")
        if abs(self.retention - self.sensitivity) >= 1:
            raise ValueError("Veridical-feedback recurrence must be stable")

    def run(self, perturbations: np.ndarray, initial: float = 0.0,
            feedback: str = "veridical") -> dict[str, np.ndarray | float]:
        """Return pretrial states, supplied errors and final updated state."""
        schedule = np.asarray(perturbations, dtype=float)
        if schedule.ndim != 1 or schedule.size == 0:
            raise ValueError("A nonempty one-dimensional schedule is required")
        if not np.isfinite(schedule).all() or not np.isfinite(initial):
            raise ValueError("Schedule and initial state must be finite")
        if feedback not in ("veridical", "clamp"):
            raise ValueError("Unknown feedback condition")
        if feedback == "clamp" and np.any(schedule != 0):
            raise ValueError("Use a zero schedule for the zero-error clamp")
        pole = self.retention
        forcing = np.zeros_like(schedule)
        if feedback == "veridical":
            pole -= self.sensitivity
            forcing = self.sensitivity * schedule
        # The filter output is the POST-trial state; zi carries x0.
        updated, _ = lfilter([1.0], [1.0, -pole], forcing,
                             zi=[pole * initial])
        states = np.concatenate(([initial], updated[:-1]))
        errors = schedule - states if feedback == "veridical" else forcing
        return {"state": states, "error": errors,
                "final_state": float(updated[-1])}


schedule = np.concatenate((np.zeros(50), np.full(200, 30.0), np.zeros(100)))
result = AdaptationModel().run(schedule)
\end{lstlisting}

The filter implements the same recurrence without a Python loop over trials.
The returned final state permits exact continuation across blocks. The index at
which washout begins comes from the schedule, not a hard-coded assertion about
every experiment. Holding the full initial state and parameters fixed must
reproduce the same trajectory; this model has no additional memory of practice.

\section{Savings, Interference and Hidden States}

Savings means faster relearning under a specified comparison protocol. The
same apparent initial performance need not imply the same hidden state. A
two-rate model makes this distinction explicit:
\begin{align}
 x_{f,k+1}&=a_f x_{f,k}+b_f e_k, &
 x_{s,k+1}&=a_s x_{s,k}+b_s e_k,\\
 x_k&=x_{f,k}+x_{s,k}, &e_k&=p_k-x_k,
\end{align}
with $a_f<a_s$ and $b_f>b_s$ in the intended regime. During veridical feedback
the joint state matrix is
$\operatorname{diag}(a_f,a_s)-(b_f,b_s)^{\mathsf T}(1,1)$.
Its eigenvalues, not retention factors alone, determine linear stability.
The parameters describe trial dynamics and do not assign fast learning to
conscious thought or slow learning to a particular brain region.

Under a zero-error clamp the two states decay separately. If initially
$x_f=-c$ and $x_s=c$, output is zero, but after $j$ trials
\begin{equation}
 x_j=c(a_s^j-a_f^j).
\end{equation}
For $0<a_f<a_s<1$ and $c>0$, this gives a positive transient rebound and then
decay. Smith and colleagues tested a prediction of this kind using force-channel
reaching after adaptation and brief opposite-field training
\cite{SmithDualRate2006Review}. The observed recovery supports multiple time
scales in that task; it does not uniquely locate two anatomical modules.

Residual hidden states can also facilitate relearning. If \emph{all} states,
parameters and inputs are reset to their original values, a deterministic
fixed-parameter model reproduces its original learning curve. Thus zero net
output is weaker than erasing every state. Savings after extended washout can
motivate additional mechanisms, including retrieval of previously successful
actions, as proposed and tested in particular visuomotor paradigms
\cite{Huang2011}; no single curve proves a unique mechanism.

\textbf{Anterograde interference} means earlier learning of A impairs subsequent
learning of B. \textbf{Retrograde interference} means later learning of B impairs
retention or expression of A. Specify exposure, test delay, washout and the
performance measure before applying either label. A change in error immediately
after switching perturbations is not sufficient to identify a memory mechanism.

\section{Learning Structure Across Tasks}

Braun and colleagues varied visuomotor transformations within a task family and
tested transfer to new transformations. Their findings supported facilitation,
interference reduction and preferential exploration related to the experienced
structure\cite{BraunStructure2009Review}. Structural learning therefore concerns
relations among tasks, rather than repetition of one solution. It need not be
assigned to a specific cortical area to state this behavioral claim.

A simple engineering illustration is a family of parameters
$\theta=\theta_0+S\eta$, with orthonormal columns of $S$. Searching in $\eta$
can reduce the number of adjustable coefficients. But for a new target
$\theta_*$, the least-squares residual is
$(I-SS^{\mathsf T})(\theta_*-\theta_0)$. A task outside the learned subspace
therefore incurs a restriction bias. The coordinates, metric and applicability
of that family must be established; lower dimension is not a guarantee of
universally faster or more accurate learning.

\section{Intertwined Changes in Golf Practice}

Changing club mass or shaft compliance changes plant dynamics. Changing a
displayed aim direction changes information or a task reference. Wind changes
ball-flight dynamics after impact. These are different interventions, although
each can change observed shot error. A useful experiment measures impact
conditions as well as ball outcome and separates familiarization, adaptation,
retention and transfer tests.

Repeated practice may change a chosen trajectory, an internal prediction, a
feedback policy or a strategy. Existing activation and stored elastic energy
affect each swing's mechanics; between-trial learning can change how those states
are prepared. The scalar recurrence illustrates one possible history dependence,
not a validated prescription for swing correction. A convincing golf claim needs
measured outcomes, a specified perturbation and competing explanations, with
generalization tested rather than inferred from a range-practice curve.
