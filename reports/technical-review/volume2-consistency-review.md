## The Argument Across Chapters

A golf swing couples geometry, feasible actuation, timing and task outcomes. Configuration and velocity coordinates describe the mechanical state; the mass matrix, loads, constraints and input map determine which accelerations can occur. A desired curve alone does not establish a feasible timed motion. Its nominal state evolution must be generated by an admissible input, with the relevant state and contact conditions satisfied. Actuator dynamics and contact transitions add their own states or mode equations when included.

These mechanics do not select the task objective. Regulation, time-indexed tracking, path following, event objectives and orbital stabilization answer different, complementary questions. Impact evaluates motion at an event whose time can itself vary under perturbation. An along-path error can change contact time or speed; discarding it requires a task-specific justification. A finite downswing need not be periodic. Stability of a periodic orbit, finite-time disturbance amplification and terminal impact sensitivity therefore cannot be treated as interchangeable certificates.

The geometry chapters supply coordinates and moving frames for those questions. Coordinate singularities concern a representation; mechanical singularities concern a mechanism; task singularities concern the velocity-to-output map. Coordinate choices and metric scaling also affect reported error magnitudes. Transverse dynamics require a valid phase construction and the terms introduced by its moving basis. A linearization describes first-order response about its stated nominal motion. A bound on a transition operator concerns that homogeneous variational system; it does not automatically bound nonlinear remainders, continuous forcing, event-time changes or reset sensitivity.

Underactuation and passive mechanics constrain the nominal motion and its corrections. Segment energy transfer does not by itself identify joint torque or a biological strategy. Optimization selects among trajectories under its declared objective and constraints; a necessary optimality condition is not a global optimum. A nonlinear funnel requires verification over its region, including input limits, disturbances and relevant transitions. Phase control, stochastic models and learning methods introduce further assumptions about timing, noise and model error. Their manufactured examples establish internal mathematical behavior; the closing golf case study remains a proposed investigation rather than a validated human result.

This chain preserves the book's central investigation: explain how mechanics, control authority, perturbation response and club delivery interact, while keeping mathematical guarantees, numerical evidence and measurements of human movement distinct.
