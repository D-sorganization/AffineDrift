## Variational Dynamics Along Trajectories

The tangent space is not merely a static geometric concept; it provides the rigorous foundation for variational analysis in dynamical systems. Consider a general nonlinear control system:

$$
\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t), u(t))
$$

where $x(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ represents the state vector, $u(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the control input, and $f: \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is a continuously differentiable vector field.

It is worth noting that for first-order input linearization, the dynamics need not be control-affine of the form $\dot{x} = f_0(x) + \sum g_j(x)u_j$. The differential formulation applies directly to general smooth couplings $f(x, u)$, because the first differential with respect to the control input $u$ yields a linear map regardless of whether $u$ enters affinely in the underlying differential equation.

To distinguish exact infinitesimal variations from finite trajectory increments, consider a one-parameter family of solutions $x_\epsilon(t)$ driven by controls $u_\epsilon(t)$, continuously differentiable in $\epsilon \in (-\epsilon_0, \epsilon_0)$, satisfying:

$$
\dot{x}_\epsilon(t) = f(x_\epsilon(t), u_\epsilon(t)).
$$

Let $\epsilon = 0$ specify the nominal reference trajectory $(x^*(t), u^*(t)) \equiv (x_0(t), u_0(t))$. The exact infinitesimal variations are defined by the parametric derivatives evaluated at $\epsilon = 0$:

$$
\xi(t) \triangleq \left.\frac{\partial x_\epsilon(t)}{\partial \epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}, \qquad \nu(t) \triangleq \left.\frac{\partial u_\epsilon(t)}{\partial \epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}.
$$

Differentiating the family equation $\dot{x}_\epsilon(t) = f(x_\epsilon(t), u_\epsilon(t))$ with respect to $\epsilon$ and interchanging the time derivative with the parameter derivative (guaranteed under $C^1$ regularity) yields the exact linear variational differential equation:

$$
\dot{\xi}(t) = A(t)\xi(t) + B(t)\nu(t)
$$

where the coefficient matrices are the Jacobians evaluated strictly along the nominal trajectory:

$$
A(t) = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{(x^*(t), u^*(t))}, \qquad B(t) = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\right|_{(x^*(t), u^*(t))}.
$$

Here $\xi(t)$ is an element of the tangent space $T_{x^*(t)}\mathbb{R}^n \cong \mathbb{R}^n$ at each instant. The linear differential equation for $\xi(t)$ is exact for the parametric derivative of the family.

## Finite Differences and Remainder Terms

In physical applications—such as tracking the finite trajectory of a golf ball subject to aerodynamic drag and wind gusts—one deals not with an infinitesimal directional derivative $\xi(t)$, but with actual finite differences:

$$
\Delta x(t) = x(t) - x^*(t), \qquad \Delta u(t) = u(t) - u^*(t).
$$

Differentiating $\Delta x(t)$ with respect to time gives:

$$
\Delta\dot{x}(t) = f(x^*(t) + \Delta x(t), u^*(t) + \Delta u(t)) - f(x^*(t), u^*(t)).
$$

When $f$ is of class $C^1$, Taylor's theorem ensures that:

$$
\Delta\dot{x}(t) = A(t)\Delta x(t) + B(t)\Delta u(t) + R_1(\Delta x(t), \Delta u(t))
$$

where the remainder satisfies $R_1(\Delta x, \Delta u) = o(\|(\Delta x, \Delta u)\|)$ as $\|(\Delta x, \Delta u)\| \to 0$. If $f$ is additionally of class $C^2$ with bounded second derivatives along the tube surrounding the trajectory, Taylor's expansion with integral or Lagrange remainder guarantees an upper-order bound on the quadratic remainder:

$$
\Delta\dot{x}(t) = A(t)\Delta x(t) + B(t)\Delta u(t) + R_2(\Delta x(t), \Delta u(t)),
$$

$$
\|R_2(\Delta x, \Delta u)\| \le \frac{1}{2} M_2 \left(\|\Delta x\|^2 + \|\Delta u\|^2\right).
$$

Notice that the second-order Hessian contribution can vanish along specific directions or for affine dynamics; it is not unconditionally non-zero, but the bounded second derivative guarantees an upper bound on truncation error. While the variational variable $\xi(t)$ satisfies $\dot{\xi} = A\xi + B\nu$ without approximation, the physical increment $\Delta x(t)$ satisfies the linear system only up to the finite remainder $R_2$.

## Moving Tangent Spaces and Embedded Bases

![Moving tangent spaces](./figures/moving_tangent.svg){fig-alt="A trajectory passes through a sequence of moving tangent spaces, each carrying the local linear perturbation dynamics."}

As the nominal state $x^*(t)$ moves through the state space along a flight path or orbit, the tangent space $T_{x^*(t)}\mathbb{R}^n$ translates and rotates.

When considering submanifolds or moving coordinate frames, care must be taken regarding transport. Consider an embedded tangent vector $\xi(t) \in T_{x^*(t)}M$ attached to a trajectory on an embedded manifold $M$ (for instance, a tangent vector to a sphere or a unit circle $S^1$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$). While the vector $\xi(t)$ itself remains in the tangent space $T_{x^*(t)}M$ for each $t$, its ordinary ambient time derivative $\dot{\xi}(t)$ in the ambient Euclidean embedding does not necessarily lie in $T_{x^*(t)}M$. On a rotating circle, for example, the time derivative of a moving unit tangent vector acquires an inward normal component proportional to the path curvature and angular velocity.

To represent variational dynamics intrinsic to moving tangent frames without missing transport terms, let $\{e_1(t), \dots, e_n(t)\}$ be a smooth time-varying basis for the tangent space, collected into an invertible matrix $E(t) = [e_1(t) \cdots e_n(t)]$. Expressing the variational vector in terms of coordinates $\eta(t)$ via $\xi(t) = E(t)\eta(t)$, its ambient time derivative is:

$$
\dot{\xi}(t) = E(t)\dot{\eta}(t) + \dot{E}(t)\eta(t).
$$

Substituting into the variational dynamics $\dot{\xi} = A(t)\xi + B(t)\nu$ yields:

$$
E(t)\dot{\eta}(t) + \dot{E}(t)\eta(t) = A(t)E(t)\eta(t) + B(t)\nu(t).
$$

Solving for the internal coordinate derivative $\dot{\eta}(t)$:

$$
\dot{\eta}(t) = E(t)^{-1}\left(A(t)E(t) - \dot{E}(t)\right)\eta(t) + E(t)^{-1}B(t)\nu(t).
$$

The explicit presence of $\dot{E}(t)$ accounts for frame transport. Omitting $\dot{E}(t)$ conflates the rate of change of the coordinates with the rotation of the moving coordinate frame.

## Superposition and Shared Reference Trajectories

Superposition is an exact structural property of linear differential equations: if $\xi_1(t)$ solves $\dot{\xi} = A(t)\xi + B(t)\nu_1$ and $\xi_2(t)$ solves $\dot{\xi} = A(t)\xi + B(t)\nu_2$, their sum $\xi_1(t) + \xi_2(t)$ solves $\dot{\xi} = A(t)\xi + B(t)(\nu_1 + \nu_2)$.

Crucially, this exact superposition requires that both variational solutions share the **identical nominal reference trajectory** $(x^*(t), u^*(t))$. Because $A(t) = D_x f(x^*(t), u^*(t))$ and $B(t) = D_u f(x^*(t), u^*(t))$ depend explicitly on the base trajectory, perturbations computed around different base paths cannot be added directly; their respective tangent spaces and system matrices differ.

When evaluating finite differences $\Delta x_1$ and $\Delta x_2$ on the nonlinear system, superposition fails due to the coupled interaction terms in the nonlinear expansion:

$$
f(x^* + \Delta x_1 + \Delta x_2, u^*) \neq f(x^*, u^*) + [f(x^* + \Delta x_1, u^*) - f(x^*, u^*)] + [f(x^* + \Delta x_2, u^*) - f(x^*, u^*)].
$$

The cross terms in the Taylor series constitute an inherent breakdown of superposition for finite increments.

## The State Transition Matrix and Finite Prediction Horizons

For the linear time-varying (LTV) variational system, the state transition matrix $\Phi(t, t_0)$ is the unique matrix solution satisfying:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi(t, t_0) = A(t)\Phi(t, t_0), \qquad \Phi(t_0, t_0) = I.
$$

While $A(t)$ is smooth along smooth trajectory segments, standard Carathéodory conditions ensure that piecewise continuous coefficients $A(t)$ and $B(t)$ are fully adequate to define a unique, absolutely continuous state transition matrix $\Phi(t, t_0)$ across discrete switches or mode transitions (such as contact phases or staging events).

The exact solution of the variational dynamics over a time horizon $[t_0, t]$ is:

$$
\xi(t) = \Phi(t, t_0)\xi(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t} \Phi(t, \tau) B(\tau) \nu(\tau) \, d\tau.
$$

When this LTV solution is used to predict the evolution of actual finite perturbations $\Delta x(t)$, its validity is confined to a bounded finite-horizon tube. Applying the variation-of-constants formula to the full perturbed equation $\Delta\dot{x} = A(t)\Delta x + B(t)\Delta u + R_2(\Delta x, \Delta u)$ yields:

$$
\Delta x(t) = \Phi(t, t_0)\Delta x(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t} \Phi(t, \tau) B(\tau) \Delta u(\tau) \, d\tau + \int_{t_0}^{t} \Phi(t, \tau) R_2(\Delta x(\tau), \Delta u(\tau)) \, d\tau.
$$

The discrepancy between the linearized forecast and the true nonlinear state increment is governed entirely by the accumulated remainder integral. By the Grönwall-Bellman inequality, if the Jacobian $A(t)$ has positive eigenvalues or substantial non-normal growth (unstable variational modes), $\|\Phi(t, \tau)\|$ can grow rapidly.

An unstable derivative $A(t)$ does not invalidate the linear tangent model as an infinitesimal description; rather, it exponentially amplifies the effect of the finite remainder $R_2$, causing the tube of practical validity around the nominal trajectory to shrink rapidly over time.

## Application: Finite Golf Trajectory Variations

Consider the flight of a golf ball modeled by positional coordinates $r = [x, y, z]^T$ and velocity $v = [v_x, v_y, v_z]^T$:

$$
\dot{r} = v, \qquad \dot{v} = g - \frac{1}{2m} \rho C_D A_{\text{ref}} \|v - w(r)\| (v - w(r)) + \frac{1}{2m} \rho C_L A_{\text{ref}} (\omega \times (v - w(r)))
$$

where $w(r)$ is a spatially varying wind field, $\omega$ is the spin vector, $g$ is gravity, and $C_D, C_L$ are aerodynamic coefficients.

1. **Nonlinear Coupling:** Aerodynamic drag depends quadratically on the relative speed $\|v - w(r)\|$. Linearizing the dynamics about a nominal ballistic trajectory $(r^*(t), v^*(t))$ yields time-varying Jacobians:
   $$
   A(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \\ A_{vr}(t) & A_{vv}(t) \end{bmatrix}.
   $$
2. **Infinitesimal vs. Finite Launch Variations:** A small variation in initial launch angle or ball speed can be formally cast as an infinitesimal parameter variation $\xi(0) = [\delta r_0^T, \delta v_0^T]^T$. The state transition matrix $\Phi(t_{\text{impact}}, 0)$ maps this initial variation directly to the final landing displacement on the green:
   $$
   \xi(t_{\text{impact}}) = \Phi(t_{\text{impact}}, 0)\xi(0).
   $$
3. **Finite Remainder and Wind Disturbances:** For finite launch errors $\Delta v_0$ and gust increments $\Delta w(t)$, the quadratic speed term generates a non-zero remainder $R_2(\Delta v, \Delta w)$. Along a high-speed driver shot, $A_{vv}(t)$ exhibits large negative damping eigenvalues along the velocity vector, keeping longitudinal variations bounded, while crosswind shears introduce off-diagonal coupling. The linearized prediction $\Phi(t, 0)\Delta x(0)$ provides an accurate prediction within a bounded tube of launch deviations, but degrades as launch angle perturbations exceed a few degrees.

## Looking Ahead

The linear variational equation $\dot{\xi} = A(t)\xi + B(t)\nu$ provides the exact infinitesimal dynamic model of a trajectory, while its integration via the state transition matrix provides the foundation for local feedback.
