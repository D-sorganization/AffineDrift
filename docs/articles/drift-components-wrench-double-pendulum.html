Natural vs Active Forces and Torques in Affine Mechanical Systems

1. Motivation

For an affine control system, we usually write [ = f(x) + G(x)u, ] and think of (f) as the drift (what the system does “on its own”) and (G(x)u) as the control effect.

For mechanical systems, there is a direct analog at the level of forces, torques, and wrenches:

  • A natural (or drift) component that arises purely from
    • inertia,
    • Coriolis and centrifugal effects,
    • gravity,
    • damping/friction,
    • contact reactions;
  • An active component generated by muscular (or actuator) input.

The key point:

For a given motion and environment, the total torques and wrenches can be decomposed into a purely natural mechanical contribution plus an active contribution generated by muscles/actuators. This is not a control design issue; it is a mechanical identity.

In this article:

  • We derive this decomposition in general form.
  • We specialize it to a planar double pendulum.
  • We implement the model in Python (ready to be wrapped in Streamlit).

2. General mechanical system: natural vs active torques

Consider an (n)-DOF mechanical system with generalized coordinates (q ^n), velocities (), accelerations (). The standard joint-space dynamics (from Lagrange or Newton–Euler) can be written as

[ M(q), + C(q,), + g(q) + d(q,) = {} + {}, ]

where

  • (M(q) ^{n n}) is the inertia matrix,
  • (C(q,)) is the vector of Coriolis and centrifugal terms,
  • (g(q)) is the gravity torque vector,
  • (d(q,)) collects dissipative effects (e.g., joint damping, friction),
  • (_{}) are internal active torques (e.g., muscles or motors),
  • (_{}) are generalized forces from external wrenches.

Suppose external wrenches () (contact forces, etc.) act at some contact frames with Jacobian (J_c(q)). Then

[ _{} = J_c(q)^. ]

Substituting into (2.1),

[ M(q), + C(q,), + g(q) + d(q,) = _{} + J_c(q)^. ]

2.1 Natural torque field

We define the natural (drift) torque field as the part entirely determined by the motion and environment:

[ _{}(q,,,) := M(q) + C(q,), + g(q) + d(q,) - J_c(q)^. ]

Interpretation:

  • (_{}) is what the world contributes:
    • inertial reaction torques,
    • velocity-dependent terms,
    • gravity,
    • dissipation,
    • plus minus the external generalized forces (so that the remaining side is “internal”).
  • It depends only on the trajectory and environment: ((q,,,)).
  • No reference to “control design” or “desired motion” appears here.

From (2.3) and (2.4), we have the identity

[ {} = {}(q,,,). ]

In words: for whatever motion you actually observe or simulate, the internal active torques equal the natural torque field given by the mass matrix, Coriolis, gravity, damping, and external wrenches.

2.2 Parametrizing active torque by inputs

Now suppose the internal active torques are generated by some input vector (u ^m):

[ _{} = B(q),u, ]

where (B(q) ^{n m}) is a configuration-dependent actuation matrix (mapping “muscle channels” or actuators into joint space).

Combining (2.5) and (2.6):

[ B(q),u = _{}(q,,,). ]

This is a purely mechanical identity:

  • Given the state trajectory and external forces, (_{}) is determined.
  • Given a model of how muscles/actuators generate torque ((B(q))), the input channels (u) must satisfy (2.7).

If (B(q)) has full column rank, a typical reconstruction of (u) is

[ u = B(q)^{+},_{}, ]

where (()^{+}) is a pseudoinverse.

3. State-space drift vs active contribution

We can also describe the same decomposition at the state level, in the usual first-order form.

Define the state

[ x = \[\begin{bmatrix} q \\ \dot{q} \end{bmatrix}\]

^{2n}. ]

From (2.3) and (2.6),

[ M(q) = B(q)u + J_c(q)^ - C(q,) - g(q) - d(q,). ]

Therefore

[ = M(q)^{-1}, ( J_c(q)^ - C(q,) - g(q) - d(q,) ) + M(q)^{-1} B(q),u. ]

Thus

[ = \[\begin{bmatrix} \dot{q} \\ M(q)^{-1}\big( J_c(q)^\top \lambda - C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} - g(q) - d(q,\dot{q}) \big) \end{bmatrix}\]
  • \[\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ M(q)^{-1} B(q) \end{bmatrix}\]
    1. ]

Define the natural drift field

[ f_{}(x,) := \[\begin{bmatrix} \dot{q} \\ M(q)^{-1}\big( J_c(q)^\top \lambda - C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} - g(q) - d(q,\dot{q}) \big) \end{bmatrix}\]

, ]

and the input field

[ G(x) := \[\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ M(q)^{-1} B(q) \end{bmatrix}\]

. ]

Then we have the familiar affine structure:

[ = f_{}(x,) + G(x)u. ]

Here, (f_{}) is exactly the evolution the system would have with no active input ((u = 0)), subject to gravity, contact, damping, and current velocities.

4. Wrench-level decomposition: natural vs active wrench

Consider an end-effector (e.g., a point on the club) with a spatial Jacobian (J(q)^{6n}) that maps joint velocities to twist:

[ v = J(q),, v ^6. ]

The spatial wrench (w ^6) (force and moment) maps to joint space as

[ {} J(q)^,w + {}, ]

where (_{}) collects internal constraint forces that do not appear as net wrench at that end-effector. If we ignore or separately model those internal contributions, we approximate

[ _{} J(q)^w. ]

Given joint torques (), we can approximate the corresponding wrench by a pseudoinverse:

[ w (J(q)){+},. ]

4.1 Natural wrench

Using the natural torque field (_{}), define the natural wrench at the end-effector:

[ w_{}(q,,,) := (J(q)){+} ,_{}(q,,,). ]

This is the wrench associated with inertia, gravity, velocity-dependent terms, dissipation, and contacts, mapped to the end-effector.

4.2 Active wrench

From (2.6), the active torque is (B(q)u). The associated active wrench is

[ w_{}(q,u) := (J(q)){+} B(q),u. ]

Define

[ W(q) := (J(q)){+} B(q) ^{6 m}, ]

so that

[ w_{}(q,u) = W(q),u. ]

Each column of (W(q)) is the wrench direction generated by unit activation of one input channel.

4.3 Total wrench decomposition

The total wrench splits as

[ w(q,,,,u) = w_{}(q,,,) + w_{}(q,u), ]

i.e.

[ w = w_{} + W(q),u. ]

This is the wrench-level analog of the usual affine decomposition: natural mechanics vs active input.

5. Planar double pendulum example

We now specialize the above to a planar double pendulum (2-link planar manipulator). This gives a concrete model you can drive from Python and plug into Streamlit.

5.1 Configuration and parameters

Let

  • (q = \[\begin{bmatrix} q_1 \\ q_2 \end{bmatrix}\] ) where
    • (q_1) = angle of link 1 from vertical (or from some fixed axis),
    • (q_2) = relative angle of link 2 from link 1.
  • Link lengths: (l_1, l_2).
  • Center-of-mass distances along each link: (c_1, c_2).
  • Masses: (m_1, m_2).
  • Inertia about COM (out of plane): (I_1, I_2).
  • Gravity: (g > 0).

We assume no explicit damping (for simplicity), i.e. (d(q,) = 0), and no external contacts ((J_c^= 0)).

Then the equations of motion take the canonical form

[ M(q) + C(q,), + g(q) = _{}, ]

with (_{} = \[\begin{bmatrix} \tau_1 \\ \tau_2 \end{bmatrix}\]

) the joint torques.

5.2 Inertia matrix (M(q))

A standard planar 2-link manipulator model gives:

[ \[\begin{aligned} M_{11}(q) &= I_1 + I_2 + m_1 c_1^2 + m_2 \big( l_1^2 + c_2^2 + 2 l_1 c_2 \cos q_2 \big), \\ M_{12}(q) &= I_2 + m_2\big( c_2^2 + l_1 c_2 \cos q_2 \big), \\ M_{21}(q) &= M_{12}(q), \\ M_{22}(q) &= I_2 + m_2 c_2^2. \end{aligned} \tag{5.2}\]

]

So

[ M(q) = \[\begin{bmatrix} M_{11}(q) & M_{12}(q) \\ M_{21}(q) & M_{22}(q) \end{bmatrix}\]

. ]

5.3 Coriolis/centrifugal terms (C(q,))

There are various equivalent parameterizations. A commonly used one defines

[ h(q) = - m_2 l_1 c_2 q_2. ]

Then the product (C(q,)) can be written as

[ C(q,) = \[\begin{bmatrix} h(q)\dot{q}_2(2\dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2) \\ - h(q)\dot{q}_1^2 \end{bmatrix}\]

. ]

You can think of this as (C(q,)) without ever writing out (C) explicitly as a matrix.

5.4 Gravity term (g(q))

Assuming angles are measured from the downward vertical for simplicity, the gravity torques are

[ \[\begin{aligned} g_1(q) &= (m_1 c_1 + m_2 l_1)\,g\,\sin q_1 + m_2 c_2 g \sin(q_1 + q_2), \\ g_2(q) &= m_2 c_2 g \sin(q_1 + q_2). \end{aligned} \tag{5.5}\]

]

So

[ g(q) = \[\begin{bmatrix} g_1(q) \\ g_2(q) \end{bmatrix}\]

. ]

5.5 Natural torques and active input

With no external contacts and no explicit damping, the natural torque field reduces to

[ _{}(q,,) = M(q) + C(q,) + g(q). ]

For a fully actuated system with inputs directly equal to torques, we can set (B(q) = I_2) (the (2) identity), so that

[ _{} = u. ]

The mechanical identity (2.7) becomes

[ u = _{}(q,,). ]

In a golf-swing context or in human biomechanics, you might replace (B(q)) by a more structured mapping from muscle activations to joint torques; the decomposition still holds.

6. Energy Transfer Decomposition

A critical aspect of the drift–control analysis is understanding power flow: how energy moves between segments and how much of that transfer is due to “natural” dynamics versus “active” input.

For a distal segment (e.g., the club, link 2), the total energy \(E_2\) changes due to work done by the joint constraint force \(\vec{F}_{21}\) and the joint torque \(\tau_{21}\).

[ 2 = {21} {} + {21} , _2. ]

We calculate the joint constraint force \(\vec{F}_{21}\) from Newton’s law applied to the COM of link 2:

[ {21} = m_2 ({c2} - ). ]

Using the affine decomposition of acceleration from (3.2), [ = _{} + M(q)^{-1} B(q) u, ] the linear acceleration of the COM \(\vec{a}_{c2}\) decomposes as:

[ {c2} = J{c2} + {c2} = { {}} + { _{}}. ]

Thus, the constraint force splits into natural and active components:

[ {} = m_2 ({} - ), {} = m_2 {}. ]

Depending on the actuator model, the joint torque \(\tau_{21}\) also splits (e.g., into passive spring/damper \(\tau_{\text{nat}}\) and active motor \(\tau_{\text{act}}\)). For a purely torque-driven joint, \(\tau_{21} = \tau_{\text{act}} = u_2\).

The Power Transfer then decomposes into:

  1. Natural Power Transfer: Energy moved into the link by passive dynamics (e.g., the arm “pulling” on the club due to centripetal acceleration). [ P_{} = {} {} + _{} _2. ]

  2. Active Power Transfer: Energy moved into the link by the instantaneous action of the control inputs. [ P_{} = {} {} + _{} _2. ]

This decomposition allows us to answer: “Is the club gaining energy because I am pulling on it (passive/geometric transfer) or because I am twisting it (active transfer)?”
