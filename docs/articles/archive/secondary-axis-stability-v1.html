title: “Secondary Axis Stability in Golf Clubs: From Bizarre Physics to Better Putters” format: html: toc: true toc-depth: 3 number-sections: false 1. Why “secondary axis stability” matters

We like to pretend the club moves around a nice clean axis:

driver: around your tilted spine

putter: around a mostly vertical axis through your shoulders

Reality is messier. The club changes shape during the swing (shaft bends, droops, lags), the hands don’t rotate perfectly about one fixed axis, and the clubhead’s mass isn’t arranged in a way that’s friendly to stable rotation.

That’s where secondary axis stability comes in:

How stable is the club’s rotation when the actual axis of motion is not one of its “nice” principal axes?

To get there, we’re going to lean on a classic rigid body phenomenon: the intermediate axis theorem (aka tennis racket theorem).

That sensitivity is exactly what you feel as:

inconsistent face closure,

the head “wobbling” off-plane,

or the putter feeling fine on some strokes and alien on others.

  1. The golf club as a changing rigid body 3.1 Principal axes of a club

For a putter head, you can roughly define:

x x-axis: pointing from face toward the back of the head

y y-axis: heel–toe direction

z z-axis: vertical

Ignoring the shaft for a moment, the head has three principal moments:

Ix I x ​

— about the face-back axis

Iy I y ​

— about the heel–toe axis

Iz I z ​

— about the vertical axis (this is the usual forgiveness MOI: resistance to twisting on off-center hits). one-putts.com +2 MDPI +2

Modern mallets push weight out to heel and toe and rearward to maximize Iz I z ​

. Typical numbers:

“Normal” putters: ~3500–6000 g·cm²

High-MOI mallets: ~6000–9000 g·cm² one-putts.com +1

That’s great for impact forgiveness, but says exactly nothing about how stable the head is when the swing axis itself is bouncing around.

3.2 Why the axis isn’t stable

In a real swing, the effective rotation axis of the clubhead is:

set by your body motion (spine tilt, secondary axis tilt, etc.), GolfWRX +2 Rotaryswing.com +2

altered by shaft bend and droop,

shifted by the fact the shaft attaches near the heel, not through the CG, Cal Poly Content +1

and influenced by how your hands “steer” the club (especially in putting).

So the axis the club actually rotates about is some ugly combination of:

your body’s rotation axis,

the shaft axis,

and whatever torque pattern your hands are applying.

That axis usually doesn’t line up with any principal axis of the head. And as the club flexes and deforms, the inertia tensor changes slightly; the principal axes move in relation to the shaft.

This is the recipe for secondary axis drama:

When the effective rotation axis drifts through the neighborhood of the intermediate principal axis, the dynamics get very sensitive.

The torque required at the hands to keep the club “behaving” can spike or change sign faster than your brain can compensate.

You won’t see a full tennis-racket flip because your hands are clamped on the thing, but you will see:

erratic closure rates,

“mysterious” pulls and pushes with what felt like the same stroke,

and a putter that feels livelier or deader depending on tiny differences in path or lie.

  1. Secondary axis stability: a working definition

For our purposes, define secondary axis stability as:

The degree to which the club’s rotation is predictable and self-consistent when the actual swing axis is not one of the primary principal axes.

More formally:

Let I1≤I2≤I3 I 1 ​

≤I 2 ​

≤I 3 ​

Let ω ω be the angular velocity of the clubhead.

If ω ω is aligned with the axis of I1 I 1 ​

or I3 I 3 ​

If ω ω lies near the intermediate axis ( I2 I 2 ​

We don’t need full-blown rigid-body math here; the takeaway is:

The more your rotation axis is forced to lie in a region where the inertia tensor is “unfriendly”,

the more you’ll need fine, time-varying hand torques to keep the face stable.

A club with good secondary axis stability does two things:

It makes it hard for your effective rotation axis to wander into the “bad” intermediate regime.

When you do wander, it doesn’t punish you with violent sensitivity.

  1. How putters are currently designed (and what they’re missing) 5.1 High-MOI, perimeter weighting

Modern mallets mostly chase maximum Iz I z ​

(vertical MOI) for impact forgiveness:

Rear and toe/heel weights,

Hollow bodies with mass pushed to the edges,

Some even approach driver-like constraints in spirit (though putters don’t share the USGA driver limit of 5900 g·cm²). Cal Poly Content +1

For off-center hits, this works: a high Iz I z ​

reduces face twist and speed loss. Argolf USA +1

But from a secondary axis perspective:

Perimeter weighting tends to make the inertia ellipsoid very “flat” in certain directions,

which can make the intermediate-axis region sharply defined and more sensitive if the swing axis passes through it.

5.2 Zero-torque and lie-angle-balanced putters

Some modern “zero-torque” or lie-angle-balanced putters attack a related issue:

They aim to align the center of mass with the shaft axis, so the torque about the shaft during the stroke is minimized. tutelman.com +2 MDPI +2

These designs greatly reduce the torque the golfer must apply to keep the face square during the stroke. That’s good.

But they still typically use some form of perimeter weighting, and they don’t explicitly target the stability of rotation when:

lie angle changes a bit,

the stroke goes slightly off-plane, or

the head flexes and the principal axes shift.

In other words: they reduce one source of “fight” (shaft torque) but don’t fully address how the head behaves when the effective rotation axis itself is wandering through secondary-axis territory.

  1. Concept: a “secondary-axis-stable” mallet

Let’s build the concept you proposed:

A mallet putter with mass oriented on its central axis, heaviest in-plane, with light sections on the outer axis.

6.1 Geometry idea

Take a standard mallet envelope:

Heel–toe span w≈100 mm w≈100 mm

Face–back depth d≈40 mm d≈40 mm

Thickness t≈20 mm t≈20 mm

Now compare two designs with the same total mass and outer dimensions:

Perimeter design (typical high-MOI mallet)

Most mass concentrated in heel and toe wings.

Central section relatively hollow/light.

Central-axis design (your concept)

A dense “spine” along the central axis (aligned with the intended stroke plane).

Outer heel and toe regions made significantly lighter.

Optionally align the spine with the shaft insertion and swing plane, not just geometric center.

Intuitively:

Design 1 maximizes Iz I z ​

(about vertical) for forgiveness.

Design 2 reduces Iz I z ​

but brings the principal axes into closer alignment with the shaft/swing plane and makes them less sensitive to shaft flex and small lie-angle changes.

7.2 Where the stability gain comes from

So why would you ever sacrifice that much MOI? Because you’re trading:

Static forgiveness at impact vs

Dynamic stability of the stroke about off-nominal axes.

Three key effects:

Axis alignment

With mass along a central spine that’s co-linear with the shaft axis and swing plane, the principal axes are more likely to stay aligned with that plane even as the shaft bends.

That means your effective rotation axis is more often close to a stable principal axis (either the min or max I I), and less likely to pass through the intermediate-axis “doom zone”.

Reduced torque variability

The torque your hands must supply to keep the face square is τ=Ieff⋅ω˙+(gyroscopic terms) τ=I eff ​

If the inertia tensor is more “friendly” relative to the actual stroke axis, then small path deviations or lie-angle changes cause smaller swings in effective inertia and required torque.

Practically: the putter feels like it wants to stay on its path and keep its face angle, instead of needing micro-adjustments.

Smoother behavior under shaft deflection

A perimeter mallet can have principal axes that move a lot when the shaft is bent or the lie angle changes by a few degrees.

A central-axis design tends to make the inertia tensor more axisymmetric with respect to the shaft/swing plane, so those changes have less impact on the rotational dynamics.

You’re effectively saying: “I’m willing to give up some help on bad strikes to get a stroke that’s inherently easier to keep on-plane and square.”

7.3 A rough “stability vs MOI” summary

Using the toy model as a guide (not gospel), you can imagine a family of designs:

Design type Qualitative vertical-axis MOI Qualitative secondary-axis stability Extreme perimeter mallet 100% (baseline) Poor–moderate (sensitive) Modern high-MOI zero-torque mallet 80–100% Moderate (better torque balance) Hybrid perimeter/central spine 60–80% Good Strong central-axis bias (your idea) 50–70% Very good

Those ranges are indicative, not measured. The main point is:

You probably don’t want to go all the way to a 50% MOI design for most golfers;

but a moderate central-axis bias could move you into a sweet spot where:

you lose maybe 20–30% of vertical MOI vs a full perimeter sledgehammer,

and gain a noticeable bump in how “self-stable” the stroke feels about the real, messy swing axis.

  1. Full swing: changing shape, changing axes

Everything above is for a putter, where things are relatively tame. In a full swing, the club is:

bending, drooping, and unbending,

experiencing rapidly changing angular velocity about multiple axes,

and the shaft axis and clubhead principal axes can get badly misaligned at high speed.

In that regime, the intermediate-axis behavior from the video is lurking under the surface:

As the club’s effective inertia tensor changes during the downswing,

the “preferred” stable axes move,

and your actual angular velocity vector can drift closer to, or away from, the intermediate-axis region.

Your hands do an enormous amount of work unconsciously to keep the club behaving. That’s one reason why elite swings look effortless but are absolutely not simple mechanically.

A club designed with secondary axis stability in mind would try to:

Keep the club’s principal axes as “friendly” as possible to the actual path and shaft deflections used in the swing,

so that the club wants to move in the way your body is trying to move it, instead of constantly demanding corrections.

That’s a much harder design problem than “make Iz I z ​

big,” but the physics is the same.

  1. Practical implications for a secondary-axis-stable mallet

If you actually built the putter we just described, here’s what you’d expect:

Pros

Stroke feels more “rail-like”: once you set it in motion, it wants to keep rotating in the same plane.

Less sensitivity to small changes in lie angle or shaft lean during the stroke.

Reduced “wobble” when the hands get a bit out of sync; the putter head behaves more like a single solid axis system.

Potentially simpler, more repeatable face-angle control for players who struggle with face rotation.

Cons

Less built-in forgiveness on mishits — especially for heel–toe strikes.

Marketing nightmare: MOI numbers would look worse on the spec sheet, even if the dynamic stability is better.

Tuning is player-specific: the “right” amount of central weighting depends on stroke style (straight-back-straight-through vs strong arc), lie angle, and shaft insertion geometry.

Who might benefit most?

Players who already strike the ball consistently near the sweet spot,

but fight face control and path consistency under pressure.

Better players who can tolerate a small drop in static forgiveness in exchange for a stroke that feels mechanically “locked in”.

  1. Where this idea fits in the current landscape

The industry has already moved:

From “feel” blades → high-MOI mallets for forgiveness,

Into “zero-torque” and lie-angle-balanced designs that reduce shaft torque and face twist. tutelman.com +2 MDPI +2

The next logical step is exactly what you’re describing:

Putters designed not just for MOI and torque balance, but for stability with respect to the real, messy, changing swing axis — i.e., true secondary axis stability.

That means optimizing:

the full 3D inertia tensor of the head + shaft,

across a range of realistic shaft deflections and lie angles,

to keep the effective stroke axis in a dynamically stable region and to minimize torque variability at the hands.

It’s a more subtle, physics-heavy design space… which is probably why you don’t see it on marketing posters yet.

  1. Summary (for your readers)

The YouTube video on bizarre rotating bodies shows that rotation is only naturally stable about the smallest and largest principal axes. The middle one is an unstable troublemaker.

Golf clubs, especially in motion, don’t rotate about a neat, fixed axis. Shaft flex, body motion, and off-center attachment mean the actual rotation axis wanders, sometimes into that unstable intermediate territory.

Most current putter design focuses on maximizing vertical MOI for impact forgiveness and, more recently, reducing shaft torque (zero-torque, lie-angle balanced). Those are good things but don’t fully address secondary axis stability.

A mallet with mass concentrated along a central spine aligned with the swing plane and shaft can reduce sensitivity to small changes in lie angle and shaft deflection — improving secondary axis stability.

A strong shift from perimeter to central-axis weighting can easily cut vertical-axis MOI by ~40–50% for the same mass and footprint in simple models. That’s a real tradeoff: you’re swapping some impact forgiveness for a stroke that behaves more predictably in 3D.

The smart play is probably not an extreme design, but a hybrid: enough perimeter mass to maintain respectable MOI, plus a robust central spine to tame the secondary axis chaos.

In short: we’ve spent decades making putters that forgive bad impact; the next frontier is making putters that forgive the messy way humans actually move.
