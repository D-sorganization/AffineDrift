AffineDrift

Affine control theory for golf swing dynamics

"What we do now echoes in eternity"

— Marcus Aurelius

Drift is not a problem to control. It is the state you want to achieve. Drift is greatest when you move fast. It happens when you accumulate momentum in the system. It happens when you move. It is the accumulation of all of your efforts to swing the club. It is an integral sum and the mountain you must climb to get where you are going. You can't stand on top while being safe at the base, feeling safe and in control. You must climb that hill. You must use its influence like a judo artist. The goal is to accumulate not to control. Swing with reckless abandon knowing that drift will always be there as a predictable ally.

AffineDrift takes its name from nonlinear control theory—the mathematics of taming complex, chaotic systems. In a control-affine system, the underlying dynamics may be nonlinear, but the influence of control inputs remains linear. This structure allows the system to be decomposed into two parts:

  • Drift: everything the system does on its own, based on its current state
  • Control: everything that arises from deliberate input

In the drift, the past writes itself into the present. The positions and velocities of every component carry the echoes of previous actions. Marcus Aurelius wasn't talking about dynamics, but he wasn't wrong: what comes before shapes what comes now.

For practitioners, the implication is simple and profound: history matters. The state of the system—its stored momentum, geometry, and timing—plays a major role in the forces and motions we observe. What we do at any moment is always a combination of our current actions and the inertia of everything that came before.

An affine control system is a fundamental concept in nonlinear control theory. These systems are characterized by being nonlinear in the state variables but linear (affine) in the control inputs.

Coordination of the Golf Swing as a Control Problem

The golf swing presents a fundamental coordination challenge: how does the golfer orchestrate the motion of multiple body segments and the club to achieve a desired outcome? This question lies at the heart of motor control research, and it is precisely the type of problem that nonlinear control theory is designed to address.

Traditional biomechanical analyses often treat the swing as a sequence of static positions or focus on individual joint motions in isolation. However, the golf swing is fundamentally a dynamic, coordinated movement where the actions of multiple joints must be synchronized in time and space. The challenge is not merely one of kinematics—which positions to achieve—but of dynamics: how to generate the right forces and torques at the right moments to produce the desired motion.

From a control perspective, the golfer must solve a trajectory optimization problem: find a sequence of muscle activations (control inputs) that drives the system from an initial state (address position) to a desired final state (ball contact) while satisfying constraints on joint ranges of motion, force production capabilities, and timing. The complexity arises because the system is highly nonlinear: the same muscle torque produces different effects depending on the current configuration of the body and club, the velocities of all segments, and the elastic state of the shaft.

Nonlinear control theory provides the mathematical framework to understand and analyze this coordination problem. The control-affine structure \(\dot{x} = f(x) + g(x)u\) naturally captures the separation between passive dynamics (what the system does on its own) and active control (what the golfer adds through muscle activation). This decomposition is crucial because it allows us to ask: which aspects of the observed motion are due to the golfer's active control, and which are due to the natural dynamics of the multibody system?

For golf researchers, this framework offers several advantages. First, it provides a rigorous way to decompose forces into drift and input components, enabling causal attribution of observed forces to specific control actions versus passive dynamics. Second, it reveals how the mechanical advantage of control inputs changes throughout the swing, explaining why certain timing patterns are more effective than others. Third, it enables the construction of counterfactual analyses: what would the motion look like if different control strategies were employed, or if the passive dynamics were different?

By framing the golf swing as a nonlinear control problem, we move beyond descriptive biomechanics toward a predictive, mechanistic understanding of how coordination emerges from the interaction between neural control and mechanical dynamics. This perspective is essential for understanding skill acquisition, injury mechanisms, and the effects of equipment modifications on swing dynamics.

Mathematical Definition

A control-affine system takes the general form:

\[\dot{x} = f(x) + g(x)u\]

where:

  • \(\dot{x}\) is the time derivative of the state vector, representing the rate of change of all system variables (positions, velocities, and any other state components). It describes how the system evolves in time.
  • \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{2(n+m)}\) is the state vector, a complete description of the system's configuration at any instant. For a mechanical system like the golf swing, \(x = [q^T, \dot{q}^T, \eta^T, \dot{\eta}^T]^T\) where:
    • \(q \in \mathbb{R}^n\) are the generalized coordinates (e.g., joint angles for rigid body segments)
    • \(\dot{q} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) are the generalized velocities (angular velocities at joints)
    • \(\eta \in \mathbb{R}^m\) are modal coordinates describing flexible shaft deformation
    • \(\dot{\eta} \in \mathbb{R}^m\) are modal velocities
    The state vector captures everything needed to predict the system's future evolution.
  • \(u \in \mathbb{R}^{m_u}\) is the control input vector, representing the external actions applied to the system. In the golf swing, these are abstracted muscular torques—the net effect of muscle activations at joints. The dimension \(m_u \leq n\) reflects that not all degrees of freedom may be directly actuated. These inputs are what the golfer (or controller) can actively modulate.
  • \(f(x)\) is the drift vector field, representing the passive dynamics of the system—how it would evolve in the absence of any control inputs. This includes gravitational forces, inertial effects, Coriolis and centrifugal forces, and any passive mechanical interactions (e.g., elastic forces from shaft deformation). The drift term depends only on the current state, not on the control inputs.
  • \(g(x)\) is the input vector field (or input matrix), a state-dependent mapping that determines how control inputs \(u\) influence the system's evolution. It encodes the "mechanical advantage" of control inputs, which varies with the system's configuration. In mechanical systems, \(g(x)\) typically has the form \(M^{-1}(q,\eta) B(q)\), where \(M\) is the inertia matrix and \(B\) maps control channels to generalized torques.

The Control Vector Field \(g(x)\)

In the control-affine notation \(\dot{x} = f(x) + g(x)u\), the term \(g(x)\) represents the input vector field that maps control inputs to state accelerations. This field is intimately related to the manipulator jacobian and the system's inertia structure.

Intuitive interpretation: The matrix \(g(x)\) encodes how effective a given control input is at changing the system's acceleration. This effectiveness depends on the current configuration (joint angles, positions) through the inverse inertia matrix. In mechanical systems, \(g(x)\) typically takes the form \(M^{-1}(q) B(q)\), where \(M(q)\) is the inertia matrix and \(B(q)\) maps control channels to generalized torques. The inverse inertia matrix acts like a "mechanical advantage" that varies with configuration—the same torque input produces different accelerations depending on the system's current pose.

For example, in a robotic manipulator, \(g(x)\) captures how joint torques translate to end-effector accelerations, with the mapping changing as the arm moves through different configurations. In the golf swing, \(g(x)\) describes how muscle torques at joints influence the acceleration of the entire golfer–club system, with the coupling strength depending on the current swing position and shaft deformation.

Drift Dynamics

The drift vector field \(f(x)\) captures all the passive dynamics of the system—how it evolves when no control inputs are applied.

This includes gravitational forces pulling on the system, inertial effects from the multibody structure, Coriolis and centrifugal forces arising from rotational motion, and any passive mechanical interactions such as elastic forces from flexible components (e.g., shaft deformation in the golf swing).

Importantly, the drift term depends only on the current state \(x\), not on the control inputs \(u\). This means that for a given configuration and velocity, the passive dynamics are completely determined, regardless of what control actions are being taken.

The drift term represents the "echoes from the past"—forces and torques that emerge from the system's current state, which itself is the result of all previous actions. Understanding the drift dynamics is crucial because it can be large and even dominant during rapid motion, meaning that observed forces may primarily reflect passive dynamics rather than active control effort.

Linearity in Input

The fundamental property of an affine control system is that control inputs \(u\) appear linearly in the dynamics equation. This means that if you double the control input, the contribution to \(\dot{x}\) doubles; if you apply two control inputs simultaneously, their effects add together (superposition).

This linearity in control is preserved even though the system may be highly nonlinear in the state variables. The input vector field \(g(x)\) can depend on the state (making the system nonlinear overall), but the control input \(u\) itself always multiplies \(g(x)\) linearly, never appearing in powers, products with state variables, or other nonlinear combinations.

This structural property enables powerful analytical tools, including the decomposition of dynamics into drift and input components, and the construction of counterfactual trajectories.

The golf swing is a remarkably sophisticated example of human motor control. When viewed through the lens of affine control theory, the swing reveals itself as a high-dimensional system where muscular inputs (controls) interact with biomechanical constraints and gravitational dynamics (drift).

System Components

State Space

Joint angles, angular velocities, club position and orientation

Drift Term \(f(x)\)

Gravity, inertial forces, passive tissue dynamics, shaft dynamics

Control Inputs \(u\)

Muscle torques at joints

Input Vector Field \(g(x)\)

The manipulator jacobian and moment arms determine how muscle torques translate to forces and torques throughout the system, with the mapping varying based on the current configuration
